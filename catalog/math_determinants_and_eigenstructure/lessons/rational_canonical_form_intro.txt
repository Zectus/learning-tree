=== SECTION 1: Cyclic subspaces and companion matrices ===

Diagonalization and upper triangularization both demand that \(p_A(x)\) split completely into linear factors over the base field \(F\). What if you are working over \(\mathbb{Q}\) or \(\mathbb{R}\) and refuse to enlarge the field—for instance, with a rotation matrix whose characteristic polynomial is \(x^2 + 1\), or a rational matrix with irreducible characteristic polynomial \(x^3 - 2\)? Without eigenvalues in \(F\), eigenvectors vanish completely. Yet there is a canonical block form that works over EVERY field \(F\) without ever factoring \(p_A(x)\) into linear factors! Its building block is not an eigenline, but the orbit generated by repeatedly applying \(T\) to a single vector.

[CALLOUT]
id: cyclic-subspace
label: Definition
title: The \(\mathbf{v}\)-annihilator and \(T\)-cyclic subspace
tone: key

Let \(T : V \to V\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional space over \(F\), and let \(\mathbf{v} \in V \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}\).

(1) The \(\mathbf{v}\)-annihilator polynomial \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(x) \in F[x]\) is the unique monic polynomial of smallest positive degree such that \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(T)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\).
(2) If \(d = \deg(f_{\mathbf{v}})\), the \(T\)-cyclic subspace generated by \(\mathbf{v}\) is
\[
Z(\mathbf{v}; T) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v},\; T\mathbf{v},\; T^2\mathbf{v},\; \dots,\; T^{d-1}\mathbf{v}\}.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Check why \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(x)\) always exists, is unique, and divides \(m_T(x)\): since \(m_T(T)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\), the set of polynomials \(g(x) \in F[x]\) with \(g(T)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\) contains nonzero elements. Taking a monic one \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(x)\) of minimal positive degree \(d \ge 1\), the exact same division-algorithm argument used for minimal polynomials proves that \(g(T)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\) if and only if \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(x) \mid g(x)\)—in particular, \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(x) \mid m_T(x)\).

Even better, the spanning set \(\mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf{v}, T\mathbf{v}, \dots, T^{d-1}\mathbf{v}\}\) is automatically a BASIS of \(Z(\mathbf{v}; T)\), and \(Z(\mathbf{v}; T)\) is \(T\)-invariant!
- Linear independence: if \(a_0 \mathbf{v} + a_1 T\mathbf{v} + \dots + a_{d-1} T^{d-1}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\) with not all \(a_j = 0\), then the nonzero polynomial \(g(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_{d-1} x^{d-1}\) of degree \(< d\) would satisfy \(g(T)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\), contradicting the minimality of \(d = \deg(f_{\mathbf{v}})\). Thus \(\dim Z(\mathbf{v}; T) = d\).
- \(T\)-invariance and matrix form: write
\[
f_{\mathbf{v}}(x) = x^d + c_{d-1} x^{d-1} + \dots + c_1 x + c_0.
\]
Since \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(T)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\), solving for \(T^d \mathbf{v}\) gives
\[
T^d \mathbf{v} = -c_0 \mathbf{v} - c_1 T\mathbf{v} - \dots - c_{d-1} T^{d-1}\mathbf{v} \in Z(\mathbf{v}; T).
\]
Watch what \(T\) does to each vector of the ordered cyclic basis \(\mathcal{C} = (\mathbf{v}, T\mathbf{v}, \dots, T^{d-1}\mathbf{v})\): it sends the first vector to the second, the second to the third, ..., the \((d - 1)\)-st to the \(d\)-th, and the \(d\)-th vector \(T^{d-1}\mathbf{v}\) to \(T^d\mathbf{v} = -c_0\mathbf{v} - c_1 T\mathbf{v} - \dots - c_{d-1} T^{d-1}\mathbf{v}\). Writing those images as columns produces the companion matrix of \(f_{\mathbf{v}}\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: companion-matrix
label: Definition & Theorem
title: Companion matrix \(C(f)\)
tone: key

For any monic polynomial \(f(x) = x^d + c_{d-1} x^{d-1} + \dots + c_1 x + c_0 \in F[x]\) (\(d \ge 1\)), its \(d \times d\) companion matrix is
\[
C(f) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -c_0 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -c_1 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & -c_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -c_{d-1} \end{bmatrix}
\]
(for \(d = 1\) and \(f(x) = x - \lambda\), \(C(x - \lambda) = [\lambda]\)).

(1) In the cyclic basis \(\mathcal{C} = (\mathbf{v}, T\mathbf{v}, \dots, T^{d-1}\mathbf{v})\) of \(Z(\mathbf{v}; T)\), the restriction matrix is \([T|_{Z(\mathbf{v}; T)}]_{\mathcal{C}} = C(f_{\mathbf{v}})\).
(2) Both the minimal polynomial AND the characteristic polynomial of \(C(f)\) equal \(f(x)\):
\[
m_{C(f)}(x) = p_{C(f)}(x) = f(x).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Let us prove [REF: companion-matrix.2 | \(m_{C(f)} = p_{C(f)} = f\)] cleanly without even needing a tedious determinant expansion! Look at the standard basis of \(F^d\) under \(C = C(f)\): by construction, \(C\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{e}_2\), \(C^2\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{e}_3\), ..., \(C^{d-1}\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{e}_d\), and \(f(C)\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{0}\).
- For ANY polynomial \(g(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_{d-1} x^{d-1}\) of degree \(< d\), we have \(g(C)\mathbf{e}_1 = a_0\mathbf{e}_1 + a_1\mathbf{e}_2 + \dots + a_{d-1}\mathbf{e}_d\), which equals \(\mathbf{0}\) ONLY when all \(a_j = 0\). Hence NO nonzero polynomial of degree \(< d\) annihilates \(C\), so \(\deg(m_C) \ge d\).
- Meanwhile, for every basis vector \(\mathbf{e}_j = C^{j-1}\mathbf{e}_1\), commutativity of polynomials in \(C\) gives \(f(C)\mathbf{e}_j = C^{j-1} f(C)\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{0}\), so \(f(C) = 0\). Because \(f\) is monic of degree \(d\) and no smaller-degree polynomial kills \(C\), we get \(m_C(x) = f(x)\).
- Finally, \(m_C(x)\) divides \(p_C(x)\), and BOTH are monic of the exact same degree \(d\), forcing \(p_C(x) = m_C(x) = f(x)\)!

=== SECTION 2: Cyclic (nonderogatory) operators ===

When can an ENTIRE operator \(T : V \to V\) on an \(n\)-dimensional space be represented by a SINGLE \(n \times n\) companion matrix? That happens precisely when there exists a cyclic vector \(\mathbf{v} \in V\) such that \(Z(\mathbf{v}; T) = V\) (equivalently, \(\deg f_{\mathbf{v}} = n\)). Such an operator is called cyclic (or nonderogatory). Remarkably, there is a single polynomial test for cyclicity.

[CALLOUT]
id: cyclic-criterion
label: Theorem
title: Characterization of cyclic (nonderogatory) operators
tone: key

A linear operator \(T : V \to V\) on an \(n\)-dimensional vector space \(V\) over \(F\) is cyclic—meaning \(V = Z(\mathbf{v}; T)\) for some \(\mathbf{v} \in V\), so \(T\) is similar over \(F\) to the single companion matrix \(C(p_T)\)—if and only if
\[
m_T(x) = p_T(x).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

The forward direction (\(\Rightarrow\)) is immediate from [REF: companion-matrix.2 | companion matrix polynomials]: if \([T]_{\mathcal{C}} = C(f_{\mathbf{v}})\), then \(m_T = m_{C(f_{\mathbf{v}})} = p_{C(f_{\mathbf{v}})} = p_T\).

The converse (\(\Leftarrow\)) rests on a wonderfully concrete fact: for EVERY operator \(T\), there exists at least one vector \(\mathbf{v} \in V\) whose annihilator EQUALS the full minimal polynomial \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(x) = m_T(x)\)! Here is the elementary proof in two short steps:
1. Coprime-sum rule: Suppose \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w} \in V\) have relatively prime annihilators, \(\gcd(f_{\mathbf{u}}, f_{\mathbf{w}}) = 1\). Then the annihilator of \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}\) is their product, \(f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}} = f_{\mathbf{u}} f_{\mathbf{w}}\).
   Check both divisibilities! Clearly \(f_{\mathbf{u}}(T) f_{\mathbf{w}}(T)(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}\), so \(f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}} \mid f_{\mathbf{u}} f_{\mathbf{w}}\). Conversely, applying \(f_{\mathbf{w}}(T)\) to \(f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}(T)(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}\) kills the \(\mathbf{w}\)-term and leaves \(f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}(T) f_{\mathbf{w}}(T)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\), so \(f_{\mathbf{u}} \mid f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}} f_{\mathbf{w}}\). Because \(\gcd(f_{\mathbf{u}}, f_{\mathbf{w}}) = 1\), we must have \(f_{\mathbf{u}} \mid f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}\). Symmetrically, \(f_{\mathbf{w}} \mid f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}\), and since they are coprime, \(f_{\mathbf{u}} f_{\mathbf{w}} \mid f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}\). Both are monic, so \(f_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}} = f_{\mathbf{u}} f_{\mathbf{w}}\).
2. Building a vector with annihilator \(m_T\): Factor \(m_T(x) = q_1(x)^{e_1} q_2(x)^{e_2}\cdots q_k(x)^{e_k}\) into powers of distinct monic irreducible polynomials over \(F\). For each \(i\), because \(m_T(x) / q_i(x)\) has strictly smaller degree than \(m_T(x)\), it does NOT annihilate \(T\); thus we can pick \(\mathbf{w}_i \in V\) with \((m_T / q_i)(T)\mathbf{w}_i \neq \mathbf{0}\). Define
\[
\mathbf{u}_i = \left(\frac{m_T}{q_i^{e_i}}\right)(T)\mathbf{w}_i.
\]
Then \(q_i(T)^{e_i}\mathbf{u}_i = m_T(T)\mathbf{w}_i = \mathbf{0}\) (so \(f_{\mathbf{u}_i} \mid q_i^{e_i}\)), while \(q_i(T)^{e_i - 1}\mathbf{u}_i = (m_T / q_i)(T)\mathbf{w}_i \neq \mathbf{0}\) (so \(f_{\mathbf{u}_i}\) cannot be a smaller power of \(q_i\)). Therefore \(f_{\mathbf{u}_i} = q_i^{e_i}\)! Because \(q_1^{e_1}, \dots, q_k^{e_k}\) are pairwise coprime, applying step 1 repeatedly to \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}_1 + \mathbf{u}_2 + \dots + \mathbf{u}_k\) yields \(f_{\mathbf{v}}(x) = q_1^{e_1}\cdots q_k^{e_k} = m_T(x)\).

Now suppose \(m_T(x) = p_T(x)\). Choosing that vector \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(f_{\mathbf{v}} = m_T = p_T\) gives \(\dim Z(\mathbf{v}; T) = \deg(p_T) = n = \dim V\), so \(Z(\mathbf{v}; T) = V\) and \(T\) is cyclic!

Notice a striking consequence: if \(A \in M_n(F)\) has an IRREDUCIBLE characteristic polynomial over \(F\) (such as \(x^3 - 2\) over \(\mathbb{Q}\)), then \(m_A(x) \mid p_A(x)\) forces \(m_A = p_A\), so \(A\) is automatically cyclic—and in fact EVERY nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v} \in F^n\) generates a cyclic basis of the entire space! By contrast, a derogatory matrix such as \(\operatorname{diag}(2, 2, 3)\) has \(m_A(x) = (x - 2)(x - 3) \neq (x - 2)^2(x - 3) = p_A(x)\), so no single vector's orbit can ever span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

=== SECTION 3: Invariant factors and Rational Canonical Form ===

When \(m_T(x) \neq p_T(x)\), a single cyclic subspace \(Z(\mathbf{v}; T)\) cannot fill \(V\). Instead, \(V\) decomposes into a direct sum of \(T\)-cyclic subspaces whose annihilators form a nested divisibility chain! The general existence and uniqueness proof of this decomposition belongs to Abstract Algebra (the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over the principal ideal domain \(F[x]\)); here we take the theorem as a supplied structural foundation and learn to compute with it and use it to decide matrix similarity over ANY field \(F\).

[CALLOUT]
id: rcf-theorem
label: Theorem
title: Rational Canonical Form (Invariant-Factor Form)
tone: key

Let \(A \in M_n(F)\) for any field \(F\) (such as \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\mathbb{R}\), or \(\mathbb{C}\)). Then \(A\) is similar OVER \(F\) to a block-diagonal matrix of companion blocks
\[
R = C(f_1) \oplus C(f_2) \oplus \dots \oplus C(f_s) = \begin{bmatrix} C(f_1) & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & C(f_2) & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & C(f_s) \end{bmatrix},
\]
where \(f_1, f_2, \dots, f_s \in F[x]\) are monic polynomials of degree \(\ge 1\)—called the invariant factors of \(A\)—satisfying:

(1) Divisibility chain: \(f_1(x) \mid f_2(x) \mid \dots \mid f_s(x)\) in \(F[x]\).
(2) Minimal polynomial: the largest invariant factor is \(f_s(x) = m_A(x)\).
(3) Characteristic polynomial: the product of all invariant factors is \(f_1(x) f_2(x) \cdots f_s(x) = p_A(x)\) (so \(\sum_{i=1}^s \deg f_i = n\)).
(4) Uniqueness and similarity: the list \((f_1, \dots, f_s)\) is UNIQUELY determined by \(A\). Two matrices \(A, B \in M_n(F)\) are similar over \(F\) if and only if they have the exact same invariant factors (equivalently, the same Rational Canonical Form \(R\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice a remarkable consequence of [REF: rcf-theorem.4 | uniqueness]: because the invariant factors of \(A \in M_n(\mathbb{Q})\) lie in \(\mathbb{Q}[x] \subset \mathbb{R}[x] \subset \mathbb{C}[x]\) and still satisfy the exact same divisibility chain over any larger field, the Rational Canonical Form of \(A\) does NOT change when you enlarge the field! Consequently, two rational (or real) matrices are similar over \(\mathbb{Q}\) (or \(\mathbb{R}\)) IF AND ONLY IF they are similar over \(\mathbb{C}\).

Watch how the rules [REF: rcf-theorem.1 | \(f_1 \mid \dots \mid f_s\)], [REF: rcf-theorem.2 | \(f_s = m_A\)], and [REF: rcf-theorem.3 | \(\prod f_i = p_A\)] pin down Rational Canonical Forms cleanly in dimensions \(2, 3,\) and \(4\):

- Every \(2 \times 2\) matrix: either \(\deg m_A = 2\) (so \(m_A = p_A\) and \(R = C(p_A)\) is one \(2 \times 2\) companion block), or \(\deg m_A = 1\), forcing \(m_A(x) = x - c\), \(f_1 = f_2 = x - c\), and \(R = cI_2\).
- A \(3 \times 3\) example over \(\mathbb{Q}\): suppose \(p_A(x) = (x - 1)^2(x + 2)\) and \(m_A(x) = (x - 1)(x + 2)\). Then \(f_s = (x - 1)(x + 2) = x^2 + x - 2\), and the remaining factor must be \(f_1 = p_A / f_s = x - 1\). Verify the divisibility chain: \((x - 1) \mid (x - 1)(x + 2)\) holds! Thus the Rational Canonical Form is
\[
R = C(x - 1) \oplus C(x^2 + x - 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
  Watch out for a common mistake here! The diagonal matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, -2)\) is similar to \(R\), yet \(\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, -2) = C(x - 1) \oplus C(x - 1) \oplus C(x + 2)\) is NOT in Rational Canonical Form because \((x - 1)\) does NOT divide \((x + 2)\)! The divisibility chain forces us to merge one \((x - 1)\) with \((x + 2)\) into the single \(2 \times 2\) block \(C(x^2 + x - 2)\).
- A \(4 \times 4\) example with an irreducible quadratic over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{Q}\): suppose \(p_A(x) = (x^2 + 1)^2\). Since \(x^2 + 1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb{R}\) (and \(\mathbb{Q}\)), there are only two possibilities for \(m_A(x)\):
  - If \(m_A(x) = x^2 + 1\), then \(f_2 = x^2 + 1\) and \(f_1 = x^2 + 1\), giving \(R = C(x^2 + 1) \oplus C(x^2 + 1)\) (two \(2 \times 2\) blocks).
  - If \(m_A(x) = (x^2 + 1)^2 = x^4 + 2x^2 + 1\), then \(f_1 = x^4 + 2x^2 + 1\), giving a single \(4 \times 4\) block \(R = C(x^4 + 2x^2 + 1)\).
- A \(4 \times 4\) example where \(p_A\) and \(m_A\) alone leave two choices: suppose \(p_A(x) = (x - 2)^4\) and \(m_A(x) = (x - 2)^2\). The remaining invariant factors multiply to \((x - 2)^2\) and must divide \((x - 2)^2\), allowing TWO valid chains:
  - Chain 1: \(f_1 = (x - 2)^2,\; f_2 = (x - 2)^2\) (two \(2 \times 2\) blocks).
  - Chain 2: \(f_1 = x - 2,\; f_2 = x - 2,\; f_3 = (x - 2)^2\) (two \(1 \times 1\) blocks and one \(2 \times 2\) block).
  How do we tell them apart? Look at \(\dim \ker(C((x - \lambda)^k) - \lambda I_k)\): because \(C((x - \lambda)^k)\) represents a single cyclic block \(Z(\mathbf{v}; T)\) with minimal polynomial \((x - \lambda)^k\), its eigenspace for \(\lambda\) is \(1\)-dimensional (spanned by \((T - \lambda I)^{k-1}\mathbf{v}\); if a second linearly independent eigenvector existed, \(\ker(T - \lambda I)\) would have dimension \(\ge 2\) and \((T - \lambda I)^{k-1}\) would have rank at most \(0\), contradicting \(m_C = (x - \lambda)^k\)!). Therefore each invariant factor divisible by \((x - \lambda)\) contributes EXACTLY \(1\) to \(\dim \ker(A - \lambda I)\):
  - Chain 1 has \(2\) invariant factors \(\implies \operatorname{nullity}(A - 2I_4) = 2\).
  - Chain 2 has \(3\) invariant factors \(\implies \operatorname{nullity}(A - 2I_4) = 3\).

\((x - 1)^2(x + 2)\) | \((x - 1)^2(x + 2)\) | \(((x - 1)^2(x + 2))\) | \(3\)
