=== SECTION 1: Invertibility and the multiplicativity theorem ===

In the previous lesson we defined \(\det : M_n(F) \to F\) by alternating multilinearity in the rows and normalization \(\det(I_n) = 1\), and we proved that \(\det(EA) = \det(E)\det(A)\) whenever \(E\) is an elementary matrix. That single identity turns out to unlock two cornerstone algebraic theorems: a sharp numerical test for invertibility, and the multiplicativity rule \(\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)\) for all square matrices \(A, B \in M_n(F)\).

Start with invertibility. Let \(A \in M_n(F)\), and let \(R\) be its reduced row echelon form. Since row reduction applies a finite sequence of elementary row operations, there exist elementary matrices \(E_1, \dots, E_k\) such that
\[
R = E_k \cdots E_2 E_1 A.
\]
Taking determinants and using \(\det(E_m X) = \det(E_m)\det(X)\) repeatedly gives
\[
\det(R) = \det(E_k) \cdots \det(E_1)\det(A).
\]
Every elementary matrix has a nonzero determinant (\(1\), \(-1\), or a nonzero scaling factor \(c \neq 0\)), so the product \(\det(E_k) \cdots \det(E_1)\) is nonzero. Consequently, \(\det(A) \neq 0\) if and only if \(\det(R) \neq 0\). Now look at the two possibilities for \(R\):
- If \(A\) is invertible, then \(R = I_n\), so \(\det(R) = 1 \neq 0\), and hence \(\det(A) \neq 0\).
- If \(A\) is singular (not invertible), then \(R\) has at least one row of all zeros, so \(\det(R) = 0\), and hence \(\det(A) = 0\).

[CALLOUT]
id: det-invertibility
label: Theorem
title: Determinant test for invertibility
tone: key

A matrix \(A \in M_n(F)\) is invertible if and only if \(\det(A) \neq 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

Now we can prove multiplicativity for ANY two \(n \times n\) matrices \(A\) and \(B\) by splitting on whether \(A\) is invertible or singular.

[CALLOUT]
id: det-multiplicativity
label: Theorem
title: Multiplicativity of the determinant
tone: key

For all \(A, B \in M_n(F)\),
\[
\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

To prove [REF: det-multiplicativity | multiplicativity], examine the two cases for \(A\):
- Case 1: \(A\) is invertible. Recall from matrix algebra that every invertible matrix is a finite product of elementary matrices, say \(A = E_1 E_2 \cdots E_k\). Applying the elementary-matrix rule repeatedly first to \(AB\) and then to \(A = E_1 \cdots E_k I_n\) yields
\[
\det(AB) = \det(E_1 E_2 \cdots E_k B) = \det(E_1)\det(E_2)\cdots\det(E_k)\det(B) = \det(E_1 E_2 \cdots E_k I_n)\det(B) = \det(A)\det(B).
\]
- Case 2: \(A\) is singular. By [REF: det-invertibility | the invertibility test], \(\det(A) = 0\), so the right-hand side \(\det(A)\det(B)\) is \(0\). What about \(AB\)? If \(AB\) were invertible, with inverse \(C\), then \(A(BC) = I_n\), which for square matrices forces \(A\) to be invertible—a contradiction. Thus \(AB\) is also singular, so \(\det(AB) = 0 = \det(A)\det(B)\).

Both cases agree, completing the proof! Notice how cleanly this proof avoids messy index sums: factoring invertible matrices into elementary matrices does almost all the work.

=== SECTION 2: Algebraic consequences and classic pitfalls ===

Three workhorse identities drop out immediately from [REF: det-multiplicativity | multiplicativity] and row multilinearity.

[CALLOUT]
id: det-algebra-rules
label: Corollary
title: Inverse, power, and scalar-multiple rules
tone: key

Let \(A \in M_n(F)\) and \(c \in F\).

(1) If \(A\) is invertible, then \(\det(A^{-1}) = \dfrac{1}{\det(A)}\).
(2) For every integer \(k \ge 0\), \(\det(A^k) = (\det A)^k\) (and for \(k < 0\) as well whenever \(A\) is invertible).
(3) \(\det(cA) = c^n \det(A)\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us verify each item directly. For [REF: det-algebra-rules.1 | the inverse rule], apply [REF: det-multiplicativity | multiplicativity] to \(A A^{-1} = I_n\): we get \(\det(A)\det(A^{-1}) = \det(I_n) = 1\), and since \(\det(A) \neq 0\), dividing gives \(\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)\). For [REF: det-algebra-rules.2 | the power rule], \(k = 0\) gives \(\det(I_n) = 1 = (\det A)^0\), positive integers follow by induction from \(\det(A^{k+1}) = \det(A^k)\det(A)\), and negative integers follow by combining positive powers with the inverse rule. For [REF: det-algebra-rules.3 | the scalar-multiple rule], write \(cA = (cI_n)A\); since \(cI_n\) is diagonal with \(n\) copies of \(c\) on its diagonal, \(\det(cI_n) = c^n\), so \(\det(cA) = \det(cI_n)\det(A) = c^n \det(A)\). (Equivalently, multiplying \(A\) by \(c\) scales each of the \(n\) rows by \(c\), pulling out \(n\) separate factors of \(c\).)

[CALLOUT]
id: det-pitfalls
label: Common pitfall
title: Two identities that fail in general
tone: caution

(1) \(\det(cA) \neq c\det(A)\) in general when \(n \ge 2\): the scalar \(c\) is raised to the matrix size \(n\), so \(\det(cA) = c^n \det(A)\). In particular, \(\det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A)\), which equals \(+\det(A)\) when \(n\) is even and \(-\det(A)\) when \(n\) is odd.
(2) \(\det(A + B) \neq \det(A) + \det(B)\) in general: the determinant is multilinear in each row separately, NOT additive in the whole matrix.
[/CALLOUT]

A \(2 \times 2\) example settles [REF: det-pitfalls.2 | non-additivity] in one line: take \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Both \(A\) and \(B\) have a zero row, so \(\det(A) = 0\) and \(\det(B) = 0\), yet \(A + B = I_2\) has \(\det(A + B) = 1 \neq 0 + 0\).

The graph below illustrates how strongly the matrix dimension \(n\) governs the scaling ratio \(\det(cA)/\det(A) = c^n\) as \(c\) varies across \([-2, 2]\). Notice both the steeper growth for larger \(n\) and the sign behavior for negative \(c\): when \(n = 2\), \(\det(-A) = (-1)^2 \det(A) = \det(A)\), whereas when \(n = 3\), \(\det(-A) = -\det(A)\).

=== SECTION 3: Transpose invariance and column operations ===

Everything we established so far was built from row operations. Does the determinant treat columns with the exact same symmetry? The answer is yes, because transposing a matrix leaves its determinant unchanged.

[CALLOUT]
id: det-transpose
label: Theorem
title: Transpose invariance
tone: key

For every matrix \(A \in M_n(F)\),
\[
\det(A^T) = \det(A).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

To prove [REF: det-transpose | transpose invariance], first check every elementary matrix \(E\):
1. If \(E\) is a row-scaling matrix (diagonal with \(c\) in entry \((i, i)\) and \(1\)s elsewhere), then \(E^T = E\), so \(\det(E^T) = \det(E) = c\).
2. If \(E\) is a row-swap matrix (obtained by swapping rows \(i\) and \(j\) of \(I_n\)), then \(E\) is symmetric (\(E^T = E\)), so \(\det(E^T) = \det(E) = -1\).
3. If \(E\) is a row-replacement matrix (having \(c\) in off-diagonal entry \((i, j)\) with \(i \neq j\)), then \(E^T\) has \(c\) in off-diagonal entry \((j, i)\)—which is STILL a row-replacement matrix, so \(\det(E^T) = 1 = \det(E)\).

Now consider an arbitrary matrix \(A \in M_n(F)\):
- If \(A\) is invertible, factor it into elementary matrices \(A = E_1 E_2 \cdots E_k\). Recall that \((XY)^T = Y^T X^T\), so \(A^T = E_k^T \cdots E_2^T E_1^T\). Applying [REF: det-multiplicativity | multiplicativity] and the elementary-matrix check above gives
\[
\det(A^T) = \det(E_k^T)\cdots\det(E_1^T) = \det(E_k)\cdots\det(E_1) = \det(E_1)\cdots\det(E_k) = \det(A),
\]
because multiplication of scalars in \(F\) is commutative.
- If \(A\) is singular, then \(A^T\) is also singular (if \(A^T\) had an inverse \(C\), then \(A C^T = (C A^T)^T = I_n^T = I_n\), forcing \(A\) to be invertible). By [REF: det-invertibility | the invertibility test], \(\det(A^T) = 0 = \det(A)\).

Because the columns of \(A\) are the rows of \(A^T\), [REF: det-transpose | transpose invariance] immediately transfers EVERY row property of the determinant to columns:
- \(\det(A)\) is multilinear in each column separately.
- Swapping two columns negates \(\det(A)\), and two identical columns force \(\det(A) = 0\).
- Scaling a column by \(c\) multiplies \(\det(A)\) by \(c\).
- Adding a multiple of one column to another column leaves \(\det(A)\) unchanged.
- Any zero column forces \(\det(A) = 0\).

In practice, this doubles our computational flexibility: you may freely mix elementary row operations and elementary column operations when reducing a matrix to compute its determinant! (Be careful not to confuse this with solving linear systems \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\), where column operations change the variables and are not allowed in the same way; for computing \(\det(A)\) alone, mixing row and column operations is completely valid.)

=== SECTION 4: Similarity invariance and the determinant of a linear operator ===

Suppose \(V\) is an \(n\)-dimensional vector space over \(F\) and \(T : V \to V\) is a linear operator. If we choose a basis \(\mathcal{B}\) of \(V\), we obtain an \(n \times n\) matrix representation \([T]_{\mathcal{B}}\). Choosing a different basis \(\mathcal{C}\) produces a different matrix \([T]_{\mathcal{C}}\), related to the first by a change-of-basis similarity transformation
\[
[T]_{\mathcal{C}} = P^{-1} [T]_{\mathcal{B}} P,
\]
where \(P\) is the invertible change-of-basis matrix from \(\mathcal{C}\) to \(\mathcal{B}\). Does the determinant depend on which basis we picked?

Apply [REF: det-multiplicativity | multiplicativity] and [REF: det-algebra-rules.1 | the inverse rule] to any similar matrices \(B = P^{-1} A P\):
\[
\det(P^{-1} A P) = \det(P^{-1})\det(A)\det(P) = \frac{1}{\det(P)}\det(A)\det(P) = \det(A).
\]
The factors \(\det(P)\) and \(\det(P)^{-1}\) cancel completely!

[CALLOUT]
id: det-similarity
label: Theorem
title: Similarity invariance and operator determinants
tone: key

(1) If \(A, B \in M_n(F)\) are similar (\(B = P^{-1} A P\) for some invertible \(P \in M_n(F)\)), then \(\det(B) = \det(A)\).
(2) For any linear operator \(T : V \to V\) on a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\), the determinant \(\det(T) = \det([T]_{\mathcal{B}})\) is independent of the choice of basis \(\mathcal{B}\) of \(V\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice what [REF: det-similarity.2 | basis independence] gives us: to compute \(\det(T)\) for an abstract operator \(T : V \to V\) (such as a differentiation or reflection operator), we are free to pick the most convenient basis of \(V\)—one that makes \([T]_{\mathcal{B}}\) triangular or diagonal! For example, let \(T : \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})\) be defined on polynomials of degree at most \(2\) by \(T(p(x)) = p(x) + x p'(x)\). In the standard basis \(\mathcal{B} = \{1, x, x^2\}\), we compute \(T(1) = 1\), \(T(x) = 2x\), and \(T(x^2) = 3x^2\), so \([T]_{\mathcal{B}} = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 3)\) and \(\det(T) = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 6\). In any other basis of \(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})\), the matrix representation would look far more complicated, yet its determinant is guaranteed to equal \(6\).

Be careful not to reverse the implication in [REF: det-similarity.1 | similarity invariance]: having \(\det(A) = \det(B)\) does NOT imply that \(A\) and \(B\) are similar. For instance, \(I_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) both have determinant \(1\), yet \(P^{-1} I_2 P = I_2\) for every invertible \(P\), so \(I_2\) is similar only to itself.

=== SECTION 5: Block-triangular matrices ===

Many matrices in linear algebra—especially when we later analyze invariant subspaces and characteristic polynomials—break naturally into blocks. Suppose an \((p + q) \times (p + q)\) matrix \(M\) is block upper-triangular:
\[
M = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & D \end{bmatrix},
\]
where \(A \in M_p(F)\), \(D \in M_q(F)\), \(B\) is \(p \times q\), and the bottom-left \(q \times p\) block is entirely zero. How does \(\det(M)\) relate to \(\det(A)\) and \(\det(D)\)?

[CALLOUT]
id: block-triangular-det
label: Theorem
title: Determinant of a block-triangular matrix
tone: key

If \(A \in M_p(F)\), \(D \in M_q(F)\), and \(B\) is a \(p \times q\) matrix, then
\[
\det\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & D \end{bmatrix} = \det(A)\det(D).
\]
By transpose invariance, the same formula holds for block lower-triangular matrices \(\begin{bmatrix} A & 0 \\ C & D \end{bmatrix}\).
[/CALLOUT]

We can prove [REF: block-triangular-det | the block-triangular formula] cleanly by row-reducing the top block and bottom block separately—without ever mixing rows from the top \(p\) rows into the bottom \(q\) rows!
1. Perform elementary row operations on the first \(p\) rows of \(M\) alone (corresponding to left-multiplying \(A\) by elementary matrices \(E_1, \dots, E_r\)) to reduce \(A\) to an upper-triangular matrix \(U_A\). These operations modify \(B\) to some matrix \(\tilde{B}\), leave the zero block \(0\) and \(D\) completely untouched, and multiply the determinant of \(M\) by the exact same scalar factor \(\alpha = \det(E_r)\cdots\det(E_1)\) that satisfies \(\det(U_A) = \alpha \det(A)\).
2. Next, perform elementary row operations on the last \(q\) rows of \(M\) alone (corresponding to left-multiplying \(D\) by elementary matrices \(F_1, \dots, F_s\)) to reduce \(D\) to an upper-triangular matrix \(U_D\). Because the bottom-left block is zero, these operations leave the zero block as \(0\) and do not touch the top \(p\) rows; they multiply the determinant by \(\beta = \det(F_s)\cdots\det(F_1)\) satisfying \(\det(U_D) = \beta \det(D)\).

After both stages, \(M\) has been transformed into the fully upper-triangular matrix
\[
\begin{bmatrix} U_A & \tilde{B} \\ 0 & U_D \end{bmatrix},
\]
whose diagonal entries are simply the diagonal entries of \(U_A\) followed by the diagonal entries of \(U_D\). Therefore
\[
\alpha \beta \det(M) = \det\begin{bmatrix} U_A & \tilde{B} \\ 0 & U_D \end{bmatrix} = \det(U_A)\det(U_D) = (\alpha \det(A))(\beta \det(D)).
\]
Since \(\alpha \neq 0\) and \(\beta \neq 0\), cancelling \(\alpha \beta\) from both sides yields \(\det(M) = \det(A)\det(D)\). By induction, the determinant of any block-triangular matrix with square diagonal blocks \(A_1, \dots, A_m\) is the product \(\det(A_1)\cdots\det(A_m)\), and the off-diagonal blocks (such as \(B\)) have zero effect on the determinant.

Notice a crucial boundary of this theorem: for a GENERAL \(2 \times 2\) block matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix}\) where BOTH \(B\) and \(C\) are nonzero, \(\det(M)\) is NOT in general equal to \(\det(A)\det(D) - \det(B)\det(C)\). Even for \(p = q = 2\), matrix blocks do not commute in general, so the scalar \(2 \times 2\) rule cannot be blindly applied to blocks!
