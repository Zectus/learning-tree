=== SECTION 1: Packaging Gaussian elimination as \(A = LU\) ===

Suppose an engineering simulation needs to solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}_1\), then \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}_2\), and eventually \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}_{100}\), where the coefficient matrix \(A\) stays fixed and only the right-hand side \(\mathbf{b}_j\) arrives one at a time. Running Gaussian elimination from scratch on \([A \mid \mathbf{b}_j]\) repeats the exact same work on \(A\) a hundred times! On the other hand, we already saw that computing \(A^{-1}\) explicitly costs three times as much arithmetic up front as a single Gaussian elimination. Is there a way to record the elimination steps performed on \(A\) ONCE, in a compact triangular factorization, so that every new right-hand side \(\mathbf{b}_j\) can be solved quickly without ever reducing \(A\) again?

That factorization is \(A = LU\).

Suppose first that forward Gaussian elimination reduces an \(m \times n\) (or square \(n \times n\)) matrix \(A\) to an upper-triangular row-echelon matrix \(U\) using ONLY downward row-replacement operations—no row swaps and no scaling of pivot rows. Specifically, at column \(1\) we clear below pivot \(a_{11}\) by applying \(R_i \leftarrow R_i - l_{i1} R_1\) for \(i = 2, \dots, m\), where \(l_{i1} = a_{i1} / a_{11}\) is the elimination multiplier; then at column \(2\) we clear below the updated pivot \(u_{22}\) by applying \(R_i \leftarrow R_i - l_{i2} R_2\) for \(i = 3, \dots, m\); and so on from left to right.

Translate those \(k\) downward row replacements into elementary matrices \(E_1, E_2, \dots, E_k\):
\[
E_k \cdots E_2 E_1 A = U.
\]
Each \(E_r\) is unit lower-triangular (\(1\)'s on the main diagonal, \(0\)'s above, and a single entry \(-l_{ij}\) below the diagonal in slot \((i, j)\) with \(i > j\)). Inverting both sides gives
\[
A = L U, \qquad \text{where } L = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} \cdots E_k^{-1}.
\]
Each inverse elementary matrix \(E_r^{-1}\) simply flips the sign of its single off-diagonal entry from \(-l_{ij}\) to \(+l_{ij}\)!

Now comes the crucial algebraic miracle that makes \(LU\) factorization effortless by hand and in code: when you multiply \(E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} \cdots E_k^{-1}\) in that exact forward-elimination order, NO cross-terms ever form—every multiplier \(l_{ij}\) simply drops directly into slot \((i, j)\) of \(L\) untouched!

Let us prove WHY no cross-terms corrupt \(L\) (and why they DO appear if you multiply in the opposite order \(E_k \cdots E_1\)). Consider the \(3 \times 3\) case, where elimination applies:
1. \(E_1\) (\(R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - l_{21} R_1\)),
2. \(E_2\) (\(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - l_{31} R_1\)),
3. \(E_3\) (\(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - l_{32} R_2\)).

Then
\[
L = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} E_3^{-1}
= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ l_{31} & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Multiply from right to left and watch what each left-multiplication does:
- First, \(E_2^{-1}\) acts on \(E_3^{-1}\) by adding \(l_{31}\) times ROW \(1\) of \(E_3^{-1}\) to row \(3\) of \(E_3^{-1}\). Because row \(1\) of \(E_3^{-1}\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\)—it has ZERO in column \(2\)!—adding \(l_{31}\) times row \(1\) places \(l_{31}\) in slot \((3, 1)\) while leaving \(l_{32}\) in slot \((3, 2)\) completely undisturbed:
\[
E_2^{-1} E_3^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
- Next, \(E_1^{-1}\) acts on \(E_2^{-1} E_3^{-1}\) by adding \(l_{21}\) times ROW \(1\) to row \(2\). Again row \(1\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), so it puts \(l_{21}\) into slot \((2, 1)\) and touches nothing else:
\[
L = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} E_3^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}!
\]
The exact same proof works for any size \(m \times m\): whenever an earlier factor \(E_{\text{early}}^{-1}\) (operating from column \(p\)) left-multiplies the accumulated product of all later factors (which only hold multipliers in columns \(q \ge p\), never in row \(p\)), row \(p\) of that right-hand product is STILL the untouched standard row \(\mathbf{e}_p^T\). Therefore adding \(l_{ip}\) times row \(p\) to row \(i\) writes \(l_{ip}\) into slot \((i, p)\) and adds \(0\) everywhere else!

By contrast, if you compute \(L^{-1} = E_3 E_2 E_1\), \(E_3\) acts on the left by subtracting \(l_{32}\) times row \(2\) (which ALREADY contains \(-l_{21}\) in column \(1\)!) from row \(3\), creating a cross-term \(+l_{32} l_{21}\) in entry \((3, 1)\). That is why we factor \(A = LU\) rather than storing \(L^{-1} A = U\): \(L\) keeps every elimination multiplier clean in place.

[CALLOUT]
id: lu-theorem
label: Theorem
title: \(LU\) factorization without row swaps
tone: key

If forward Gaussian elimination reduces \(A\) to an upper-triangular (row-echelon) matrix \(U\) using ONLY downward row replacements \(R_i \leftarrow R_i - l_{ij} R_j\) (\(i > j\), moving left to right across columns), then
\[
A = LU,
\]
where \(L\) is the unit lower-triangular matrix (\(1\)'s on the main diagonal, \(0\)'s above) whose entry in position \((i, j)\) for \(i > j\) is simply the multiplier \(l_{ij}\) used to eliminate position \((i, j)\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us compute an \(LU\) factorization by hand for
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 4 & 9 & -3 \\ -2 & -3 & 7 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Column \(1\) (pivot \(2\)):
- To eliminate \(4\) in \((2, 1)\): multiplier \(l_{21} = 4/2 = 2\), perform \(R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2 R_1\).
- To eliminate \(-2\) in \((3, 1)\): multiplier \(l_{31} = -2/2 = -1\), perform \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - (-1) R_1 = R_3 + R_1\).
Intermediate matrix:
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Column \(2\) (pivot \(1\)):
- To eliminate \(1\) in \((3, 2)\): multiplier \(l_{32} = 1/1 = 1\), perform \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 1 R_2\).
We obtain
\[
U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Verify by multiplying \(LU\):
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 4 & 9 & -3 \\ -2 & -3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} = A.
\]
Watch the sign convention carefully: the multiplier recorded in \(L\) is the number SUBTRACTED in \(R_i \leftarrow R_i - l_{ij} R_j\). Because row \(3\) used \(R_3 + R_1 = R_3 - (-1) R_1\), the entry \(l_{31}\) is \(-1\), NOT \(+1\)!

=== SECTION 2: Solving \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) via triangular solves and cost analysis ===

Once \(A = LU\) is known, solving \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) splits cleanly into two fast triangular systems. Rewrite \(LU\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) by defining the intermediate vector \(\mathbf{y} = U\mathbf{x}\):

1. Forward substitution: solve the lower-triangular system \(L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}\) from top to bottom (\(y_1\), then \(y_2\), ..., up to \(y_n\)).
2. Back substitution: solve the upper-triangular system \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\) from bottom to top (\(x_n\), then \(x_{n-1}\), ..., up to \(x_1\)).

Verify why this solves the original system: \(A\mathbf{x} = (LU)\mathbf{x} = L(U\mathbf{x}) = L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}\).

Let us solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\) using the \(L\) and \(U\) we found in Section 1:
- Forward substitution \(L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}\):
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
\implies
\begin{aligned}
y_1 &= 2, \\
2(2) + y_2 &= 8 \implies y_2 = 4, \\
-1(2) + 1(4) + y_3 &= 6 \implies y_3 = 4.
\end{aligned}
\]
- Back substitution \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\):
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}
\implies
\begin{aligned}
4x_3 &= 4 \implies x_3 = 1, \\
x_2 + 1 &= 4 \implies x_2 = 3, \\
2x_1 + 4(3) - 2(1) &= 2 \implies 2x_1 = -8 \implies x_1 = -4.
\end{aligned}
\]
Check directly: \(A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2(-4) + 4(3) - 2(1) \\ 4(-4) + 9(3) - 3(1) \\ -2(-4) - 3(3) + 7(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\). Exact!

Now count the floating-point operations (additions, subtractions, multiplications, and divisions) for a large \(n \times n\) system:
- Computing \(A = LU\) once takes roughly \(\frac{2}{3} n^3\) operations—growing cubically, written \(O(n^3)\).
- Each forward substitution \(L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}\) takes roughly \(n^2\) operations, and each back substitution \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\) takes roughly \(n^2\) operations—together about \(2 n^2\) operations per right-hand side, written \(O(n^2)\).

When you solve \(A\mathbf{x}_j = \mathbf{b}_j\) for \(k\) different right-hand sides:
- Fresh Gaussian elimination each time costs \(\approx \frac{2}{3} k n^3\).
- Factoring \(A = LU\) ONCE and running \(k\) triangular solve pairs costs \(\approx \frac{2}{3} n^3 + 2 k n^2\).

For \(n = 1000\) and \(k = 20\), repeated elimination needs \(\approx 1.33 \times 10^{10}\) operations, whereas one \(LU\) plus \(20\) triangular solves needs only \(\approx 7.07 \times 10^8\) operations—nearly \(19\) times faster! Even compared to computing \(A^{-1}\) once (\(\approx 2 n^3\)) plus \(k\) matrix-vector products (\(\approx 2 k n^2\)), \(LU\) saves roughly \(\frac{4}{3} n^3\) operations up front while having the same \(2 n^2\) cost per extra right-hand side.

=== SECTION 3: Permutation matrices and \(PA = LU\) factorization ===

Not every invertible matrix admits a swap-free factorization \(A = LU\). Take the simplest possible example:
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
If \(A = LU\) with unit lower-triangular \(L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ l & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and upper-triangular \(U = \begin{bmatrix} u_{11} & u_{12} \\ 0 & u_{22} \end{bmatrix}\), then entry \((1, 1)\) forces \(u_{11} = 0\), which then makes entry \((2, 1) = l u_{11} = 0 \neq 1\)—impossible! Forward elimination requires a row swap whenever a zero sits in a needed pivot position (and, as we will see in numerical conditioning, row swaps are also used to avoid tiny pivots).

To incorporate row swaps cleanly inside matrix factorization, we track all swaps using a permutation matrix.

[CALLOUT]
id: perm-matrix-def
label: Definition
title: Permutation matrix and its inverse
tone: key

An \(m \times m\) permutation matrix \(P\) is any matrix obtained from \(I_m\) by reordering its rows (equivalently, any product \(P = E_r \cdots E_1\) of row-swap elementary matrices). Every row and every column of \(P\) contains a single \(1\) and zeros elsewhere.

Every permutation matrix \(P\) is invertible, and its inverse is simply its transpose:
\[
P^{-1} = P^T.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Let us prove \(P^{-1} = P^T\) in one line! Every single row-swap elementary matrix \(E\) is both symmetric (\(E^T = E\)) and its own inverse (\(E^{-1} = E\)). Writing \(P = E_r \cdots E_2 E_1\) and applying order reversal for both inverses and transposes gives
\[
P^{-1} = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} \cdots E_r^{-1} = E_1^T E_2^T \cdots E_r^T = (E_r \cdots E_2 E_1)^T = P^T.
\]
Beware: while a SINGLE swap satisfies \(P^{-1} = P\), a product of two or more swaps need NOT satisfy \(P^{-1} = P\)—you must take the transpose \(P^T\)!

Now suppose we perform Gaussian elimination on a general matrix \(A\), swapping rows whenever a zero pivot is encountered. The result is a factorization
\[
P A = L U,
\]
where \(P\) records the cumulative row swaps, \(L\) is unit lower-triangular, and \(U\) is in row-echelon (upper-triangular) form. Think of \(PA = LU\) as saying: "if we had known all the needed row swaps in advance and pre-permuted the rows of \(A\) into \(PA\), then \(PA\) would have reduced cleanly to \(U\) without any swaps at all!"

There is ONE crucial bookkeeping rule when building \(P\), \(L\), and \(U\) simultaneously on the fly: whenever you swap rows \(i\) and \(j\) at step \(k\), you MUST swap:
1. Rows \(i\) and \(j\) of the working matrix \(U\);
2. Rows \(i\) and \(j\) of \(P\) (starting from \(P = I_m\));
3. The ALREADY-RECORDED subdiagonal multipliers in rows \(i\) and \(j\) of \(L\) (entries in columns \(1, \dots, k - 1\) only—keep the \(1\)'s on the main diagonal and \(0\)'s above untouched!).

Let us watch WHY rule 3 matters on a concrete \(3 \times 3\) example where the swap happens in column \(2\), AFTER column \(1\) multipliers have already been recorded:
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Start with \(P = I_3\) and \(L\) having \(1\)'s on the diagonal and blank subdiagonal slots.
- Column \(1\): multipliers \(l_{21} = 2\) and \(l_{31} = -1\). Perform \(R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2 R_1\) and \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + R_1\):
\[
U^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\text{recorded col 1 of } L: \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & * & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
- Column \(2\): entry \((2, 2)\) is \(0\), so we must swap \(R_2 \leftrightarrow R_3\)! Applying all three swap updates (including swapping \(2\) and \(-1\) in column \(1\) of \(L\), while leaving the diagonal \(1\)'s in place!) gives:
\[
U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Notice that after the swap, entry \((3, 2)\) of \(U\) is already \(0\), so \(l_{32} = 0\). Check \(PA = LU\) directly:
\[
PA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
LU = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = PA!
\]
Had we forgotten to swap the already-recorded multipliers \(2\) and \(-1\) in column \(1\) of \(L\), \(LU\) would have failed to match \(PA\).

Finally, how do we solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) once we have \(PA = LU\)? Multiply both sides of \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) on the left by \(P\):
\[
PA\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{b} \implies LU\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{b}.
\]
Thus you simply permute the right-hand side into \(P\mathbf{b}\), solve \(L\mathbf{y} = P\mathbf{b}\) by forward substitution, and solve \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\) by back substitution!

[TABLE]
Stage of solving \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) via \(PA = LU\) | Operation | Cost
1. Factor once | Find \(P, L, U\) with \(PA = LU\) | \(\approx \frac{2}{3} n^3\)
2. Permute right-hand side | Compute \(\tilde{\mathbf{b}} = P\mathbf{b}\) (reorder entries) | Negligible (\(n\) moves)
3. Forward substitution | Solve \(L\mathbf{y} = \tilde{\mathbf{b}}\) | \(\approx n^2\)
4. Back substitution | Solve \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\) | \(\approx n^2\)
[/TABLE]
