=== SECTION 1: Vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and linear combinations ===

In two- and three-dimensional coordinate geometry, a point or displacement is listed as an ordered pair or triple of real numbers. When a linear system has \(n\) unknowns or \(m\) equations, it is far more illuminating to package those \(n\) numbers into a single object—a column vector—so that an entire system of equations can be read as one geometric question about combining vectors.

[CALLOUT]
id: vector-ops-def
label: Definition
title: Column vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), addition, and scalar multiplication
tone: key

The space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) consists of all ordered lists of \(n\) real numbers written vertically as column vectors:
\[
\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) are equal if and only if they have the same size \(n\) and matching entries \(u_i = v_i\) for every \(i = 1, \dots, n\). For \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and any scalar \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), vector addition and scalar multiplication are defined entry by entry:
\[
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_n + v_n \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
c\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} c u_1 \\ c u_2 \\ \vdots \\ c u_n \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The vector whose entries are all zero is the zero vector \(\mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^n\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice that addition is defined only between vectors of the same size \(n\): you cannot add a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) to a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Because both operations act independently on each coordinate, the familiar arithmetic laws of real numbers carry over immediately to \(\mathbb{R}^n\): addition is commutative (\(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\)) and associative (\((\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})\)), \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}\), and scalar multiplication distributes over both vector addition and scalar addition: \(c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}\) and \((c + d)\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}\).

Combining scaling and addition across several vectors produces the single most important construction in linear algebra: a linear combination.

[CALLOUT]
id: lincomb-span-def
label: Definition
title: Linear combination and span
tone: key

Given vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^m\) and scalars (called weights) \(c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}\), the vector
\[
\mathbf{y} = c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k \mathbf{v}_k
\]
is called a linear combination of \(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\).

The set of all linear combinations of \(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\) is called the span of those vectors and is denoted
\[
\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \{ c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_k \mathbf{v}_k : c_1, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} \} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

To see a linear combination geometrically in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), take \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\). With weights \(c_1 = 2\) and \(c_2 = 1\), we scale \(\mathbf{v}_1\) by \(2\) to reach \((4, 2)\) and then move from \((4, 2)\) by \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1, 2)\) to land at
\[
2\mathbf{v}_1 + 1\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The graph below displays this tip-to-tail construction; drag the slider \(s\) to watch how varying the weight on \(\mathbf{v}_2\) sweeps \(2\mathbf{v}_1 + s\mathbf{v}_2\) along a line parallel to \(\mathbf{v}_2\).

What geometric shapes can \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) take inside \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\mathbb{R}^3\)? First observe that \(\mathbf{0}\) belongs to EVERY span, because choosing all weights \(c_1 = \dots = c_k = 0\) always gives \(\mathbf{0}\). Beyond the origin:
- For a single nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) or \(\mathbb{R}^3\), \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}\} = \{c\mathbf{v} : c \in \mathbb{R}\}\) is the line through the origin in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\).
- For two vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): if neither is a scalar multiple of the other (they are not collinear), then every target point in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) can be reached by traveling along \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and then along \(\mathbf{v}_2\), so \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\} = \mathbb{R}^2\). If one IS a scalar multiple of the other—say \(\mathbf{v}_2 = -2\mathbf{v}_1\) with \(\mathbf{v}_1 \neq \mathbf{0}\)—then \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2(-2\mathbf{v}_1) = (c_1 - 2c_2)\mathbf{v}_1\) never leaves the line \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1\}\).
- For two non-collinear vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}\) is the flat plane through the origin containing both \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\). Adding a third vector \(\mathbf{v}_3\) enlarges the span to all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) if \(\mathbf{v}_3\) points out of that plane, but leaves the span unchanged as that same plane if \(\mathbf{v}_3\) already lies inside \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}\).

Notice the key lesson: counting how many vectors are listed is not enough to know the shape of their span. You must ask whether each new vector actually points in a direction not already reachable from the others.

How do we test concretely whether a target vector \(\mathbf{b}\) belongs to \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\)? We ask whether there exist scalars \(c_1, \dots, c_k\) solving the vector equation
\[
c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k \mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{b}.
\]
Comparing coordinate by coordinate converts this single vector equation directly into a system of \(m\) linear equations in the \(k\) unknowns \(c_1, \dots, c_k\). For instance, to test whether \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}\) lies in \(\operatorname{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}\), we solve
\[
c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}
\iff
\begin{aligned}
c_1 + 2c_2 &= 5, \\
c_2 &= 1, \\
2c_1 + c_2 &= 7.
\end{aligned}
\]
The second equation fixes \(c_2 = 1\); the first then gives \(c_1 = 5 - 2(1) = 3\); checking the third gives \(2(3) + 1 = 7\), which matches. Thus \(\mathbf{b} = 3\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 \in \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}\). Had the third entry of \(\mathbf{b}\) been \(8\) instead of \(7\), the third equation \(2(3) + 1 = 8\) would fail, proving that \(\mathbf{b}\) lies outside the plane \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}\).

=== SECTION 2: The matrix-vector product \(A\mathbf{x}\) ===

When we collect \(n\) column vectors \(\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n \in \mathbb{R}^m\) side by side, they form an \(m \times n\) matrix
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Taking a linear combination of those columns with weights \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\)—the entries of a vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\)—occurs so constantly that we give it its own multiplication notation.

[CALLOUT]
id: matvec-def
label: Definition
title: Matrix-vector product (column-combination definition)
tone: key

Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix}\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix with columns \(\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n \in \mathbb{R}^m\), and let \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n\). The matrix-vector product \(A\mathbf{x}\) is the vector in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) defined as the linear combination of the columns of \(A\) using the entries of \(\mathbf{x}\) as weights:
\[
A\mathbf{x} = x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \dots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Check the sizes carefully: \(A\mathbf{x}\) is defined ONLY when the number of columns of \(A\) equals the number of entries of \(\mathbf{x}\). An \(m \times n\) matrix multiplies an \(n \times 1\) column vector to produce an \(m \times 1\) column vector—the inner dimensions \(n\) match and disappear, leaving \(m\).

Let us compute an example by the column rule:
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 4 \\ 3 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
= 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + 5 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + (-1) \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 10 - 4 \\ 6 + 0 + 1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -12 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Notice one especially useful special case of [REF: matvec-def | the column definition]. Let \(\mathbf{e}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n\) denote the \(j\)-th standard basis vector—the vector with \(1\) in entry \(j\) and \(0\) in every other entry. Then
\[
A\mathbf{e}_j = 0\mathbf{a}_1 + \dots + 1\mathbf{a}_j + \dots + 0\mathbf{a}_n = \mathbf{a}_j.
\]
Multiplying \(A\) by \(\mathbf{e}_j\) simply extracts the \(j\)-th column of \(A\).

There is a second, equivalent way to compute \(A\mathbf{x}\) one entry at a time. Write out the \(i\)-th coordinate of the sum \(x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + \dots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n\): since the \(i\)-th entry of column \(\mathbf{a}_j\) is \(a_{ij}\), the \(i\)-th entry of \(A\mathbf{x}\) is
\[
(A\mathbf{x})_i = x_1 a_{i1} + x_2 a_{i2} + \dots + x_n a_{in} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j.
\]
That sum is precisely the dot product of the \(i\)-th row of \(A\) with the column vector \(\mathbf{x}\). We have thus proved that the column-combination rule and the row-by-column dot-product rule always produce the exact same vector!

In the example above, the row-dot-product rule gives:
- Row \(1\) dotted with \(\mathbf{x}\): \(1(2) + (-2)(5) + 4(-1) = 2 - 10 - 4 = -12\).
- Row \(2\) dotted with \(\mathbf{x}\): \(3(2) + 0(5) + (-1)(-1) = 6 + 0 + 1 = 7\).

Both viewpoints are valuable for different reasons. The row-by-column rule is fast when computing individual numeric entries by hand, while the column-combination definition immediately reveals what \(A\mathbf{x}\) means geometrically and algebraically: the set of all possible outputs \(A\mathbf{x}\) as \(\mathbf{x}\) varies over \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is literally \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n\}\).

[TABLE]
Perspective | Formula | Best used for
Column combination | \(A\mathbf{x} = x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + \dots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n\) | Understanding span, consistency, and zero entries in \(\mathbf{x}\)
Row-by-column dot product | \((A\mathbf{x})_i = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j\) | Computing a single coordinate of \(A\mathbf{x}\) or reading back scalar equations
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 3: Linearity of \(A\mathbf{x}\) and the three-way equivalence ===

Why does matrix-vector multiplication sit at the center of linear algebra? Because for any fixed \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\), the operation \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}\) obeys two exact algebraic rules called linearity.

[CALLOUT]
id: matvec-linearity
label: Theorem
title: Algebraic linearity of the matrix-vector product
tone: key

Let \(A\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix, let \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), and let \(c \in \mathbb{R}\). Then:

(1) Additivity: \(A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v}\).
(2) Homogeneity: \(A(c\mathbf{u}) = c(A\mathbf{u})\).

Combining both rules yields \(A(c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v}) = c(A\mathbf{u}) + d(A\mathbf{v})\) for all scalars \(c, d \in \mathbb{R}\), and in particular \(A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Both properties follow in one line from [REF: matvec-def | the column definition]. Writing \(\mathbf{u}\) with entries \(u_j\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) with entries \(v_j\), the \(j\)-th entry of \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) is \(u_j + v_j\), so
\[
A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})
= \sum_{j=1}^n (u_j + v_j)\mathbf{a}_j
= \sum_{j=1}^n u_j \mathbf{a}_j + \sum_{j=1}^n v_j \mathbf{a}_j
= A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v}.
\]
Similarly, the \(j\)-th entry of \(c\mathbf{u}\) is \(c u_j\), so
\[
A(c\mathbf{u})
= \sum_{j=1}^n (c u_j)\mathbf{a}_j
= c \sum_{j=1}^n u_j \mathbf{a}_j
= c(A\mathbf{u}).
\]
Setting \(c = 0\) gives \(A\mathbf{0} = 0(A\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}\). Notice how powerful [REF: matvec-linearity | linearity] is: even if you are never told a single entry of \(A\), knowing \(A\mathbf{u}\) and \(A\mathbf{v}\) immediately determines \(A(c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v})\) for every linear combination of \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\).

We can now unite the two languages we have built—linear systems of equations and linear combinations of column vectors—into a single three-way equivalence.

[CALLOUT]
id: three-way-equiv
label: Theorem
title: Three-way equivalence for linear systems
tone: key

Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix}\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix with entries \(a_{ij}\), let \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), and let \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^m\). The following three equations have the exact same solution set:

(1) The linear system of \(m\) equations in \(n\) unknowns:
\[
a_{i1} x_1 + a_{i2} x_2 + \dots + a_{in} x_n = b_i \qquad (i = 1, \dots, m).
\]
(2) The vector equation:
\[
x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \dots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n = \mathbf{b}.
\]
(3) The matrix equation:
\[
A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}.
\]

Consequently, the matrix equation \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) is consistent if and only if \(\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n\}\).
[/CALLOUT]

The proof is already in our hands. By [REF: matvec-def | the column definition], \(A\mathbf{x}\) and \(x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + \dots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n\) are literally the same vector, so (2) and (3) are identical. By the row-dot-product formula proved in Section 2, the \(i\)-th entry of \(A\mathbf{x}\) is \(a_{i1} x_1 + \dots + a_{in} x_n\); since two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) are equal if and only if all \(m\) of their entries agree, \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) holds if and only if every equation in (1) holds. Finally, by [REF: lincomb-span-def | the definition of span], weights \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) solving (2) exist if and only if \(\mathbf{b} \in \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n\}\).

Look at what this equivalence achieves conceptually. Consider the \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\). Its second column is \(-2\) times its first column, so \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2\}\) is just the line through the origin along \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\). By [REF: three-way-equiv | the three-way equivalence], the system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) is consistent if and only if \(\mathbf{b}\) lies on that line (for example, \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\) works, whereas \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\) fails).
