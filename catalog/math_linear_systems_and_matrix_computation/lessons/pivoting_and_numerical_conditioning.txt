=== SECTION 1: How exact algebra fails in finite precision ===

In exact arithmetic, Gaussian elimination only requires a row swap when a pivot is literally \(0\). On a real computer—which stores every number to a fixed number of significant digits (such as \(7\) decimal digits in single precision or about \(16\) in double precision)—a TINY nonzero pivot can be far more dangerous than a zero pivot! A zero pivot halts the algorithm loudly; a tiny pivot silently destroys every digit of the answer.

Watch this catastrophe happen in \(3\)-significant-digit rounding arithmetic on a completely ordinary, well-behaved \(2 \times 2\) system:
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 0.0001 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
First find the EXACT solution for comparison. Subtracting \(10000\) times row \(1\) from row \(2\) yields \(-9999 x_2 = -9998\), so
\[
x_2 = \frac{9998}{9999} \approx 0.999900, \qquad
x_1 = \frac{1 - x_2}{0.0001} = \frac{10000}{9999} \approx 1.000100.
\]
Rounded to \(3\) significant digits, the true answer is \((x_1, x_2) = (1.00, 1.00)\).

Now run Gaussian elimination WITHOUT pivoting, rounding the result of EVERY arithmetic operation to \(3\) significant digits (standard floating-point model):
1. Multiplier: \(l_{21} = 1 / 0.0001 = 10000 = 1.00 \times 10^4\).
2. Update entry \((2, 2)\): \(1 - 10000(1) = -9999 \xrightarrow{\text{3 sig figs}} -1.00 \times 10^4\).
3. Update right-hand side \(b_2\): \(2 - 10000(1) = -9998 \xrightarrow{\text{3 sig figs}} -1.00 \times 10^4\).
The reduced system in \(3\)-digit arithmetic is
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 0.0001 & 1.00 \\ 0 & -1.00 \times 10^4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}_1 \\ \hat{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.00 \\ -1.00 \times 10^4 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
4. Back-substitute:
- From row \(2\): \(\hat{x}_2 = (-1.00 \times 10^4) / (-1.00 \times 10^4) = 1.00\).
- From row \(1\): \(0.0001 \hat{x}_1 + 1.00(1.00) = 1.00 \implies 0.0001 \hat{x}_1 = 0.00 \implies \hat{x}_1 = 0.00\)!

Look at that disaster: \(\hat{x}_1 = 0.00\) instead of \(1.00\)—a \(100\%\) error! Notice the exact mechanism that wiped out the answer: dividing by the tiny pivot \(0.0001\) created a massive multiplier \(l_{21} = 10^4\). When \(1 - 10^4\) and \(2 - 10^4\) were rounded to \(3\) digits, the numbers \(1\) and \(2\) from the second equation were completely swamped and erased, leaving row \(2\) carrying zero information about the original \(x_1 + x_2 = 2\)!

Now solve the EXACT same system in the EXACT same \(3\)-significant-digit arithmetic, except swap rows \(1\) and \(2\) first so the larger entry \(1\) becomes the pivot:
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0.0001 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
1. Multiplier: \(l_{21} = 0.0001 / 1 = 0.0001\) (tiny!).
2. Update entry \((2, 2)\): \(1 - 0.0001(1) = 0.9999 \xrightarrow{\text{3 sig figs}} 1.00\).
3. Update \(b_2\): \(1 - 0.0001(2) = 0.9998 \xrightarrow{\text{3 sig figs}} 1.00\).
4. Back-substitute: \(\hat{x}_2 = 1.00 / 1.00 = 1.00\), and \(\hat{x}_1 = 2.00 - 1.00 = 1.00\)!

Every single significant digit is now exact! Simply swapping rows before eliminating prevented the huge multiplier and saved the computation.

=== SECTION 2: Partial pivoting and scaled partial pivoting in \(PA = LU\) ===

In the \(PA = LU\) lesson, we swapped rows only when a pivot was \(0\). To prevent large multipliers from destroying digits in finite precision, standard numerical solvers ALWAYS pivot—even when the current diagonal entry is nonzero!

[CALLOUT]
id: partial-pivoting-def
label: Method
title: Partial pivoting and scaled partial pivoting
tone: key

At elimination step \(k\) (clearing column \(k\) below row \(k\)):

(1) Partial pivoting: scan entries \(a_{ik}\) in column \(k\) for \(i = k, k+1, \dots, m\) and choose a pivot row \(p \ge k\) maximizing the absolute value \(\lvert a_{ik} \rvert\). Swap row \(k\) and row \(p\) (updating \(U\), \(P\), and the already-recorded entries in columns \(1, \dots, k - 1\) of \(L\)). Because \(\lvert a_{ik} \rvert \le \lvert a_{pk} \rvert\) for all \(i > k\), every multiplier satisfies
\[
\lvert l_{ik} \rvert = \frac{\lvert a_{ik} \rvert}{\lvert a_{pk} \rvert} \le 1.
\]

(2) Scaled partial pivoting: first compute each row's scale factor \(s_i = \max_{1 \le j \le n} \lvert a_{ij} \rvert\) (the largest magnitude in row \(i\) of the original matrix \(A\)). At step \(k\), choose the row \(p \ge k\) maximizing the relative ratio \(\lvert a_{ik} \rvert / s_i\) (comparing each candidate pivot against the size of its own row), and swap rows \(k\) and \(p\) (along with their scale factors \(s_k\) and \(s_p\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us execute \(PA = LU\) with [REF: partial-pivoting-def.1 | partial pivoting] on a concrete \(3 \times 3\) matrix:
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -4 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Start with \(P = I_3\) and unit lower-triangular \(L\) with blank subdiagonal entries.
- Step 1 (column \(1\)): compare magnitudes \(\lvert 1 \rvert = 1\), \(\lvert -4 \rvert = 4\), \(\lvert 2 \rvert = 2\). The largest is \(4\) in row \(2\), so swap \(R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2\) in both the working matrix and \(P\):
\[
\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
P^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Multipliers: \(l_{21} = 1 / (-4) = -0.25\) and \(l_{31} = 2 / (-4) = -0.5\) (both have magnitude \(\le 1\)!). Apply \(R_2 \leftarrow R_2 + 0.25 R_1\) and \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + 0.5 R_1\):
\[
U^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2.25 & 1.5 \\ 0 & -2.5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\text{col 1 of } L: \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -0.25 & 1 & 0 \\ -0.5 & * & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
- Step 2 (column \(2\), rows \(2\)–\(3\)): compare magnitudes \(\lvert 2.25 \rvert = 2.25\) and \(\lvert -2.5 \rvert = 2.5\). The largest is \(2.5\) in row \(3\), so swap \(R_2 \leftrightarrow R_3\) in \(U^{(1)}\), in \(P^{(1)}\), AND in column \(1\) of \(L\) (swapping \(-0.25\) and \(-0.5\) while leaving the diagonal \(1\)'s untouched!):
\[
\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2.5 & 6 \\ 0 & 2.25 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\text{col 1 of } L: \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 1 & 0 \\ -0.25 & * & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Now compute \(l_{32} = 2.25 / (-2.5) = -0.9\) (again \(\lvert l_{32} \rvert \le 1\)!) and apply \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + 0.9 R_2\):
\[
U = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2.5 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 6.9 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 1 & 0 \\ -0.25 & -0.9 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Verify \(LU = PA\): row \(1\) of \(LU\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = R_2(A)\); row \(2\) is \((-0.5)\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2.5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -3 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = R_3(A)\); row \(3\) is \((-0.25)\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} + (-0.9)\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2.5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 6.9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = R_1(A)\). Exact!

Why does [REF: partial-pivoting-def.2 | scaled partial pivoting] exist if plain partial pivoting already guarantees \(\lvert l_{ik} \rvert \le 1\)? Because row scaling can FOOL plain partial pivoting! Take our Section 1 system and simply multiply equation \(1\) by \(10^5\) (changing units, say from meters to \(10^{-5}\) meters):
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 10 & 10^5 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10^5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Plain partial pivoting compares \(\lvert 10 \rvert = 10\) against \(\lvert 1 \rvert = 1\), decides \(10 > 1\), does NOT swap, uses multiplier \(l_{21} = 0.1 \le 1\)—and STILL suffers the exact same catastrophe in \(3\)-digit arithmetic: \(u_{22} = \operatorname{fl}(1 - 0.1 \cdot 10^5) = -1.00 \times 10^4\), \(\tilde{b}_2 = \operatorname{fl}(2 - 0.1 \cdot 10^5) = -1.00 \times 10^4\), yielding \(\hat{x}_2 = 1.00\) and \(\hat{x}_1 = 0.00\)! Even though \(\lvert l_{21} \rvert = 0.1\) looked small, the entry \(10\) was STILL tiny relative to \(10^5\) in its own row!

Now apply scaled partial pivoting:
- Row scales: \(s_1 = \max(10, 10^5) = 10^5\), \(s_2 = \max(1, 1) = 1\).
- Relative ratios in column \(1\):
\[
\text{Row 1: } \frac{\lvert 10 \rvert}{10^5} = 10^{-4}, \qquad
\text{Row 2: } \frac{\lvert 1 \rvert}{1} = 1.
\]
Because \(1 > 10^{-4}\), scaled partial pivoting immediately sees that row \(1\)'s entry is tiny on its own row's scale, swaps \(R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2\), and recovers \((\hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_2) = (1.00, 1.00)\) accurately!

=== SECTION 3: Ill-conditioned systems and the small-residual trap ===

In Section 1, the system \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.0001 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) was completely WELL-CONDITIONED: its two equations represent nearly perpendicular lines (slopes \(-0.0001\) and \(-1\)), so tiny changes in the input data produce tiny changes in the exact solution. The failure without pivoting was algorithmic instability—a bad elimination order, completely cured by pivoting.

Now contrast that with an intrinsically ILL-CONDITIONED system, where the problem itself is hypersensitive to tiny changes in \(A\) or \(\mathbf{b}\), EVEN IN EXACT ARITHMETIC! Consider the \(2 \times 2\) system
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.05 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.05 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The two lines \(x_1 + x_2 = 2\) (slope \(-1\)) and \(x_1 + 1.05 x_2 = 2.05\) (slope \(-1/1.05 \approx -0.952\)) are nearly parallel! At \(d = 0\), the exact intersection is \((x_1, x_2) = (1, 1)\).

Now perturb the second right-hand-side entry by a tiny amount \(d\): \(\mathbf{b}(d) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.05 + d \end{bmatrix}\). Subtracting equation \(1\) from equation \(2\) gives \(0.05 x_2 = 0.05 + d\), so the EXACT solution of the perturbed system is
\[
x_2 = 1 + 20 d, \qquad x_1 = 1 - 20 d.
\]
Every tiny perturbation \(d\) is amplified by a factor of \(20\)! Setting \(d = 0.05\) changes \(b_2\) by less than \(2.5\%\) (from \(2.05\) to \(2.10\)), yet shifts the exact intersection from \((1, 1)\) all the way to \((0, 2)\)—a \(100\%\) change in both coordinates! And if \(1.05\) were \(1.001\), the amplification factor would jump from \(20\) to \(1000\)!

No pivoting strategy can erase intrinsic ill-conditioning: pivoting prevents the algorithm from injecting unnecessary extra error, but once input measurements or rounding introduce even a tiny perturbation into \(A\) or \(\mathbf{b}\), the EXACT solution of the perturbed problem has already moved drastically. (Formal quantitative measurement of this sensitivity via matrix-norm condition numbers \(\kappa(A)\) and singular values is developed in later numerical linear algebra and inner-product modules.)

Ill-conditioning also produces one of the most dangerous traps in practical computation: trusting the residual blindly!

[CALLOUT]
id: small-residual-trap
label: Common pitfall
title: A tiny residual does NOT guarantee an accurate solution
tone: caution

Given a candidate solution \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) to \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\), its residual vector is \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) and its true error is \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}\). They are linked by
\[
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{e}.
\]
When \(A\) is ill-conditioned (close to a singular matrix), there exist large nonzero vectors \(\mathbf{e}\) that \(A\) nearly annihilates, making \(A\mathbf{e}\) tiny even when \(\mathbf{e}\) is huge! Therefore a small residual \(\mathbf{r}\) proves only that \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) solves a nearby problem \(A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{r}\)—NOT that \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) is close to the true solution \(\mathbf{x}\).
[/CALLOUT]

See [REF: small-residual-trap | this trap] in action on our exact \(2 \times 2\) system \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.05 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.05 \end{bmatrix}\) (true solution \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)). Suppose someone hands you the terribly inaccurate approximation \(\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) (with huge error \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\), \(100\%\) off in every component!). Compute its residual:
\[
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.05 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.05 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.05 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.10 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -0.05 \end{bmatrix}!
\]
The residual is \(20\) times smaller than the true error because \(\mathbf{e} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) lies right in the null space of the nearby singular matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\)! Plugging \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) back into \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) and seeing a tiny discrepancy can lull an unwary solver into trusting a completely wrong answer.

[TABLE]
Failure mode | Root cause | Diagnostic clue | Remedy
Algorithmic instability (poor pivot order) | Tiny pivot creates huge multipliers, swamping digits during elimination | Well-conditioned matrix, yet unpivoted elimination loses accuracy while pivoted elimination succeeds | Use partial pivoting (or scaled partial pivoting when row scales vary wildly)
Intrinsic ill-conditioning | Matrix \(A\) is close to singular (nearly parallel equations / nearly dependent columns) | Tiny perturbations in \(A\) or \(\mathbf{b}\) move the exact solution wildly; small residual hides huge error | Higher precision, problem reformulation, or regularization (pivoting alone cannot remove sensitivity)
[/TABLE]
