=== SECTION 1: Explore first — test small cases before choosing a proof method ===

In real mathematics, problems rarely arrive wearing a label like "Prove by contraposition" or "Find a counterexample." You are handed an unlabeled conjecture — "For every integer \(n\), \(n^2 + n + 17\) is prime," or "For all real numbers \(x, y\), if \(x + y \ge 2\), then \(x \ge 1\) or \(y \ge 1\)" — and you must decide BOTH whether it is true AND how to prove or refute it.

Rushing immediately into abstract symbols is the fastest way to waste twenty minutes trying to prove a FALSE statement. Always begin with a brief exploration phase: test small values and boundary cases (\(0, 1, -1, 2\), fractions like \(\frac{1}{2}\), negative numbers, equal variables \(x = y\)).
- If a single test case makes the hypothesis true and the conclusion false, STOP — you have just found a counterexample to a universal claim!
- If several small and boundary cases confirm the claim, watch WHY they worked: the concrete calculation almost always reveals the algebraic mechanism of the general proof.

Try this on two unlabeled conjectures right now:
1. Conjecture A: "For every integer \(n \ge 0\), the number \(n^2 + n + 17\) is prime."
   Test small values: \(n = 0 \to 17\) (prime), \(n = 1 \to 19\) (prime), \(n = 2 \to 23\) (prime), \(n = 3 \to 29\) (prime). Do not stop at four successes — test a structural boundary value! What if \(n = 17\) (or even \(n = 16\))? At \(n = 16\), \(16^2 + 16 + 17 = 16(17) + 17 = 17 \cdot 17 = 289\), which is NOT prime! One strategic boundary test refuted the conjecture immediately.
2. Conjecture B: "For all real numbers \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\), if \(x + y \ge 2\), then \(x \ge 1\) or \(y \ge 1\)."
   Test boundaries: \((1, 1)\) works; \((2, 0)\) works; \((1.5, 0.5)\) works. Trying to build a counterexample forces \(x < 1\) AND \(y < 1\) — which immediately adds to \(x + y < 2\), contradicting \(x + y \ge 2\)! That failed search for a counterexample literally handed us the contrapositive proof!

[CALLOUT]
id: workflow-unlabeled-claim
label: Workflow
title: Systematic workflow for an unlabeled mathematical claim
tone: key

(1) Parse the outer logical form and domain: identify all quantifiers (\(\forall, \exists, \exists!\)), their order, and the main connective (\(\Rightarrow\), \(\Leftrightarrow\), \(\vee\), \(\wedge\), or negation).
(2) Probe with small and edge cases (\(0, \pm 1, 2\), non-integers in \(\mathbb{Q}\) or \(\mathbb{R}\), equal inputs, extreme signs) to test whether the claim is plausible or false.
(3) If a universal claim fails on a test case, write a crisp, verified counterexample (checking domain membership, hypothesis, and failure of conclusion).
(4) If the claim appears true, match its outer logical structure to the cleanest proof method instead of defaulting automatically to contradiction.
[/CALLOUT]

The table below summarizes how the outer logical shape of a true claim maps onto your primary proof strategy:

[TABLE]
Outer logical shape or clue | Primary method to try first | Key trigger
\(\forall x \in D,\, P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\) with explicit algebraic \(P(x)\) | Direct proof | Unpacking \(P(x)\) gives formulas leading cleanly to \(Q(x)\)
\(\forall x \in D,\, P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\) where \(Q(x)\) is negative or \(Q_1 \vee Q_2\), or \(P(x)\) is composite | Proof by contraposition | \(\neg Q(x)\) gives a positive formula or rich conjunction \(\neg Q_1 \wedge \neg Q_2\)
Hypothesis or domain splits naturally (parity, sign, \(\lvert x \rvert\), remainders mod \(m\)) | Proof by exhaustive cases (with WLOG if symmetric) | Different regimes require different algebraic formulas
Nonexistence (\(\neg\exists\)), irrationality, infinitude, or \(P \Rightarrow Q\) needing BOTH \(P\) and \(\neg Q\) | Proof by contradiction | Assuming \(\neg S\) produces concrete objects that force \(R \wedge \neg R\)
\(\exists x,\, P(x)\) or \(\forall x \exists y,\, P(x, y)\) | Constructive existence | Solve backward in scratch work to build witness \(x\) (or \(y\) depending only on \(x\))
\(\exists! x,\, P(x)\) | Existence plus uniqueness | Show at least one witness exists, then assume \(P(x_1) \wedge P(x_2)\) and deduce \(x_1 = x_2\)
\(P \Leftrightarrow Q\) or TFAE for \(k\) statements | Two directions or strategic \(k\)-cycle | Mix methods across directions; order a \(k\)-cycle to minimize algebra
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 2: Choosing the cleanest proof method ===

Even when a claim is TRUE, multiple valid proof methods may succeed — yet one is dramatically cleaner, shorter, and less error-prone than the others. Good mathematical judgment means matching the method to the structure of the problem rather than forcing every theorem through proof by contradiction.

Compare two approaches to the theorem:
"For every integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\), \(n^3 - n\) is divisible by \(3\)."
- Clumsy attempt (contradiction): "Assume \(3 \nmid (n^3 - n)\)... wait, now what? Knowing \(n^3 - n = 3q + 1\) or \(3q + 2\) gives you a cubic equation you cannot factor for \(n\)."
- Clean choice (direct proof plus cases): First factor \(n^3 - n = n(n - 1)(n + 1) = (n - 1)n(n + 1)\), a product of three consecutive integers. Now split by remainder of \(n\) modulo \(3\):
  - If \(n = 3q\), then \(3 \mid n\), so \(3 \mid (n - 1)n(n + 1)\).
  - If \(n = 3q + 1\), then \(n - 1 = 3q\), so \(3 \mid (n - 1)n(n + 1)\).
  - If \(n = 3q + 2\), then \(n + 1 = 3(q + 1)\), so \(3 \mid (n - 1)n(n + 1)\).
  Every case finishes in one line!

Now compare two approaches to:
"For all real numbers \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\), if \(a \ne b\), then \(a^2 + b^2 > 2ab\)."
- Unnecessary contraposition: assuming \(a^2 + b^2 \le 2ab\) gives \((a - b)^2 \le 0\), forcing \((a - b)^2 = 0\), hence \(a = b\) (this works cleanly too!).
- Even more direct: assume \(a \ne b\). Then \(a - b \ne 0\), so \((a - b)^2 > 0\). Expanding gives \(a^2 - 2ab + b^2 > 0\), hence \(a^2 + b^2 > 2ab\).

And what about a mixed-quantifier claim like:
"For every rational number \(r \in \mathbb{Q}\), there exists a rational number \(s \in \mathbb{Q}\) such that \(r < s < r + 1\)"?
Here the outer quantifier is \(\forall r \in \mathbb{Q}\) and the inner quantifier is \(\exists s \in \mathbb{Q}\), so \(s\) is allowed to depend on \(r\). Scratch work suggests the midpoint \(s = r + \frac{1}{2}\): check domain membership (\(r \in \mathbb{Q} \Rightarrow r + \frac{1}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}\)) and check both inequalities (\(r < r + \frac{1}{2} < r + 1\)). Constructive existence solves it in two lines — no contradiction needed!

=== SECTION 3: Line-by-line proof auditing — diagnosing defects and salvageability ===

Reading a proof critically is just as important as writing one. A single flawed line can hide inside an otherwise persuasive paragraph — and worse, a flawed proof can be attached to either a TRUE theorem (salvageable by repairing the proof) or a FALSE claim (which must be refuted by a counterexample).

When auditing a proof, inspect every line against the seven classic defects catalogued below.

[CALLOUT]
id: audit-checklist
label: Checklist
title: Seven classic proof defects to audit line by line
tone: caution

(1) Circular reasoning (assuming the conclusion): using the target statement \(Q\) — or a statement equivalent to \(Q\) — before it has been proved, or arguing backward from \(Q\) using irreversible steps.
(2) Converse or inverse error: proving \(Q \Rightarrow P\) or \(\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q\) when the theorem requires \(P \Rightarrow Q\).
(3) Illegal algebraic operation: dividing by an expression that might be \(0\), multiplying an inequality by a potentially negative quantity without checking sign, or treating squaring as reversible.
(4) Witness reuse or unverified instantiation: using the SAME existential variable \(k\) for two independent objects (e.g., writing two arbitrary even numbers as \(2k\) and \(2k\)), or claiming an object exists without verifying domain membership.
(5) Reversed quantifier dependence: proving \(\exists y \forall x\, P(x, y)\) by letting \(y\) depend on \(x\) (which only proves \(\forall x \exists y\, P(x, y)\)).
(6) Incomplete case split or illicit WLOG: overlooking a boundary case (such as \(x = 0\) or a remainder class) or invoking WLOG when the statement is NOT symmetric in the swapped variables.
(7) Faulty negation of compound or quantified statements: negating \(P \Rightarrow Q\) as \(\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q\), failing to flip \(\wedge/\vee\) under De Morgan's laws, or mis-negating nested quantifiers in a contradiction/contrapositive setup.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us audit two realistic flawed proofs line by line — identifying BOTH the exact defect AND whether the underlying claim is true (salvageable) or false.

Audit Case 1:
Claim: "For every real number \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), if \(x^2 = 3x\), then \(x = 3\)."
Purported proof:
- Line 1: Let \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) be arbitrary and assume \(x^2 = 3x\).
- Line 2: Divide both sides by \(x\) to obtain \(x = 3\).
- Line 3: Therefore \(x = 3\).

Diagnosis:
- Broken step: Line 2 commits [REF: audit-checklist.3 | illegal division by zero] because \(x\) could equal \(0\).
- Salvageable or false? Test the boundary case \(x = 0\): \(0^2 = 3(0)\) is TRUE, yet \(0 = 3\) is FALSE! The underlying claim is FALSE (refuted by \(x = 0\)). To make it true, one must either strengthen the hypothesis to \(x \ne 0\) or weaken the conclusion to "\(x = 0\) or \(x = 3\)."

Audit Case 2:
Claim: "For all integers \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\), if \(a\) is even and \(b\) is even, then \(a + b\) is even."
Purported proof:
- Line 1: Let \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\) be arbitrary and assume \(a\) is even and \(b\) is even.
- Line 2: By definition of even, there exists \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(a = 2k\) and \(b = 2k\).
- Line 3: Then \(a + b = 2k + 2k = 4k = 2(2k)\), which is even.

Diagnosis:
- Broken step: Line 2 commits [REF: audit-checklist.4 | existential witness reuse], forcing \(a = b\) and proving the theorem only when both integers happen to be equal!
- Salvageable or false? The underlying theorem is TRUE and immediately salvageable: introduce TWO independent integer witnesses \(a = 2k\) and \(b = 2m\) (\(k, m \in \mathbb{Z}\)), giving \(a + b = 2(k + m)\), which is even because \(k + m \in \mathbb{Z}\). (Notice also that the flawed proof falsely seemed to prove \(4 \mid (a + b)\), which fails for \(a = 2, b = 4\)!)

Audit Case 3 (subtle quantifier-dependence trap):
Claim: "There exists a real number \(M \in \mathbb{R}\) such that for every real number \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), \(x < M\)."
Purported proof:
- Line 1: Let \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) be arbitrary.
- Line 2: Set \(M = x + 1 \in \mathbb{R}\).
- Line 3: Clearly \(x < x + 1 = M\), so such an \(M\) exists.

Diagnosis:
- Broken step: Lines 1–2 commit [REF: audit-checklist.5 | reversed quantifier dependence]: the claim has outer \(\exists M\) and inner \(\forall x\), so ONE fixed \(M\) must work simultaneously for ALL \(x\), yet Line 2 lets \(M\) depend on \(x\)!
- Salvageable or false? The claim is FALSE — its negation \(\forall M \in \mathbb{R},\, \exists x \in \mathbb{R},\, x \ge M\) is proved by taking \(x = M\) (or \(x = M + 1\)).
