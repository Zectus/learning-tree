=== SECTION 1: When direct proof stalls — the contrapositive template ===

Try to prove this simple-looking theorem directly:
\[
\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\, (n^2 \text{ is even} \implies n \text{ is even}).
\]
Follow the direct proof template: let \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) be arbitrary and assume \(n^2\) is even. By the definition of even, \(n^2 = 2k\) for some \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\). Now try to solve for \(n\): you get \(n = \pm\sqrt{2k}\). Immediately the algebra stalls! Taking a square root drags us out of \(\mathbb{Z}\) and tells us nothing obvious about whether \(n\) has the form \(2m\).

Yet recall a fundamental equivalence we proved in propositional logic: every conditional \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is logically equivalent to its contrapositive \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\). Therefore, for any predicates \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\) over a domain \(D\),
\[
\bigl(\forall x \in D,\, P(x) \implies Q(x)\bigr) \;\equiv\; \bigl(\forall x \in D,\, \neg Q(x) \implies \neg P(x)\bigr).
\]
Watch what happens to our stalled theorem when we flip to its contrapositive:
- Original: "If \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even."
- Contrapositive: "If \(n\) is NOT even (i.e., \(n\) is odd), then \(n^2\) is NOT even (i.e., \(n^2\) is odd)."

Now the algebra flows effortlessly in the forward direction! Instead of taking a square root, we simply SQUARE \(n = 2k + 1\).

[CALLOUT]
id: template-contraposition
label: Method
title: Proof by contraposition for \(\forall x \in D,\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\)
tone: key

(1) Fix an arbitrary \(x \in D\) and announce the contrapositive strategy: "We prove the contrapositive. Assume \(\neg Q(x)\)."
(2) Simplify \(\neg Q(x)\) cleanly (applying De Morgan's laws or quantifier negation if \(Q(x)\) is compound) and unpack its definitions.
(3) Deduce \(\neg P(x)\) by a direct chain of valid steps.
(4) Conclude that because \(\neg Q(x) \Rightarrow \neg P(x)\) holds for arbitrary \(x \in D\), the original implication \(P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\) holds for all \(x \in D\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us execute [REF: template-contraposition | proof by contraposition] on our opening theorem.

[CALLOUT]
id: proof-n2-even
label: Proof
title: Theorem: For every \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\), if \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even
tone: note

Proof: Let \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) be arbitrary. We prove the contrapositive: if \(n\) is not even, then \(n^2\) is not even.

Assume \(n\) is not even; then \(n\) is odd, so there exists \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(n = 2k + 1\). Squaring gives
\[
n^2 = (2k + 1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1.
\]
Since \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\), we have \(m = 2k^2 + 2k \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(n^2 = 2m + 1\). Hence \(n^2\) is odd, which means \(n^2\) is not even. By contraposition, if \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even.
[/CALLOUT]

Notice that [REF: proof-n2-even | this proof] relies on one basic parity fact about \(\mathbb{Z}\): every integer is either even or odd, and never both (so "not even" is equivalent to "odd"). With that in hand, contraposition turned an awkward square root into a two-line polynomial expansion.

=== SECTION 2: Structural clues that signal contraposition ===

Experienced mathematicians do not try direct proof blindly and switch to contraposition only after getting stuck; they spot structural signatures in the theorem statement immediately. Three signatures reliably point to contraposition:

1. Composite hypothesis, simpler conclusion. When \(P(x)\) puts \(x\) inside a complicated expression (like \(n^2\), \(n^3 + 5\), or \(ab\)) while \(Q(x)\) is a simple property of \(x\), assuming \(\neg Q(x)\) gives you a clean formula for \(x\) that you can plug directly into the composite expression!
2. Disjunctive conclusion (\(Q_1 \vee Q_2\)). Proving a disjunction directly can be awkward because you must decide which side holds. Taking the contrapositive applies De Morgan's law to \(\neg(Q_1 \vee Q_2) \equiv \neg Q_1 \wedge \neg Q_2\), turning a weak disjunction in the conclusion into a RICH CONJUNCTION of two simultaneous assumptions!
3. Negative conclusion ("irrational," "not divisible by," "\(x \ne 0\)"). Negative properties are hard to construct directly—there is no simple formula for "an arbitrary irrational number." Negating a negative conclusion yields a POSITIVE assumption ("rational," "divisible by," "\(x = 0\)") with a concrete algebraic representation you can compute with!

Let us work through two classic proofs showcasing signatures (2) and (3) simultaneously.

[CALLOUT]
id: proof-ab-even
label: Proof
title: Theorem: For all \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\), if \(ab\) is even, then \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even
tone: note

Proof: Let \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\) be arbitrary. We prove the contrapositive. The negation of "\(a\) is even or \(b\) is even" is (by De Morgan's law) "\(a\) is odd and \(b\) is odd."

Assume \(a\) is odd and \(b\) is odd. Then there exist integers \(k, m \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(a = 2k + 1\) and \(b = 2m + 1\). Multiplying gives
\[
ab = (2k + 1)(2m + 1) = 4km + 2k + 2m + 1 = 2(2km + k + m) + 1.
\]
Since \(2km + k + m \in \mathbb{Z}\), the product \(ab\) is odd, so \(ab\) is not even. By contraposition, if \(ab\) is even, then \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even.
[/CALLOUT]

Look how cleanly De Morgan's law worked in [REF: proof-ab-even | that proof]: assuming BOTH \(a\) and \(b\) are odd gave us explicit formulas for both variables at once!

Now watch signature (2) and signature (3) combine on real numbers. Recall that a real number is irrational if and only if it is NOT rational (\(x \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(x \notin \mathbb{Q}\)).

[CALLOUT]
id: proof-sum-irrational
label: Proof
title: Theorem: For all \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\), if \(x + y\) is irrational, then \(x\) is irrational or \(y\) is irrational
tone: note

Proof: Let \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\) be arbitrary. We prove the contrapositive: if it is NOT the case that "\(x\) is irrational or \(y\) is irrational," then \(x + y\) is rational.

By De Morgan's law and double negation, the negation of "\(x\) is irrational or \(y\) is irrational" is "\(x\) is rational and \(y\) is rational." Assume \(x \in \mathbb{Q}\) and \(y \in \mathbb{Q}\). As we proved in our earlier lesson on direct proof, the sum of two rational numbers is rational, so \(x + y \in \mathbb{Q}\); that is, \(x + y\) is not irrational. By contraposition, if \(x + y\) is irrational, then \(x\) is irrational or \(y\) is irrational.
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: proof-sum-irrational | that proof] did not even need to unpack fractions from scratch—reusing our previously proved theorem (closure of \(\mathbb{Q}\) under addition) turned a scary statement about irrational numbers into a three-line contrapositive proof!

=== SECTION 3: Pitfalls — converse errors, ambient hypotheses, and quantified components ===

Because contraposition reverses the direction of the arrow AND negates both sides, three subtle errors show up repeatedly when students apply it.

[CALLOUT]
id: caution-contra-pitfalls
label: Caution
title: Three pitfalls in contrapositive proofs
tone: caution

(1) The converse error: Proving \(Q(x) \Rightarrow P(x)\) instead of \(\neg Q(x) \Rightarrow \neg P(x)\). For instance, proving "if \(n\) is even, then \(n^2\) is even" does NOTHING to prove "if \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even."
(2) Negating a conjunction hypothesis carelessly: If the theorem has the shape \((P_1 \wedge P_2) \Rightarrow Q\), its full contrapositive is \(\neg Q \Rightarrow (\neg P_1 \vee \neg P_2)\). Often, however, one part (say \(P_1\), such as "\(x > 0\)" or "\(a \in \mathbb{Z}\)") is an ambient standing assumption! Remember the propositional equivalence
\[
(P_1 \wedge P_2) \implies Q \;\equiv\; P_1 \implies (P_2 \implies Q) \;\equiv\; P_1 \implies (\neg Q \implies \neg P_2).
\]
Thus you are allowed to KEEP \(P_1\) fixed as a standing assumption, assume \(\neg Q\), and prove \(\neg P_2\)!
(3) Mis-negating a quantified hypothesis or conclusion: When \(P\) or \(Q\) itself contains \(\forall\) or \(\exists\), apply the quantifier negation rules strictly before writing the contrapositive.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us examine both Pitfall (2) and Pitfall (3) on concrete theorems so you can see how cleanly they work when handled properly.

First, look at Pitfall (2) on an ambient hypothesis:
Theorem: "For all real numbers \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\), if \(x > 0\) and \(xy\) is irrational, then \(x\) is irrational or \(y\) is irrational."
Wait—here \(x > 0\) is not even needed, so consider a theorem where the ambient hypothesis IS needed:
Theorem: "For all real numbers \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\), if \(x \ne 0\) and \(xy\) is irrational, then \(x\) is irrational or \(y\) is irrational." (Wait—even here if \(x = 0\), \(xy = 0\) is rational, so the implication holds vacuously. Look at a better theorem where \(x \ne 0\) genuinely enters!)
Consider:
Theorem: "For all \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\), if \(x\) is nonzero and rational and \(xy\) is irrational, then \(y\) is irrational."
Here we have TWO hypotheses: \(P_1\): "\(x \in \mathbb{Q}\) and \(x \ne 0\)" (ambient assumption about \(x\)), and \(P_2\): "\(xy\) is irrational," with conclusion \(Q\): "\(y\) is irrational."
Keeping \(P_1\) fixed and taking the contrapositive of \(P_2 \Rightarrow Q\):
- Fix arbitrary \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(x \in \mathbb{Q}\) and \(x \ne 0\).
- Assume \(\neg Q\): \(y \in \mathbb{Q}\).
- Since \(x \in \mathbb{Q}\) and \(y \in \mathbb{Q}\), their product \(xy\) is rational (if \(x = p_1/q_1\) and \(y = p_2/q_2\) with \(q_1, q_2 \ne 0\), then \(xy = (p_1 p_2)/(q_1 q_2) \in \mathbb{Q}\)). Thus \(\neg P_2\) holds: \(xy\) is not irrational.
- By contraposition (with \(P_1\) fixed), if \(x \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}\) and \(xy\) is irrational, then \(y\) is irrational.
(Notice that the full contrapositive \(\neg Q \Rightarrow (\neg P_1 \vee \neg P_2)\) is logically equivalent to \((P_1 \wedge \neg Q) \Rightarrow \neg P_2\), because \(A \Rightarrow (B \vee C) \equiv (A \wedge \neg B) \Rightarrow C\).)

Now look at Pitfall (3) on a quantified hypothesis—a cornerstone lemma in real analysis!
Theorem: "Let \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) be fixed. If \(|x| < \varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), then \(x = 0\)."
Write the logical structure explicitly:
\[
\bigl(\forall \varepsilon > 0,\, |x| < \varepsilon\bigr) \implies x = 0.
\]
Trying a direct proof is awkward: how do you turn infinitely many inequalities into \(x = 0\)? Now take the contrapositive!
- \(\neg Q\): \(x \ne 0\).
- \(\neg P\): \(\neg(\forall \varepsilon > 0,\, |x| < \varepsilon) \equiv \exists \varepsilon > 0,\, |x| \ge \varepsilon\).

Look how effortless the contrapositive proof becomes:
Assume \(x \ne 0\). Then \(|x| > 0\), so we may choose \(\varepsilon = \frac{|x|}{2} > 0\) (or even \(\varepsilon = |x| > 0\)). For this specific \(\varepsilon > 0\), we have \(|x| \ge \varepsilon\) (indeed \(|x| > |x|/2\)), which shows \(\exists \varepsilon > 0,\, |x| \ge \varepsilon\). By contraposition, if \(|x| < \varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), then \(x = 0\).

"Assume \(n\) is odd. Then \(n = 2k + 1\) for some \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\). Substituting gives \(n^2 - 6n + 5 = (2k + 1)^2 - 6(2k + 1) + 5 = 4k^2 - 8k = 2(2k^2 - 4k)\), which is even. Done."

(B) Valid except for a small algebra error in expanding \((2k + 1)^2 - 6(2k + 1) + 5\).

(D) Invalid because it proved the inverse instead of the contrapositive.
