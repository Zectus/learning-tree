=== SECTION 1: Predicates, domains of discourse, and single quantifiers ===

Propositional logic treats whole sentences like \(P\) and \(Q\) as indivisible blocks. Yet most mathematical statements talk about properties of objects ranging over a number system: "every integer squared is nonnegative," or "there is a real number whose cube is \(5\)." In propositional logic, an open sentence such as "\(x^2 > 4\)" is not a statement at all because its truth depends on \(x\). To turn open sentences into full mathematical statements, we need predicates, domains of discourse, and quantifiers.

[CALLOUT]
id: def-predicate-domain
label: Definition
title: Predicate and domain of discourse
tone: key

A predicate (or open sentence) \(P(x)\) is a statement template containing a variable \(x\) that becomes a definite mathematical statement (true or false) once \(x\) is replaced by a specific object from a specified collection \(D\), called the domain of discourse (or universe of discourse).
[/CALLOUT]

Throughout this lesson we use the familiar number systems informally as domains: the natural numbers \(\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\), the integers \(\mathbb{Z}\), the rational numbers \(\mathbb{Q}\), and the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\). We write \(x \in D\) to mean "\(x\) belongs to the domain \(D\)."

For example, let \(P(x)\) be the predicate "\(x^2 = 2\)." On its own, \(P(x)\) has no truth value. Plugging in \(x = 3\) gives the false statement \(P(3): 3^2 = 2\); plugging in \(x = \sqrt{2}\) gives the true statement \(P(\sqrt{2}): (\sqrt{2})^2 = 2\).

Instead of substituting one number at a time, mathematics uses two quantifiers to make claims about an entire domain at once.

[CALLOUT]
id: def-quantifiers
label: Definition
title: Universal and existential quantifiers
tone: key

Let \(P(x)\) be a predicate with domain of discourse \(D\).

(1) The universal quantification \(\forall x \in D,\, P(x)\) (read "for all \(x\) in \(D\), \(P(x)\)" or "for every \(x\) in \(D\), \(P(x)\)") is true if \(P(x)\) is true for EVERY element \(x \in D\), and false if there is at least one element \(x \in D\) for which \(P(x)\) is false.
(2) The existential quantification \(\exists x \in D,\, P(x)\) (read "there exists \(x\) in \(D\) such that \(P(x)\)" or "for some \(x\) in \(D\), \(P(x)\)") is true if \(P(x)\) is true for AT LEAST ONE element \(x \in D\), and false if \(P(x)\) is false for every element \(x \in D\).
[/CALLOUT]

Once a variable \(x\) is introduced by \(\forall x \in D\) or \(\exists x \in D\), it is bound (no longer free): the quantified expression \(\forall x \in D,\, P(x)\) is now a complete proposition with a fixed truth value \(T\) or \(F\). Notice also that the letter \(x\) is a dummy variable: \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, x^2 \ge 0\) and \(\forall t \in \mathbb{R},\, t^2 \ge 0\) state the exact same mathematical fact.

Crucially, the truth value of a quantified statement can flip completely when you change the domain \(D\)! Never judge a quantified statement without checking its domain first.

[TABLE]
Quantified statement | Domain \(D = \mathbb{N}\) | Domain \(D = \mathbb{Z}\) | Domain \(D = \mathbb{Q}\) | Domain \(D = \mathbb{R}\)
\(\forall x \in D,\, x^2 \ge x\) | \(T\) | \(T\) | \(F\) (e.g., \(x = 1/2\)) | \(F\) (e.g., \(x = 1/2\))
\(\exists x \in D,\, 2x = 1\) | \(F\) | \(F\) | \(T\) (\(x = 1/2\)) | \(T\) (\(x = 1/2\))
\(\exists x \in D,\, x^2 = 2\) | \(F\) | \(F\) | \(F\) | \(T\) (\(x = \sqrt{2}\))
\(\forall x \in D,\, x > 0\) | \(T\) | \(F\) (e.g., \(x = 0\)) | \(F\) (e.g., \(x = 0\)) | \(F\) (e.g., \(x = 0\))
[/TABLE]

Look at the first row of this table: \(\forall x \in \mathbb{Z},\, x^2 \ge x\) is true (if \(x \le 0\), then \(x^2 \ge 0 \ge x\); if \(x \ge 1\), multiplying \(x \ge 1\) by \(x > 0\) gives \(x^2 \ge x\)). Yet enlarging the domain from \(\mathbb{Z}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\) introduces numbers strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), such as \(x = 1/2\), where \((1/2)^2 = 1/4 < 1/2\), flipping the universal statement from \(T\) to \(F\). Conversely, enlarging a domain can flip an existential statement from \(F\) to \(T\), because adding elements can supply a missing witness (like \(x = 1/2\) for \(2x = 1\) when moving from \(\mathbb{Z}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\)).

=== SECTION 2: Restricted quantification and the pairing convention ===

In practice, we rarely assert a property for every single number in \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{Z}\); we restrict attention to those elements satisfying a condition \(P(x)\). For instance:
- "Every prime number greater than \(2\) is odd."
- "Some even integer is a perfect square."

How do we symbolize these two sentences over a domain \(D\) (say \(D = \mathbb{Z}\)) using predicates \(P(x)\) ("\(x\) is a prime greater than \(2\)" or "\(x\) is even") and \(Q(x)\) ("\(x\) is odd" or "\(x\) is a perfect square")? Here lies one of the most important structural rules in logic: universal quantifiers pair with \(\Rightarrow\), whereas existential quantifiers pair with \(\wedge\).

[CALLOUT]
id: rule-pairing
label: Principle
title: The quantifier-connective pairing convention
tone: key

Let \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\) be predicates over a domain \(D\).

(1) "Every \(x \in D\) satisfying \(P(x)\) also satisfies \(Q(x)\)" is symbolized as
\[
\forall x \in D,\, \bigl(P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\bigr).
\]
(2) "There exists an \(x \in D\) satisfying \(P(x)\) that also satisfies \(Q(x)\)" is symbolized as
\[
\exists x \in D,\, \bigl(P(x) \wedge Q(x)\bigr).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Why is it an error to swap \(\Rightarrow\) and \(\wedge\) in [REF: rule-pairing | this pairing convention]? Let us examine both mistakes concretely on \(D = \mathbb{Z}\).

Mistake 1: Writing \(\forall x \in \mathbb{Z},\, (P(x) \wedge Q(x))\) for "every even integer is divisible by \(2\)" (where \(P(x)\) is "\(x\) is even" and \(Q(x)\) is "\(x\) is divisible by \(2\)").
What does \(\forall x \in \mathbb{Z},\, (P(x) \wedge Q(x))\) actually say? It says that EVERY integer \(x\) is BOTH even AND divisible by \(2\)—which is blatantly false at \(x = 3\)! Using \(\wedge\) under \(\forall\) forces the filter condition \(P(x)\) onto every element of the entire domain. By contrast, \(\forall x \in \mathbb{Z},\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\) tests only the elements where \(P(x)\) holds: whenever \(x\) is odd, \(P(x)\) is false, so \(P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\) is vacuously true and does not disrupt the claim.

Mistake 2: Writing \(\exists x \in \mathbb{Z},\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\) for "some prime integer is greater than \(100\) and divisible by \(4\)" (where \(P(x)\) is "\(x\) is prime" and \(Q(x)\) is "\(x > 100\) and \(x\) is divisible by \(4\)").
Clearly no prime integer is divisible by \(4\), so the intended English claim is FALSE. Yet what is the truth value of \(\exists x \in \mathbb{Z},\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\)? Pick \(x = 6\). Since \(6\) is NOT prime, \(P(6)\) is false, which makes the material conditional \(P(6) \Rightarrow Q(6)\) VACUOUSLY TRUE! Therefore \(\exists x \in \mathbb{Z},\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\) evaluates to TRUE—simply because there is at least one non-prime integer in \(\mathbb{Z}\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: caution-vacuous-universal
label: Caution
title: Vacuous truth of restricted universal statements
tone: caution

What if NO element of \(D\) satisfies the condition \(P(x)\)? Consider \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x^2 < 0 \Rightarrow x = 17)\). Because \(x^2 < 0\) is false for every real number \(x\), the implication \(x^2 < 0 \Rightarrow x = 17\) is vacuously true for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), so the universal statement is TRUE. An existential counterpart \(\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x^2 < 0 \wedge x = 17)\), however, is FALSE because no \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) satisfies both conjuncts.
[/CALLOUT]

Shorthand notation is also common in mathematical writing: you will often see \(\forall x > 0,\, Q(x)\) (with the domain \(\mathbb{R}\) understood from context) as a compact way of writing \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x > 0 \Rightarrow Q(x))\), and \(\exists x > 0,\, Q(x)\) as shorthand for \(\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x > 0 \wedge Q(x))\). Keeping the underlying \(\Rightarrow\) versus \(\wedge\) structure in mind makes translating and negating such shorthands effortless.

=== SECTION 3: Negating single-quantified statements ===

Suppose someone claims "every prime number is odd," symbolized as \(\forall x \in \mathbb{N},\, (P(x) \Rightarrow O(x))\). What must be true for that claim to be FALSE? You do NOT need every prime number to be even! It suffices for at least one prime number to fail to be odd—namely \(x = 2\).

Now suppose someone claims "there exists an integer \(x\) such that \(x^2 = 3\)," symbolized as \(\exists x \in \mathbb{Z},\, (x^2 = 3)\). What must be true for that claim to be FALSE? It is not enough for one integer to fail \(x^2 = 3\); EVERY integer \(x \in \mathbb{Z}\) must satisfy \(x^2 \ne 3\).

This symmetry produces the fundamental negation rules for single quantifiers, which behave like generalized De Morgan's laws: \(\forall\) acts like a giant conjunction across the domain, and \(\exists\) acts like a giant disjunction across the domain.

[CALLOUT]
id: laws-quantifier-negation
label: Theorem
title: Negation of single-quantified statements
tone: key

Let \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\) be predicates over a domain \(D\).

(1) Negating a universal statement:
\[
\neg\bigl(\forall x \in D,\, P(x)\bigr) \equiv \exists x \in D,\, \neg P(x).
\]
(2) Negating an existential statement:
\[
\neg\bigl(\exists x \in D,\, P(x)\bigr) \equiv \forall x \in D,\, \neg P(x).
\]
(3) Negating a universal conditional (combining (1) with \(\neg(A \Rightarrow B) \equiv A \wedge \neg B\)):
\[
\neg\bigl(\forall x \in D,\, P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\bigr) \equiv \exists x \in D,\, \bigl(P(x) \wedge \neg Q(x)\bigr).
\]
(4) Negating a restricted existential (combining (2) with De Morgan's law):
\[
\neg\bigl(\exists x \in D,\, P(x) \wedge Q(x)\bigr) \equiv \forall x \in D,\, \bigl(\neg P(x) \vee \neg Q(x)\bigr) \equiv \forall x \in D,\, \bigl(P(x) \Rightarrow \neg Q(x)\bigr).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: laws-quantifier-negation.3 | the negation of a universal conditional] preserves [REF: rule-pairing | the pairing convention] automatically! Starting from \(\forall x \in D,\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\), pushing \(\neg\) across \(\forall\) flips it to \(\exists\), and pushing \(\neg\) across \(\Rightarrow\) flips it to \(\wedge\) with \(\neg Q(x)\):
\[
\neg\bigl(\forall x \in D,\, P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\bigr) \equiv \exists x \in D,\, \neg\bigl(P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)\bigr) \equiv \exists x \in D,\, \bigl(P(x) \wedge \neg Q(x)\bigr).
\]
Notice also what does NOT change during negation: the domain \(D\) stays fixed! When you negate \(\forall x \in \mathbb{N},\, x > 5\), you get \(\exists x \in \mathbb{N},\, x \le 5\)—never \(\exists x \notin \mathbb{N},\, x \le 5\).

Let us practice writing clean, positive negations (pushing \(\neg\) all the way inside so no "it is not the case that" remains) on three mathematical claims.

Example 1. Negate: "For every real number \(x\), if \(x > 1\), then \(x^2 > x\)."
In symbols: \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x > 1 \Rightarrow x^2 > x)\).
By [REF: laws-quantifier-negation.3 | universal conditional negation], the negation is:
\[
\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x > 1 \wedge x^2 \le x).
\]
In clean English: "There exists a real number \(x\) such that \(x > 1\) and \(x^2 \le x\)." (Since the original statement is true, this negation is false.)

Example 2. Negate: "For every integer \(n\), if \(n^2\) is even or \(n > 10\), then \(n\) is divisible by \(4\)."
In symbols: \(\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\, \bigl((n^2 \text{ is even} \vee n > 10) \Rightarrow 4 \text{ divides } n\bigr)\).
Push \(\neg\) inward step by step:
\[
\exists n \in \mathbb{Z},\, \bigl((n^2 \text{ is even} \vee n > 10) \wedge 4 \text{ does not divide } n\bigr).
\]
Notice that the hypothesis \((n^2 \text{ is even} \vee n > 10)\) is NOT negated! When negating \(A \Rightarrow B\), the hypothesis \(A\) stays intact and only the conclusion \(B\) is negated.

Example 3. Negate: "There exists a real number \(x\) such that \(x > 0\) and \(x^2 + 1 = 0\)."
In symbols: \(\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x > 0 \wedge x^2 + 1 = 0)\).
By [REF: laws-quantifier-negation.4 | restricted existential negation], we get two equivalent clean forms:
\[
\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x \le 0 \vee x^2 + 1 \ne 0) \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad \forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x > 0 \Rightarrow x^2 + 1 \ne 0).
\]
In idiomatic English: "For every positive real number \(x\), \(x^2 + 1 \ne 0\)."
