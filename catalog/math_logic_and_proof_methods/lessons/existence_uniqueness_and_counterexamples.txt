=== SECTION 1: Constructive existence proofs and quantifier dependence ===

Our direct and contrapositive proofs so far focused on universal conditionals \(\forall x \in D,\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\). Now we turn to three equally essential tasks in mathematics: proving that an object EXISTS (\(\exists\)), proving that it is UNIQUE (\(\exists!\)), and DISPROVING a false conjecture cleanly.

The most straightforward way to prove an existential statement \(\exists x \in D,\, P(x)\) is a constructive existence proof: explicitly exhibit one specific object \(x_0\) (called a witness), and verify two things—first that \(x_0 \in D\), and second that \(P(x_0)\) is true.

[CALLOUT]
id: template-existence
label: Method
title: Constructive existence proof
tone: key

(1) For a bare existential \(\exists x \in D,\, P(x)\):
- Exhibit a concrete candidate \(x_0\) (found via scratchwork).
- Verify domain membership: \(x_0 \in D\).
- Verify the predicate: \(P(x_0)\) holds.

(2) For a nested \(\forall x \in D_1\,\exists y \in D_2,\, P(x, y)\):
- Fix an arbitrary \(x \in D_1\).
- Construct \(y\) explicitly as a formula depending ONLY on \(x\) (and constants).
- Verify \(y \in D_2\) and \(P(x, y)\) for that constructed \(y\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: template-existence.2 | part (2)] honors quantifier dependence from our nested-quantifiers lesson! In \(\forall x \in D_1\,\exists y \in D_2,\, P(x, y)\), the existential variable \(y\) sits to the right of \(\forall x\), so \(y\) is allowed to depend on \(x\). By contrast, to prove \(\exists y \in D_2\,\forall x \in D_1,\, P(x, y)\), you must exhibit ONE constant \(y_0 \in D_2\) independent of \(x\), and then prove \(P(x, y_0)\) for arbitrary \(x \in D_1\). Never let an outer existential depend on an inner universal!

Let us look at two clean constructive existence proofs—one bare existential and one nested \(\forall\exists\) theorem.

Example 1 (Bare existential). Prove: \(\exists n \in \mathbb{N},\, (n^2 + n + 41 \text{ is composite})\).
(Recall that an integer \(m > 1\) is composite if \(m = ab\) for some integers \(a, b > 1\).)
Note that plugging in \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, 39\) produces prime numbers every time (\(43, 47, 53, \dots\))! Yet we only need ONE witness. Choose \(n = 41\).
- Domain check: \(41 \in \mathbb{N}\).
- Predicate check: \(41^2 + 41 + 41 = 41(41 + 1 + 1) = 41 \cdot 43\). Since \(41 > 1\) and \(43 > 1\) are integers, \(41^2 + 41 + 41\) is composite. (Even \(n = 40\) works: \(40^2 + 40 + 41 = 40(41) + 41 = 41^2\).)

Example 2 (Nested \(\forall\exists\): density of \(\mathbb{Q}\) between rationals). Prove:
\[
\forall x \in \mathbb{Q}\,\forall y \in \mathbb{Q},\, \bigl(x < y \implies \exists z \in \mathbb{Q},\, (x < z \wedge z < y)\bigr).
\]
Proof: Let \(x, y \in \mathbb{Q}\) be arbitrary with \(x < y\). Define the witness
\[
z = \frac{x + y}{2}.
\]
- Domain check (\(z \in \mathbb{Q}\)): Since \(x, y \in \mathbb{Q}\), write \(x = p_1/q_1\) and \(y = p_2/q_2\) with \(p_1, q_1, p_2, q_2 \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(q_1, q_2 \ne 0\). Then
\[
z = \frac{p_1 q_2 + p_2 q_1}{2 q_1 q_2},
\]
whose numerator and denominator are integers with \(2 q_1 q_2 \ne 0\); hence \(z \in \mathbb{Q}\).
- Predicate check (\(x < z < y\)): Since \(x < y\), adding \(x\) to both sides gives \(2x < x + y\), so \(x < \frac{x + y}{2} = z\); adding \(y\) to both sides gives \(x + y < 2y\), so \(z = \frac{x + y}{2} < y\).

Both checks are explicit and airtight—and notice how \(z = (x + y)/2\) depends only on the universal variables \(x\) and \(y\) standing to the left of \(\exists z\).

[QUESTION 1]
[ANSWER: C]
A student wants to prove the claim \(\exists y \in \mathbb{R}\,\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, (x + y > 0)\) and writes:
"Let \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) be arbitrary. Choose \(y = 1 - x \in \mathbb{R}\). Then \(x + y = x + (1 - x) = 1 > 0\), which proves the claim."
What is wrong with this proof?

(A) The student forgot to verify that \(1 - x \in \mathbb{R}\).
(B) The arithmetic \(x + (1 - x) = 1\) is incorrect when \(x < 0\).
(C) The student violated quantifier dependence: in \(\exists y\,\forall x\), the witness \(y\) must be chosen BEFORE \(x\) and cannot depend on \(x\) (the student actually proved \(\forall x\,\exists y,\, x + y > 0\), whereas \(\exists y\,\forall x,\, x + y > 0\) is false).
(D) The student used a constructive witness instead of a proof by cases.

=== SECTION 2: Uniqueness proofs and \(\exists!\) theorems ===

Recall from our nested-quantifiers lesson that \(\exists! x \in D,\, P(x)\) is the conjunction of two independent assertions:
1. Existence: \(\exists x \in D,\, P(x)\).
2. Uniqueness: \(\forall x_1 \in D\,\forall x_2 \in D,\, \bigl((P(x_1) \wedge P(x_2)) \Rightarrow x_1 = x_2\bigr)\).

Therefore, proving \(\exists! x \in D,\, P(x)\) ALWAYS has two distinct halves! Never confuse the two: proving uniqueness alone only shows there is AT MOST ONE solution (it does not rule out zero solutions).

[CALLOUT]
id: template-uniqueness
label: Method
title: Two templates for proving uniqueness
tone: key

To prove uniqueness for \(P(x)\) over \(D\) (as the second half of an \(\exists!\) proof):

(1) Two-element template: Let \(x_1, x_2 \in D\) be arbitrary, assume both \(P(x_1)\) and \(P(x_2)\) hold, and deduce \(x_1 = x_2\).
(2) Forced-formula template: Let \(x \in D\) be arbitrary, assume \(P(x)\) holds, and deduce that \(x\) must equal one specific value \(x_0\) (then any two solutions both equal \(x_0\), hence equal each other).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us see both templates in action on two classic \(\exists!\) theorems.

[CALLOUT]
id: proof-linear-unique
label: Proof
title: Theorem: Unique solution of a linear equation
tone: note

Statement: For all \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(a \ne 0\), \(\exists! x \in \mathbb{R},\, ax + b = 0\).

Proof: Fix arbitrary \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(a \ne 0\).

Existence: Set \(x_0 = -b/a\). Because \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(a \ne 0\), we have \(x_0 \in \mathbb{R}\), and
\[
a x_0 + b = a\left(-\frac{b}{a}\right) + b = -b + b = 0.
\]

Uniqueness (forced-formula template): Suppose \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) satisfies \(ax + b = 0\). Subtracting \(b\) gives \(ax = -b\), and dividing by \(a \ne 0\) forces \(x = -b/a = x_0\). Thus \(x_0 = -b/a\) is the unique real solution.
[/CALLOUT]

Now try a nonlinear equation where you CANNOT solve for \(x\) by simple inversion—here the two-element template shines!

[CALLOUT]
id: proof-cubic-unique
label: Proof
title: Theorem: There is a unique real number \(r\) such that \(r^3 + r = 2\)
tone: note

Proof:

Existence: Take \(r_0 = 1 \in \mathbb{R}\). Then \(1^3 + 1 = 2\), so at least one real solution exists.

Uniqueness (two-element template): Suppose \(r_1, r_2 \in \mathbb{R}\) both satisfy the equation:
\[
r_1^3 + r_1 = 2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad r_2^3 + r_2 = 2.
\]
Subtracting the second equation from the first yields
\[
(r_1^3 - r_2^3) + (r_1 - r_2) = 0.
\]
Factoring \(r_1^3 - r_2^3 = (r_1 - r_2)(r_1^2 + r_1 r_2 + r_2^2)\) gives
\[
(r_1 - r_2)(r_1^2 + r_1 r_2 + r_2^2 + 1) = 0.
\]
Complete the square on the second factor:
\[
r_1^2 + r_1 r_2 + r_2^2 + 1 = \left(r_1 + \frac{r_2}{2}\right)^2 + \frac{3}{4}r_2^2 + 1 \ge 1 > 0.
\]
Because the second factor is strictly positive (never \(0\)), dividing by it forces \(r_1 - r_2 = 0\), i.e., \(r_1 = r_2\).

Combining existence and uniqueness establishes \(\exists! r \in \mathbb{R},\, r^3 + r = 2\).
[/CALLOUT]

Pause to appreciate why [REF: proof-cubic-unique | that proof] is so instructive: even without a formula for solving general cubics, the two-element template let us subtract the two equations, factor out \((r_1 - r_2)\), and verify that the remaining factor never vanishes! Notice also why checking existence was NOT optional: if the equation had been \(r^3 + r = 2\) over \(\mathbb{Z}\), \(r_0 = 1 \in \mathbb{Z}\) still works; whereas for \(r^3 + r = 3\) over \(\mathbb{Z}\), the exact same uniqueness proof STILL goes through (showing AT MOST ONE integer solution), yet ZERO integer solutions exist (\(1^3 + 1 = 2 < 3 < 10 = 2^3 + 2\)), so \(\exists! r \in \mathbb{Z},\, r^3 + r = 3\) is FALSE!

[QUESTION 2]
[ANSWER: B]
Consider the claim: "There exists a unique integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(3n = 7\)." A student writes: "Suppose \(n_1, n_2 \in \mathbb{Z}\) both satisfy \(3n_1 = 7\) and \(3n_2 = 7\). Then \(3n_1 = 3n_2\), so dividing by \(3\) gives \(n_1 = n_2\). Therefore \(\exists! n \in \mathbb{Z},\, 3n = 7\)." What is wrong with this argument?

(A) Dividing by \(3\) is invalid in \(\mathbb{Z}\).
(B) The student proved only uniqueness (at most one solution) and omitted the existence half—which is actually false over \(\mathbb{Z}\) since \(7/3 \notin \mathbb{Z}\).
(C) The student should have assumed \(n_1 \ne n_2\) instead of \(3n_1 = 7\) and \(3n_2 = 7\).
(D) Uniqueness fails because \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) could have opposite signs.

[/QUESTION]

=== SECTION 3: Rigorous disproof — counterexamples versus universal proofs ===

Disproving a false statement \(S\) is NOT a separate kind of logic: disproving \(S\) means PROVING ITS NEGATION \(\neg S\)! The exact structure of \(\neg S\) tells you immediately whether a single concrete counterexample suffices or whether a general proof is required.

[TABLE]
False claim \(S\) | Its negation \(\neg S\) (what you must prove) | What a disproof requires
\(\forall x \in D,\, P(x)\) | \(\exists x \in D,\, \neg P(x)\) | One verified counterexample \(x_0 \in D\) with \(\neg P(x_0)\)
\(\forall x \in D,\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\) | \(\exists x \in D,\, (P(x) \wedge \neg Q(x))\) | One verified counterexample \(x_0 \in D\) satisfying BOTH \(P(x_0)\) AND \(\neg Q(x_0)\)
\(\exists x \in D,\, P(x)\) | \(\forall x \in D,\, \neg P(x)\) | A general proof covering EVERY \(x \in D\) (examples never suffice!)
\(\exists y \in D_2\,\forall x \in D_1,\, P(x, y)\) | \(\forall y \in D_2\,\exists x \in D_1,\, \neg P(x, y)\) | For an arbitrary \(y \in D_2\), construct \(x \in D_1\) (in terms of \(y\)) with \(\neg P(x, y)\)
[/TABLE]

Look carefully at Row 2 of this table! To refute a universal conditional \(\forall x \in D,\, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\), your counterexample \(x_0 \in D\) MUST satisfy the hypothesis \(P(x_0)\) AND violate the conclusion \(Q(x_0)\).

Test this rule on the false conjecture: "For every integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\), if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^2 - 1\) is divisible by \(16\)."
- Does \(n = 2\) refute this claim? NO! Because \(2\) is NOT odd, \(P(2)\) is false, so \(P(2) \Rightarrow Q(2)\) is vacuously true! An element that fails the hypothesis is NEVER a counterexample.
- Does \(n = 3\) refute this claim? Check all three requirements:
  1. \(3 \in \mathbb{Z}\).
  2. Hypothesis holds: \(3\) is odd.
  3. Conclusion fails: \(3^2 - 1 = 8\), and \(16 \nmid 8\) (since \(8 = 16k\) has no integer solution \(k\)).
  Yes! \(n = 3\) is a complete, airtight disproof.

Now look at Row 3: disproving an existential claim. Suppose someone conjectures: "There exists an integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(4n + 3\) is a perfect square." Testing \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\) gives \(3, 7, 11, 15, 19\)—none are perfect squares—yet checking five numbers (or five million!) does NOT disprove an \(\exists\) claim. Instead, we must prove its negation:
\[
\forall n \in \mathbb{Z}\,\forall k \in \mathbb{Z},\, k^2 \ne 4n + 3.
\]
Proof: Let \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\) be arbitrary. By our modulo-\(4\) square theorem from the previous lesson (splitting on \(k\) even or odd), \(k^2\) has the form \(4m\) or \(4m + 1\) for some \(m \in \mathbb{Z}\). By the uniqueness of remainders in the division algorithm on \(\mathbb{Z}\), \(k^2\) leaves remainder \(0\) or \(1\) upon division by \(4\), never remainder \(3\). Hence \(k^2 \ne 4n + 3\) for all \(n, k \in \mathbb{Z}\), disproving the existential conjecture!

Finally, look at Row 4: disproving \(\exists y \in \mathbb{R}\,\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, x \cdot y = x + y\).
Its negation is \(\forall y \in \mathbb{R}\,\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\, x \cdot y \ne x + y\).
Proof: Let \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) be arbitrary. Choose \(x = 1 \in \mathbb{R}\). Then \(x \cdot y = y\), whereas \(x + y = 1 + y \ne y\) (since \(0 \ne 1\)). Thus for every \(y \in \mathbb{R}\), there exists \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) (namely \(x = 1\)) with \(x \cdot y \ne x + y\), refuting the claim!

[CALLOUT]
id: caution-examples-asymmetry
label: Caution
title: The sharp asymmetry of examples
tone: caution

- One verified counterexample DISPROVES a universal statement (\(\forall\)), and one verified witness PROVES an existential statement (\(\exists\)).
- Conversely, NO finite number of examples can EVER prove a universal statement (\(\forall\)) on an infinite domain, and NO finite number of failed trials can EVER disprove an existential statement (\(\exists\)) on an infinite domain.
[/CALLOUT]
