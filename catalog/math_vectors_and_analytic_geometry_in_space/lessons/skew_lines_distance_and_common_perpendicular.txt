=== SECTION 1: Embedding skew lines in parallel planes ===

Two skew lines \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) are neither parallel nor intersecting—they pass by each other at different depths, like two non-parallel airplane flight corridors at different altitudes. How can we find the exact minimum clearance \(d(L_1, L_2)\) between them?

Write the two lines in vector form:
\[
L_1:\; \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{p}_1 + t\mathbf{d}_1, \qquad L_2:\; \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \mathbf{p}_2 + s\mathbf{d}_2,
\]
where \(P_1\) (position \(\mathbf{p}_1\)) lies on \(L_1\), \(P_2\) (position \(\mathbf{p}_2\)) lies on \(L_2\), and the direction vectors are non-parallel, so their cross product
\[
\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 \ne \mathbf{0}.
\]
Here is the decisive geometric insight: because \(\mathbf{n}\) is perpendicular to BOTH \(\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(\mathbf{d}_2\), there exists a unique plane \(\Pi_1\) passing through \(P_1\) with normal \(\mathbf{n}\) (which contains all of \(L_1\)), and a unique parallel plane \(\Pi_2\) passing through \(P_2\) with the EXACT same normal \(\mathbf{n}\) (which contains all of \(L_2\)). Every point of \(L_1\) sits inside \(\Pi_1\), and every point of \(L_2\) sits inside \(\Pi_2\), so the shortest distance between \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) CANNOT be smaller than the perpendicular separation \(d(\Pi_1, \Pi_2)\) between the two parallel planes—and as we will confirm in Section 2, there is a unique pair of points \(Q_1 \in L_1\) and \(Q_2 \in L_2\) whose connecting segment runs strictly along \(\mathbf{n}\), achieving that exact minimum!

Therefore, \(d(L_1, L_2)\) equals the distance from \(P_2 \in \Pi_2\) to \(\Pi_1\), which is the magnitude of the scalar projection of the bridge vector \(\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = \mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_1\) onto the common normal \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2\):
\[
d(L_1, L_2) = \left|\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{n}} \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}\right| = \frac{\left|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot (\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2)\right|}{\|\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2\|}.
\]
Notice the second geometric interpretation hiding in this fraction: the numerator \(\left|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot (\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2)\right|\) is the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by \(\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}\), \(\mathbf{d}_1\), and \(\mathbf{d}_2\), while the denominator \(\|\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2\|\) is the area of its base spanned by \(\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(\mathbf{d}_2\). Dividing volume by base area recovers the perpendicular height between the two parallel planes!

[CALLOUT]
id: skew-dist
label: Theorem
title: Shortest distance between two non-parallel lines in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
tone: key

Let \(L_1: \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{p}_1 + t\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \mathbf{p}_2 + s\mathbf{d}_2\) be two lines with \(\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 \ne \mathbf{0}\).

(1) Shortest distance formula:
\[
d(L_1, L_2) = \frac{\left|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot (\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2)\right|}{\|\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2\|}.
\]
(2) Coplanarity and intersection check: \(d(L_1, L_2) = 0\) if and only if \(\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot (\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2) = 0\), which occurs precisely when \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) lie in a common plane and intersect at a point.
[/CALLOUT]

[CALLOUT]
id: skew-parallel-warn
label: Common pitfall
title: Never apply the skew-line formula to parallel lines
tone: caution

If \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) are parallel, then \(\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 = \mathbf{0}\), so [REF: skew-dist.1 | the skew-line distance formula] collapses to \(\frac{0}{0}\)—even when the lines are far apart! Always verify \(\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 \ne \mathbf{0}\) first. When \(\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 = \mathbf{0}\), compute the distance between the parallel lines as the point-to-line distance from any point \(P_2 \in L_2\) to \(L_1\):
\[
d(L_1, L_2) = \frac{\left\|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \times \mathbf{d}_1\right\|}{\|\mathbf{d}_1\|}.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Let us run a concrete example: compute the shortest distance between \(L_1: \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \langle 0, 1, 2 \rangle + t\langle 1, 2, -1 \rangle\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \langle 2, 0, 1 \rangle + s\langle 2, 1, 1 \rangle\).
- First compute the common normal:
\[
\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 = \langle 2(1) - (-1)(1),\; (-1)(2) - 1(1),\; 1(1) - 2(2) \rangle = \langle 3, -3, -3 \rangle.
\]
Its length is \(\|\mathbf{n}\| = \sqrt{9 + 9 + 9} = 3\sqrt{3}\). (Notice we may factor out \(3\) and work with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \langle 1, -1, -1 \rangle\) of length \(\sqrt{3}\) in both numerator and denominator, since any positive scalar multiple cancels in \(\frac{|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot \mathbf{n}|}{\|\mathbf{n}\|}\).)
- Form the bridge vector \(\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = \langle 2 - 0,\; 0 - 1,\; 1 - 2 \rangle = \langle 2, -1, -1 \rangle\).
- Dot with \(\langle 1, -1, -1 \rangle\): \(2(1) + (-1)(-1) + (-1)(-1) = 4\).
- Thus \(d(L_1, L_2) = \frac{|4|}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{4\sqrt{3}}{3}\).

=== SECTION 2: Finding the closest points and the common perpendicular ===

Knowing that two skew pipes clear each other by \(2\) meters is useful, yet engineers often need more: WHERE on \(L_1\) and WHERE on \(L_2\) does that closest approach occur, and what line segment connects them?

Look straight down along the common normal \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2\) onto the plane \(\Pi_1\). Because \(\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(\mathbf{d}_2\) are non-parallel, the shadow of \(L_2\) in \(\Pi_1\) is not parallel to \(L_1\), so the two shadows cross at ONE unique point. Directly above that crossing sits \(Q_2 = \mathbf{r}_2(s^*) \in L_2\), and directly below it sits \(Q_1 = \mathbf{r}_1(t^*) \in L_1\). The segment \(Q_1 Q_2\) is the unique common perpendicular of \(L_1\) and \(L_2\): it meets \(L_1\) at a right angle and meets \(L_2\) at a right angle.

Drag the slider \(u\) in the top-down projection below (looking along \(\mathbf{n}\)) to slide a point along \(L_1\): its horizontal gap to \(L_2\) closes to zero at the unique crossing where \(Q_1\) sits directly beneath \(Q_2\).

How do we solve algebraically for the exact parameters \((t^*, s^*)\) of \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\)? Form the general connecting vector from \(L_1\) to \(L_2\):
\[
\mathbf{w}(t, s) = \mathbf{r}_2(s) - \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} + s\mathbf{d}_2 - t\mathbf{d}_1.
\]
The vector \(\mathbf{w}(t^*, s^*)\) is perpendicular to BOTH \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) if and only if its dot product with each direction vector vanishes simultaneously:
\[
\mathbf{w}(t, s) \cdot \mathbf{d}_1 = 0, \qquad \mathbf{w}(t, s) \cdot \mathbf{d}_2 = 0.
\]
Expanding these two dot products yields a \(2 \times 2\) linear system in the two scalar unknowns \(t\) and \(s\):
\[
(\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_1)\,t - (\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2)\,s = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot \mathbf{d}_1,
\]
\[
(\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2)\,t - (\mathbf{d}_2 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2)\,s = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot \mathbf{d}_2.
\]
Why is this \(2 \times 2\) system GUARANTEED to have a unique solution whenever \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) are non-parallel? Compute its coefficient determinant:
\[
-(\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_1)(\mathbf{d}_2 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2) + (\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2)^2 = -\bigl(\|\mathbf{d}_1\|^2 \|\mathbf{d}_2\|^2 - (\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2)^2\bigr) = -\|\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2\|^2,
\]
where the last equality is Lagrange's identity! Because \(\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 \ne \mathbf{0}\), this determinant is strictly nonzero, proving that the closest points \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) exist and are unique!

[CALLOUT]
id: common-perp
label: Method
title: Finding the closest points and common perpendicular of two skew lines
tone: key

Given skew lines \(L_1: \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{p}_1 + t\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \mathbf{p}_2 + s\mathbf{d}_2\):

(1) Form \(\mathbf{w}(t, s) = \mathbf{r}_2(s) - \mathbf{r}_1(t)\) and solve the \(2 \times 2\) orthogonality system
\[
\mathbf{w}(t, s) \cdot \mathbf{d}_1 = 0, \qquad \mathbf{w}(t, s) \cdot \mathbf{d}_2 = 0
\]
for the unique parameter pair \((t^*, s^*)\).
(2) Evaluate the closest points \(Q_1 = \mathbf{r}_1(t^*) \in L_1\) and \(Q_2 = \mathbf{r}_2(s^*) \in L_2\).
(3) Common perpendicular segment and line: \(\mathbf{r}(u) = \mathbf{r}_1(t^*) + u\bigl(\mathbf{r}_2(s^*) - \mathbf{r}_1(t^*)\bigr)\), with \(0 \le u \le 1\) for the segment from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_2\) and \(u \in \mathbb{R}\) for the infinite common perpendicular line.
(4) Built-in verification: check that \(\overrightarrow{Q_1 Q_2}\) is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2\) and that \(\|\overrightarrow{Q_1 Q_2}\|\) matches [REF: skew-dist.1 | the triple-product distance formula].
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: common-perp | the common-perpendicular method] to \(L_1: \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \langle 1, 0, -1 \rangle + t\langle 1, 1, 0 \rangle\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \langle 0, 2, 1 \rangle + s\langle 0, 1, 2 \rangle\).
- First write the connecting vector:
\[
\mathbf{w}(t, s) = \mathbf{r}_2(s) - \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \langle -1 - t,\; 2 + s - t,\; 2 + 2s \rangle.
\]
- Impose both orthogonality equations:
\[
\mathbf{w} \cdot \langle 1, 1, 0 \rangle = (-1 - t) + (2 + s - t) = 1 + s - 2t = 0 \implies s = 2t - 1,
\]
\[
\mathbf{w} \cdot \langle 0, 1, 2 \rangle = (2 + s - t) + 2(2 + 2s) = 6 + 5s - t = 0.
\]
- Substitute \(s = 2t - 1\) into the second equation:
\[
6 + 5(2t - 1) - t = 1 + 9t = 0 \implies t^* = -\frac{1}{9}, \qquad s^* = -\frac{11}{9}.
\]
- Evaluate \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\):
\[
Q_1 = \mathbf{r}_1\!\left(-\frac{1}{9}\right) = \left(\frac{8}{9},\; -\frac{1}{9},\; -1\right), \qquad Q_2 = \mathbf{r}_2\!\left(-\frac{11}{9}\right) = \left(0,\; \frac{7}{9},\; -\frac{13}{9}\right).
\]
- Independent verification: \(\overrightarrow{Q_1 Q_2} = \left\langle -\frac{8}{9},\; \frac{8}{9},\; -\frac{4}{9} \right\rangle = -\frac{4}{9}\langle 2, -2, 1 \rangle\). Notice \(\mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 = \langle 2, -2, 1 \rangle\) has length \(3\), so \(\|\overrightarrow{Q_1 Q_2}\| = \frac{4}{9}\cdot 3 = \frac{4}{3}\). Checking via [REF: skew-dist.1 | the triple-product formula] gives \(\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = \langle -1, 2, 2 \rangle\), \(\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot \langle 2, -2, 1 \rangle = -2 - 4 + 2 = -4\), and \(d = \frac{|-4|}{3} = \frac{4}{3}\)—an exact match!
