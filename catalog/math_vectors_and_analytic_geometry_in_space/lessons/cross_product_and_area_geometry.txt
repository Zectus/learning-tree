=== SECTION 1: Constructing a vector perpendicular to two directions ===

Given two non-parallel vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), they span a flat plane through the origin. In three-dimensional geometry and physics, one task appears over and over: find a nonzero vector that is simultaneously orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)—and whose length encodes the area of the parallelogram they span. The dot product outputs a scalar, so it cannot produce a new vector. We need a genuinely three-dimensional vector-valued product: the cross product \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\).

To write its formula cleanly without memorizing six scattered terms, we introduce two compact algebraic shorthand notations called \(2 \times 2\) and \(3 \times 3\) determinants (no prior matrix theory is needed—treat them purely as expansion rules):
\[
\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc,
\]
and for a \(3 \times 3\) array expanded along its top row,
\[
\begin{vmatrix} r_1 & r_2 & r_3 \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} = r_1 \begin{vmatrix} u_2 & u_3 \\ v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} - r_2 \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} + r_3 \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & u_2 \\ v_1 & v_2 \end{vmatrix}.
\]
Notice the alternating signs \(+\), \(-\), \(+\) across the top row: each entry multiplies the \(2 \times 2\) determinant formed by crossing out its own row and column, with a minus sign in front of the middle term.

[CALLOUT]
id: cross-def
label: Definition
title: The cross product in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
tone: key

For any two vectors \(\mathbf{u} = \langle u_1, u_2, u_3 \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3 \rangle\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), their cross product is the vector \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) given by the formal determinant expansion
\[
\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} = (u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2)\mathbf{i} - (u_1 v_3 - u_3 v_1)\mathbf{j} + (u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1)\mathbf{k}.
\]
In component form, distributing the minus sign in the middle slot gives
\[
\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \langle u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2,\; u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3,\; u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \rangle.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Why does this exact combination guarantee orthogonality to both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)? Check directly using the dot product!
\[
\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = u_1(u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2) + u_2(u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3) + u_3(u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1).
\]
Expand the six products: \(u_1 u_2 v_3 - u_1 u_3 v_2 + u_2 u_3 v_1 - u_1 u_2 v_3 + u_1 u_3 v_2 - u_2 u_3 v_1 = 0\)—every single term cancels in pairs! An identical pairwise cancellation verifies \(\mathbf{v} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = 0\). That also gives you an effortless built-in error check after every cross-product calculation: dot your answer with \(\mathbf{u}\) and with \(\mathbf{v}\). If either dot product is nonzero, you made an arithmetic or sign mistake (most often forgetting the minus sign in front of the \(\mathbf{j}\) term).

Work through an example: let \(\mathbf{a} = \langle 1, 3, -2 \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \langle 2, -1, 4 \rangle\). Applying [REF: cross-def | the cofactor expansion]:
\[
\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 3 & -2 \\ 2 & -1 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = \bigl(3(4) - (-2)(-1)\bigr)\mathbf{i} - \bigl(1(4) - (-2)(2)\bigr)\mathbf{j} + \bigl(1(-1) - 3(2)\bigr)\mathbf{k} = 10\mathbf{i} - 8\mathbf{j} - 7\mathbf{k}.
\]
Quick orthogonality check: \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \langle 10, -8, -7 \rangle = 10 - 24 + 14 = 0\) and \(\mathbf{b} \cdot \langle 10, -8, -7 \rangle = 20 + 8 - 28 = 0\). Both vanish, confirming our calculation.

=== SECTION 2: Algebraic laws, cyclic basis products, and failure of associativity ===

Unlike real-number multiplication and the dot product, the cross product does NOT commute and is NOT associative. Understanding its exact algebraic rules prevents serious sign and grouping errors.

[CALLOUT]
id: cross-laws
label: Theorem
title: Algebraic properties of the cross product
tone: key

For all vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) and every scalar \(c \in \mathbb{R}\):

(1) Anti-commutativity: \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u})\).
(2) Self-cross vanishes: \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\).
(3) Bilinearity (distributivity and scalar pull-out):
\[
(c\mathbf{u}) \times \mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u} \times (c\mathbf{v}), \qquad \mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}, \qquad (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) \times \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}.
\]
(4) Cyclic basis products:
\[
\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}, \qquad \mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}, \qquad \mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j},
\]
and reversing any pair negates the result (\(\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{i} = -\mathbf{k}\), \(\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{j} = -\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{k} = -\mathbf{j}\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Why does [REF: cross-laws.1 | anti-commutativity] hold? Swapping \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) in [REF: cross-def | the component formula] swaps the two products inside every \(2 \times 2\) difference—turning \(u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2\) into \(v_2 u_3 - v_3 u_2 = -(u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2)\) in all three slots. Setting \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}\) immediately forces \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{u} = -(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{u})\), so \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\).

Crucially, the cross product is NOT associative: in general, \((\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \mathbf{w} \ne \mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})\). Test it on the standard basis vectors \(\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{i}\), and \(\mathbf{j}\):
\[
(\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{i}) \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{0} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{0},
\]
whereas regrouping the parentheses gives
\[
\mathbf{i} \times (\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j}) = \mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{k} = -\mathbf{j} \ne \mathbf{0}.
\]
Because grouping changes the result, an unparenthesized string like \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}\) is ambiguous and must never be written.

=== SECTION 3: Lagrange's identity, magnitude, and right-hand orientation ===

We already know \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\) is orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\). Two geometric questions remain: how long is \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\), and which of the two opposite perpendicular directions does it point in?

To find its length, expand \(\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\|^2\) directly from components and compare it with \(\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2\):
\[
\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\|^2 = (u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2)^2 + (u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3)^2 + (u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1)^2
\]
\[
= u_2^2 v_3^2 + u_3^2 v_2^2 + u_3^2 v_1^2 + u_1^2 v_3^2 + u_1^2 v_2^2 + u_2^2 v_1^2 - 2(u_2 u_3 v_2 v_3 + u_1 u_3 v_1 v_3 + u_1 u_2 v_1 v_2).
\]
Meanwhile,
\[
\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 = (u_1^2 + u_2^2 + u_3^2)(v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2) - (u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + u_3 v_3)^2.
\]
Expanding both products cancels the diagonal terms \(u_1^2 v_1^2 + u_2^2 v_2^2 + u_3^2 v_3^2\) and leaves the exact same six squared cross terms minus the exact same three doubled cross terms! Thus we have proved Lagrange's identity:
\[
\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2.
\]
Now substitute the geometric dot-product formula \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\| \cos\theta\) (where \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)):
\[
\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 (1 - \cos^2\theta) = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \sin^2\theta.
\]
Because \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\) implies \(\sin\theta \ge 0\), taking the nonnegative square root yields the geometric magnitude formula.

[CALLOUT]
id: cross-geom
label: Theorem
title: Geometric characterization of the cross product
tone: key

Let \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) be nonzero vectors with angle \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\) between them.

(1) Magnitude: \(\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta\).
(2) Direction (right-hand rule): \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\) is orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\), pointing in the direction of your right thumb when the fingers of your right hand curl from \(\mathbf{u}\) toward \(\mathbf{v}\) through the angle \(\theta \in (0, \pi)\).
(3) Parallelism criterion: \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\) if and only if \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are parallel (i.e., \(\theta = 0\) or \(\theta = \pi\), or one vector is \(\mathbf{0}\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: cross-geom.3 | the parallelism criterion] complements the dot-product orthogonality test: \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\) detects perpendicular vectors (\(\cos\theta = 0\)), whereas \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\) detects parallel vectors (\(\sin\theta = 0\)). Also notice why the right-hand rule agrees with [REF: cross-laws.4 | our cyclic basis products]: curling your right fingers from \(\mathbf{i}\) to \(\mathbf{j}\) points your thumb along \(+\mathbf{k}\), matching \(\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}\).

=== SECTION 4: Spatial areas, perpendicular directions, and torque ===

Place two non-parallel vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) tail to tail in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). They span a parallelogram whose base has length \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\) and whose perpendicular altitude is \(h = \|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta\). Multiplying base by height gives
\[
\text{Area}(\text{parallelogram}) = \|\mathbf{u}\| \cdot (\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta) = \|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\|.
\]
Splitting the parallelogram along a diagonal yields two congruent triangles, so the triangle with adjacent sides \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)—or equivalently, with vertices \(P, Q, R \in \mathbb{R}^3\)—has exact area
\[
\text{Area}(\triangle PQR) = \frac{1}{2}\|\overrightarrow{PQ} \times \overrightarrow{PR}\|.
\]

Drag the angle slider \(a\) (in radians) below to watch the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{u} = \langle 3, 0 \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 2\cos a, 2\sin a \rangle\). Its area \(\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\| = 6\sin a\) peaks when the sides are perpendicular (\(a = \pi/2\)) and collapses to \(0\) as the parallelogram flattens along the \(x\)-axis (\(a \to 0\) or \(a \to \pi\)).

Two more everyday applications follow directly from [REF: cross-geom | the geometric theorem]:
1. Unit normals to a pair of directions: Given two non-parallel vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), every vector orthogonal to both is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}\). Normalizing produces exactly two unit vectors perpendicular to both \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\):
\[
\pm \frac{\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}\|}.
\]
Never forget the \(\pm\) sign when a problem asks for "all unit vectors orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\)."
2. Physical torque: When a force \(\mathbf{F}\) is applied at a point with position vector \(\mathbf{r}\) relative to a pivot \(O\), its turning effect (torque) about \(O\) is the vector
\[
\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}.
\]
Its magnitude \(\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\| = \|\mathbf{r}\|\,\|\mathbf{F}\|\sin\theta\) measures the lever arm times the perpendicular component of force \(F_\perp = \|\mathbf{F}\|\sin\theta\) (pulling straight along the wrench gives \(\theta = 0\) and zero torque), and its direction points along the axis of rotation given by the right-hand rule. Remember order matters: \(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\), not \(\mathbf{F} \times \mathbf{r}\).
