=== SECTION 1: The vector triple product and the BAC-CAB identity ===

Taking the dot product of a vector with a cross product produces the scalar triple product \(\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})\). What happens if instead we take the CROSS product of a vector with another cross product, forming the vector triple product \(\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})\)?

Before computing a single component, pause and ask where the output vector \(\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})\) must point geometrically! Suppose \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\) are non-parallel, so they span a unique plane \(\Pi\) through the origin.
- First, \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}\) stands strictly perpendicular to \(\Pi\).
- Next, \(\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}\).
- Any vector perpendicular to the normal of \(\Pi\) must lie BACK inside \(\Pi\) itself!

Therefore, \(\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})\) MUST be a linear combination of \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\):
\[
\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) = \alpha\,\mathbf{v} + \beta\,\mathbf{w}
\]
for two scalars \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) depending on \(\mathbf{u}\), \(\mathbf{v}\), and \(\mathbf{w}\). To discover the exact scalars \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), expand the first component directly. Let \(\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w} = \langle v_2 w_3 - v_3 w_2,\; v_3 w_1 - v_1 w_3,\; v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1 \rangle\). The first component of \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{p}\) is
\[
u_2 p_3 - u_3 p_2 = u_2(v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1) - u_3(v_3 w_1 - v_1 w_3) = (u_2 w_2 + u_3 w_3)v_1 - (u_2 v_2 + u_3 v_3)w_1.
\]
Now add and subtract the missing term \(u_1 v_1 w_1\) so that full dot products emerge:
\[
(u_1 w_1 + u_2 w_2 + u_3 w_3)v_1 - (u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + u_3 v_3)w_1 = (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w})v_1 - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})w_1.
\]
By cyclic symmetry of the indices \((1, 2, 3)\), the second and third components satisfy the exact same formula! This proves the celebrated BAC–CAB identity (named after writing \(\mathbf{a} \times (\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{b}(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})\)).

What if the parentheses sit on the LEFT instead, giving \((\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \mathbf{w}\)? Never guess—apply anti-commutativity of the cross product once to move the single vector to the left, and then apply BAC–CAB:
\[
(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \mathbf{w} = -\mathbf{w} \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = -\bigl[(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{u} - (\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}\bigr] = (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{u}.
\]
Notice the structural contrast: \(\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})\) always lies in the plane of \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\), whereas \((\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \mathbf{w}\) always lies in the plane of \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)! Their difference makes the failure of cross-product associativity completely quantitative:
\[
\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) - (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{u} - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{w}.
\]

id: bac-cab
label: Theorem
title: Vector triple product (BAC–CAB) identities
tone: key

For any three vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3\):

(1) Right-parenthesized triple product:
\[
\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{w}.
\]
(2) Left-parenthesized triple product:
\[
(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{u}.
\]
Memory rule for both: (outer vector dotted with the FARTHER inner vector) times the MIDDLE vector, MINUS (outer vector dotted with the NEARER inner vector) times the OTHER inner vector.
[/CALLOUT]

Check [REF: bac-cab | the BAC–CAB identities] on the standard counterexample \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{j}\):
- \(\mathbf{i} \times (\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j}) = (\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{j})\mathbf{i} - (\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{i})\mathbf{j} = 0\mathbf{i} - 1\mathbf{j} = -\mathbf{j}\).
- \((\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{i}) \times \mathbf{j} = (\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{j})\mathbf{i} - (\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{j})\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{0}\).
Both match direct evaluation immediately, and the difference formula gives \((\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{j})\mathbf{i} - (\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{i})\mathbf{j} = -\mathbf{j}\).

=== SECTION 2: The Jacobi and Binet–Cauchy identities ===

Once [REF: bac-cab.1 | the BAC–CAB identity] is in hand, two major 3D vector identities drop out in just a couple of lines without any component algebra.

First, even though the cross product fails associativity, the sum of all three cyclic right-parenthesized triple products vanishes identically! Expand each term with [REF: bac-cab.1 | BAC–CAB]:
\[
\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{w},
\]
\[
\mathbf{v} \times (\mathbf{w} \times \mathbf{u}) = (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{w} - (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{u},
\]
\[
\mathbf{w} \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = (\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{u} - (\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}.
\]
Because the dot product is symmetric (\(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}\), etc.), adding the three right-hand sides cancels every \(\mathbf{u}\)-term, every \(\mathbf{v}\)-term, and every \(\mathbf{w}\)-term in pairs, leaving \(\mathbf{0}\).

Second, consider the dot product of TWO cross products, \((\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{c} \times \mathbf{d})\). Treat \(\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{c} \times \mathbf{d}\) as a single vector and apply the dot-cross interchange rule \((\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{a} \cdot (\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{q})\) from the scalar triple product:
\[
(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{c} \times \mathbf{d}) = \mathbf{a} \cdot \bigl(\mathbf{b} \times (\mathbf{c} \times \mathbf{d})\bigr).
\]
Expand the inner vector triple product using [REF: bac-cab.1 | BAC–CAB]:
\[
\mathbf{a} \cdot \bigl[(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{d})\mathbf{c} - (\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c})\mathbf{d}\bigr] = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c})(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{d}) - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{d})(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c}).
\]
Notice what happens when you set \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{b}\):
\[
\|\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}\|^2 = (\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a})(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b}) - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})^2 = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 \|\mathbf{b}\|^2 - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})^2,
\]
recovering Lagrange's identity in a single step!

[CALLOUT]
id: jacobi-binet
label: Theorems
title: The Jacobi identity and the Binet–Cauchy identity
tone: key

For all vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^3\):

(1) Jacobi identity:
\[
\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) + \mathbf{v} \times (\mathbf{w} \times \mathbf{u}) + \mathbf{w} \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}.
\]
(2) Binet–Cauchy identity:
\[
(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{c} \times \mathbf{d}) = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c})(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{d}) - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{d})(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c}) = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c} & \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{d} \\ \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c} & \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{d} \end{vmatrix}.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Writing [REF: jacobi-binet.2 | the Binet–Cauchy identity] as a \(2 \times 2\) determinant of dot products makes the pairing impossible to mix up: rows are labeled by \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\) (the first cross product) and columns by \(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}\) (the second cross product).

=== SECTION 3: Orthogonal plane projection via double cross products ===

What happens when the same vector \(\mathbf{n}\) appears twice in a vector triple product, as in \(\mathbf{n} \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{n})\)? Apply [REF: bac-cab.1 | BAC–CAB]:
\[
\mathbf{n} \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{n}) = (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{n})\mathbf{u} - (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{n} = \|\mathbf{n}\|^2\,\mathbf{u} - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}.
\]
When \(\mathbf{n}\) is a UNIT normal vector (\(\|\mathbf{n}\| = 1\)) to a plane \(\Pi\) through the origin, this formula simplifies to
\[
\mathbf{n} \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{n}) = \mathbf{u} - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}.
\]
Recognize that right-hand side! Because \((\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{n})\mathbf{n} = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{u}\) is the component of \(\mathbf{u}\) perpendicular to \(\Pi\), subtracting it leaves \(\mathbf{u}_\perp\), the exact orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{u}\) ONTO the plane \(\Pi\). Notice why the two cross products accomplish this geometrically: the first cross product \(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{n}\) kills the component of \(\mathbf{u}\) along \(\mathbf{n}\) and rotates the planar component by \(90^\circ\) inside \(\Pi\); crossing on the left with \(\mathbf{n}\) rotates it back by \(90^\circ\) inside \(\Pi\) to land right on \(\mathbf{u}_\perp\)!

Drag the angle slider \(a\) below to rotate \(\mathbf{u}\) in the vertical plane spanned by \(\mathbf{n}\) and \(\Pi\): the double cross product \(\mathbf{n} \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{n})\) always tracks the exact horizontal shadow \(\mathbf{u}_\perp\) of \(\mathbf{u}\) inside \(\Pi\).

=== SECTION 4: Solving the cross-product equation \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) ===

In ordinary scalar algebra, solving \(ax = b\) for \(x\) (with \(a \ne 0\)) produces a single unique answer \(x = b/a\). In 3D vector algebra, solving the cross-product equation
\[
\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \qquad (\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0})
\]
for an unknown vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) behaves dramatically differently—you CANNOT "divide by a vector," not every right-hand side \(\mathbf{b}\) has a solution, and when a solution exists, infinitely many solutions exist!

Let us derive the exact solvability condition and complete solution family directly from [REF: bac-cab.1 | BAC–CAB].
1. Necessary condition for any solution to exist: because \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x}\) is always orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\), dotting both sides with \(\mathbf{a}\) forces
\[
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} \cdot (\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x}) = 0.
\]
If \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} \ne 0\), the equation \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) has NO solution at all!
2. Extracting \(\mathbf{x}\) when \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0\): cross both sides on the left with \(\mathbf{a}\) and apply [REF: bac-cab.1 | BAC–CAB]:
\[
\mathbf{a} \times (\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} \implies (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{x})\mathbf{a} - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a})\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}.
\]
Rearranging for \(\mathbf{x}\) and using \(-\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}\) gives
\[
\|\mathbf{a}\|^2\,\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}) + (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{x})\mathbf{a} \implies \mathbf{x} = \frac{\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2} + \left(\frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2}\right)\mathbf{a}.
\]
3. Notice that the scalar \(t = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2}\) is completely unconstrained by \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\)! Why? Verify by substituting \(\mathbf{x} = \frac{\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2} + t\mathbf{a}\) back into \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x}\) for ANY real \(t\):
\[
\mathbf{a} \times \left(\frac{\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2} + t\mathbf{a}\right) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \times (\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a})}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2} + t(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{a}) = \frac{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2\mathbf{b} - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{b},
\]
where the last equality holds precisely because \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0\)!

title: Complete solution of \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\)

Let \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) with \(\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0}\).

(1) Solvability criterion: the equation \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) has a solution \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) if and only if \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0\).
(2) General solution: when \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0\), the complete solution set is the line parallel to \(\mathbf{a}\):
\[
\mathbf{x} = \frac{\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2} + t\mathbf{a} \qquad (t \in \mathbb{R}).
\]
(3) Minimum-norm solution: setting \(t = 0\) gives the unique solution \(\mathbf{x}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2}\) orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\). Imposing any auxiliary dot-product condition \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{x} = c\) fixes \(t = \frac{c}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^2}\) uniquely.
[/CALLOUT]
