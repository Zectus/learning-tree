=== SECTION 1: What a generalized cylinder really is ===

When most people hear the word "cylinder," they picture a soup can: a round tube extending straight up and down. In three-dimensional analytic geometry, however, circular cross-sections are only one special case. Why? Because the defining geometric mechanism of a cylinder is not roundness—it is parallel translation of a straight line along any planar curve.

[CALLOUT]
id: gen-cylinder
label: Definition
title: Generalized cylinder, directrix, and rulings
tone: key

Let \(C\) be a curve lying in a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and let \(\mathbf{L}\) be a fixed line not parallel to that plane. The generalized cylinder generated by \(C\) is the surface swept out by all lines parallel to \(\mathbf{L}\) that intersect \(C\).

(1) The planar generating curve \(C\) is called the directrix (or base curve).
(2) The parallel lines sweeping out the surface are called the rulings (or generators).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice what happens when the directrix lies in one of the three coordinate planes and the rulings run perpendicular to that plane—that is, parallel to the remaining coordinate axis. Take the equation
\[
z = x^2
\]
viewed inside \(\mathbb{R}^3\) rather than \(\mathbb{R}^2\). In the \(xz\)-plane (\(y = 0\)), this equation draws the familiar parabola \(z = x^2\). Because the variable \(y\) does not appear anywhere in the equation, once a pair \((x_0, z_0)\) satisfies \(z_0 = x_0^2\), every point \((x_0, y, z_0)\) satisfies the equation for all real \(y \in \mathbb{R}\). Geometrically, through every point \((x_0, 0, z_0)\) on the parabola passes an entire horizontal ruling parallel to the \(y\)-axis, sweeping out a parabolic trough called a parabolic cylinder.

zlabel: z
xrange: -2.2, 2.2

This rule holds in full generality for any coordinate-aligned cylinder:

[CALLOUT]
id: missing-var-rule
label: Rule
title: Missing-variable criterion for coordinate cylinders
tone: key

An equation in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) involving only two of the three variables \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) defines a generalized cylinder whose rulings are parallel to the axis of the missing variable. Its directrix is the planar curve defined by the same equation in the coordinate plane of the two present variables.
[/CALLOUT]

Look at how quickly [REF: missing-var-rule | the missing-variable criterion] classifies a wide range of surfaces that are all genuine cylinders—even when none of them looks like a round tube:

[TABLE]
Equation in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) | Missing variable | Ruling direction | Directrix shape | Surface name
\(x^2 + y^2 = 9\) | \(z\) | \(z\)-axis | Circle of radius \(3\) in \(xy\)-plane | Circular cylinder
\(\frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1\) | \(z\) | \(z\)-axis | Ellipse in \(xy\)-plane | Elliptic cylinder
\(z = x^2\) | \(y\) | \(y\)-axis | Parabola in \(xz\)-plane | Parabolic cylinder
\(z^2 - x^2 = 1\) | \(y\) | \(y\)-axis | Hyperbola in \(xz\)-plane | Hyperbolic cylinder
\(z = \sin y\) | \(x\) | \(x\)-axis | Sine wave in \(yz\)-plane | Sinusoidal cylinder
[/TABLE]

Always check the ambient dimension before interpreting an equation. In \(\mathbb{R}^2\), \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\) is a one-dimensional circle; in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), where \(z\) is completely unconstrained, the exact same formula describes a two-dimensional infinite tube extending along the entire \(z\)-axis. Similarly, \(z = 2x + 1\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) omits \(y\), so it is a flat plane whose rulings run parallel to the \(y\)-axis—a plane is simply the special cylinder whose directrix is a straight line.

=== SECTION 2: Sketching and writing equations of cylinders ===

Because every cross-section perpendicular to the rulings of a coordinate cylinder is an exact copy of its directrix, sketching or building a cylinder reduces to a two-step habit: first identify the missing coordinate (which locks in the ruling direction), and second analyze the 2D curve in the plane of the other two variables.

Suppose you want to sketch the elliptic cylinder \(\frac{y^2}{4} + \frac{z^2}{9} = 1\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Since \(x\) is missing, the rulings run parallel to the \(x\)-axis. In the \(yz\)-plane (\(x = 0\)), the equation gives an ellipse with \(y\)-intercepts at \((0, \pm 2, 0)\) and \(z\)-intercepts at \((0, 0, \pm 3)\). Copying that exact ellipse at \(x = -2\) and \(x = 2\) and connecting corresponding vertices by straight lines parallel to the \(x\)-axis produces an accurate sketch of the tube.

Going in reverse—writing the equation of a cylinder from geometric data—works just as cleanly. Suppose a cylinder has rulings parallel to the \(y\)-axis, and its cross-section in the plane \(y = 4\) is the curve \(z = \cos x\). Does the number \(4\) enter the equation of the cylinder? No: because every ruling runs parallel to the \(y\)-axis, any point \((x, y, z)\) on the cylinder has the same \(x\)- and \(z\)-coordinates as the point \((x, 4, z)\) where its ruling pierces the plane \(y = 4\). Therefore \((x, y, z)\) lies on the cylinder if and only if \(z = \cos x\), regardless of \(y\).

=== SECTION 3: Surfaces of revolution around a coordinate axis ===

A second fundamental way to build a curved surface from a 2D curve is to spin it rather than slide it. Imagine taking a planar profile curve \(C\) lying in a coordinate plane—say \(z = f(x)\) (with \(x \ge 0\)) in the \(xz\)-plane—and revolving it \(360^\circ\) around the \(z\)-axis. What equation in \((x, y, z)\) must every point on the swept surface obey?

Track a single point \(P_0(r_0, 0, z_0)\) on the profile curve in the \(xz\)-plane, where \(r_0 \ge 0\) is its perpendicular distance from the \(z\)-axis. As \(P_0\) revolves around the \(z\)-axis, its height \(z = z_0\) stays fixed, and its distance from the \(z\)-axis stays constant at \(r_0\). Therefore \(P_0\) traces out a horizontal circle of radius \(r_0\) in the plane \(z = z_0\), centered on the \(z\)-axis at \((0, 0, z_0)\).

[GRAPH]

ylabel: \(y\)

xrange: -2.6, 2.6
yrange: -2.6, 2.6

segment: 0, 0, 1.2, 0 | label: \(x\) | dashed: true
segment: 1.2, 0, 1.2, 1.6 | label: \(y\) | dashed: true
point: 0, 0 | label: \((0, 0, z_0)\)
point: 2, 0 | label: \(P_0(r_0, 0, z_0)\)
point: 1.2, 1.6 | label: \(P(x, y, z_0)\)
[/GRAPH]

Every point \(P(x, y, z_0)\) on that circular orbit satisfies \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r_0\). Since the profile curve relates height \(z_0\) to radial distance \(r_0\) by \(z_0 = f(r_0)\), replacing the radial distance \(r_0\) with \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) immediately gives the equation of the entire surface:
\[
z = f\bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\bigr).
\]
Notice that the same derivation works around the \(x\)-axis or \(y\)-axis: in each case, identify which variable measures distance from the axis of revolution in the starting coordinate plane, and replace that variable by the 3D distance from that axis.

[CALLOUT]
id: rev-rules
label: Rule
title: Equations of surfaces of revolution around coordinate axes
tone: key

When a planar curve in a coordinate plane (lying on the nonnegative side of the radial variable) is revolved about a coordinate axis, replace the radial variable with the distance from the axis of revolution:

(1) About the \(z\)-axis: from a curve \(F(x, z) = 0\) in the \(xz\)-plane (or \(F(y, z) = 0\) in the \(yz\)-plane), replace the radial coordinate \(x\) (or \(y\)) with \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), giving \(F\bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2},\; z\bigr) = 0\).
(2) About the \(y\)-axis: from a curve \(F(x, y) = 0\) in the \(xy\)-plane (or \(F(z, y) = 0\) in the \(yz\)-plane), replace \(x\) (or \(z\)) with \(\sqrt{x^2 + z^2}\), giving \(F\bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + z^2},\; y\bigr) = 0\).
(3) About the \(x\)-axis: from a curve \(F(y, x) = 0\) in the \(xy\)-plane (or \(F(z, x) = 0\) in the \(xz\)-plane), replace \(y\) (or \(z\)) with \(\sqrt{y^2 + z^2}\), giving \(F\bigl(\sqrt{y^2 + z^2},\; x\bigr) = 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

Apply [REF: rev-rules.1 | the z-axis rule] to two familiar profile curves in the \(xz\)-plane:
- Revolve the parabola \(z = \frac{1}{2}x^2\) about the \(z\)-axis. Replacing \(x\) by \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) gives \(z = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\bigr)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(x^2 + y^2)\), a circular paraboloid (bowl shape).
- Revolve the half-line \(z = x\) (with \(x \ge 0\)) about the \(z\)-axis. Replacing \(x\) by \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) gives \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), the upper nappe of a right circular cone (notice that squaring to \(z^2 = x^2 + y^2\) adds the lower nappe \(z \le 0\), corresponding to revolving the full line \(z = x\)).

[GRAPH]
type: surface3d
title: Circular paraboloid \(z = \frac{1}{2}(x^2 + y^2)\) generated by revolving \(z = \frac{1}{2}x^2\) about the \(z\)-axis
xlabel: \(x\)
ylabel: \(y\)
zlabel: z
xrange: -2.4, 2.4
yrange: -2.4, 2.4

[/GRAPH]

Watch carefully for sign changes when you eliminate a square root by squaring. If you revolve \(y = e^x\) in the \(xy\)-plane about the \(x\)-axis, [REF: rev-rules.3 | the x-axis rule] replaces the radial variable \(y\) (which is already positive since \(e^x > 0\)) with \(\sqrt{y^2 + z^2}\), giving \(\sqrt{y^2 + z^2} = e^x\). Because both sides are nonnegative for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), squaring yields the completely equivalent equation \(y^2 + z^2 = e^{2x}\). By contrast, if a profile equation had \(y = x\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) and we revolved only the half \(y \ge 0\) (i.e. \(x \ge 0\)), squaring \(\sqrt{y^2 + z^2} = x\) to \(y^2 + z^2 = x^2\) would admit points with \(x < 0\) unless we explicitly retain the condition \(x \ge 0\).

=== SECTION 4: Detecting rotational symmetry and recovering the profile ===

In practice, you are often handed a 3D equation and asked whether it represents a surface of revolution—and if so, around which axis and from what generating curve. Look directly at how the variables enter the formula:

[CALLOUT]
id: rev-detect
label: Criterion
title: Recognizing a surface of revolution from its equation
tone: key

An equation in \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) defines a surface of revolution about a coordinate axis if and only if the other two variables appear exclusively in the radial combination of their sum of squares (up to a common constant factor):

(1) About the \(z\)-axis: \(x\) and \(y\) enter only through \(x^2 + y^2\).
(2) About the \(y\)-axis: \(x\) and \(z\) enter only through \(x^2 + z^2\).
(3) About the \(x\)-axis: \(y\) and \(z\) enter only through \(y^2 + z^2\).

To recover a generating profile curve, set either one of those two radial variables to \(0\) to slice the surface with a coordinate plane containing the axis of revolution.
[/CALLOUT]

Compare three quick examples to see [REF: rev-detect | this criterion] in action:
- Consider \(3x^2 + 3z^2 - y = 2\). Because \(x\) and \(z\) appear only as \(3(x^2 + z^2)\) with identical coefficients, every slice \(y = y_0\) with \(y_0 > -2\) is a circle \(x^2 + z^2 = (y_0 + 2)/3\) centered on the \(y\)-axis. It is a surface of revolution about the \(y\)-axis. Setting \(z = 0\) recovers the generating parabola \(3x^2 - y = 2\) (i.e., \(y = 3x^2 - 2\)) in the \(xy\)-plane.
- Consider \(x^2 + 4y^2 = z\). Here \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) have unequal coefficients (\(1 \ne 4\)), so horizontal slices \(z = c > 0\) are ellipses rather than circles. It is not a surface of revolution about the \(z\)-axis (nor about the \(x\)- or \(y\)-axis).
- Consider \(x^2 + y^2 + 2x = z\). Even though \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) share coefficient \(1\), the extra linear term \(2x\) shifts the horizontal circles to \((x + 1)^2 + y^2 = z + 1\), centered along the vertical line \(x = -1, y = 0\) rather than along the \(z\)-axis. It is a surface of revolution about the shifted line \(x = -1, y = 0\), not about a coordinate axis.

Notice also a useful overlap between Sections 1 and 3: a circular cylinder such as \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\) is both a generalized cylinder (rulings parallel to the \(z\)-axis) and a surface of revolution (revolving the vertical line \(x = 3\) in the \(xz\)-plane about the \(z\)-axis). Likewise, a sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = R^2\) is a surface of revolution about all three coordinate axes simultaneously.
