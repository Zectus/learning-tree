=== SECTION 1: Vector and parametric equations of lines in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) ===

In the two-dimensional plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\), a single linear equation \(ax + by = c\) specifies a straight line. Moving into \(\mathbb{R}^3\), students often expect a single linear equation \(ax + by + cz = d\) to define a line as well—but it does not! One linear constraint among three variables leaves two independent degrees of freedom, which produces a flat two-dimensional plane rather than a one-dimensional line. To describe a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), we want a description with exactly one free parameter \(t \in \mathbb{R}\).

How is a straight line \(L\) in space uniquely determined? Pick any base point \(P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)\) on \(L\) (with position vector \(\mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle\)) and any nonzero direction vector \(\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle\) parallel to \(L\). A point \(P(x, y, z)\) with position vector \(\mathbf{r} = \langle x, y, z \rangle\) lies on \(L\) if and only if the displacement \(\overrightarrow{P_0 P} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\)—that is, \(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0 = t\mathbf{v}\) for some real scalar \(t\). Rearranging gives the vector equation of \(L\), and equating components yields its parametric equations.

[CALLOUT]
id: line-param
label: Definition
title: Vector, parametric, and segment equations of a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
tone: key

Let \(P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)\) be a point on a line \(L\) with position vector \(\mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle\), and let \(\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle \ne \mathbf{0}\) be a direction vector of \(L\).

(1) Vector equation: \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{v}\), for \(t \in \mathbb{R}\).
(2) Parametric equations:
\[
x = x_0 + at, \qquad y = y_0 + bt, \qquad z = z_0 + ct \qquad (t \in \mathbb{R}).
\]
(3) Line segment from \(P_0\) to \(P_1\): setting \(\mathbf{v} = \overrightarrow{P_0 P_1} = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_0\) and restricting \(t\) to \([0, 1]\) gives
\[
\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_0) = (1 - t)\mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{r}_1 \qquad (0 \le t \le 1).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: line-param.3 | the segment parametrization] behaves: at \(t = 0\), \(\mathbf{r}(0) = \mathbf{r}_0\) (starting at \(P_0\)); at \(t = 1\), \(\mathbf{r}(1) = \mathbf{r}_1\) (arriving at \(P_1\)); and values \(t \in (0, 1)\) sweep out every interior point of the segment \(P_0 P_1\) proportionally (for instance, \(t = 1/2\) lands at the midpoint). Allowing \(t < 0\) or \(t > 1\) extends the segment infinitely past \(P_0\) and \(P_1\) along the full line.

Drag the parameter slider \(t\) in the \(xy\)-plane projection below to watch \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1, 1 \rangle + t\langle 2, 1 \rangle\) move along the line through \(P_0(1, 1)\) and \(P_1(3, 2)\), crossing the highlighted segment precisely on \(0 \le t \le 1\).

For a 3D example, find parametric equations for the line through \(A(2, -1, 4)\) and \(B(5, 3, 4)\). Choose \(\mathbf{v} = \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 5 - 2, 3 - (-1), 4 - 4 \rangle = \langle 3, 4, 0 \rangle\). Using \(A\) as the base point gives
\[
x = 2 + 3t, \qquad y = -1 + 4t, \qquad z = 4 \qquad (t \in \mathbb{R}).
\]
Notice that \(z = 4\) stays constant for every \(t\): the entire line lies flat inside the horizontal plane \(z = 4\).

=== SECTION 2: Symmetric equations and zero direction components ===

Sometimes we want a parameter-free description of a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Starting from [REF: line-param.2 | the parametric equations] \(x = x_0 + at\), \(y = y_0 + bt\), \(z = z_0 + ct\), suppose first that all three direction components \(a, b, c\) are nonzero. Solving each equation for \(t\) gives
\[
t = \frac{x - x_0}{a}, \qquad t = \frac{y - y_0}{b}, \qquad t = \frac{z - z_0}{c}.
\]
Equating the three expressions eliminates \(t\) and produces the symmetric equations of the line.

What if one or two components of \(\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle\) equal \(0\)? You can never divide by zero! Instead, any zero direction component simply pins its coordinate to a constant value, while the remaining nonzero components stay in symmetric form.

[CALLOUT]
id: line-symm
label: Formulas
title: Symmetric equations of a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
tone: key

For the line through \(P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)\) with direction vector \(\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle \ne \mathbf{0}\):

(1) If \(a \ne 0\), \(b \ne 0\), and \(c \ne 0\):
\[
\frac{x - x_0}{a} = \frac{y - y_0}{b} = \frac{z - z_0}{c}.
\]
(2) If one component vanishes—for example, \(a = 0\) with \(b, c \ne 0\)—write the constant coordinate separately alongside the remaining equality:
\[
x = x_0, \qquad \frac{y - y_0}{b} = \frac{z - z_0}{c}.
\]
(3) If two components vanish—for example, \(a = 0\) and \(b = 0\) with \(c \ne 0\)—the line is specified by two constant-coordinate equations: \(x = x_0\) and \(y = y_0\) (with \(z\) free).
[/CALLOUT]

When reading a base point and direction vector back out of symmetric equations, always make sure each numerator has the exact form \(x - x_0\), \(y - y_0\), or \(z - z_0\) with coefficient \(+1\) in front of the variable. Watch what happens on a disguised equation like
\[
\frac{3 - 2x}{4} = \frac{y + 1}{5}, \qquad z = 7.
\]
Factor \(-2\) out of the first numerator to isolate \(x\) with coefficient \(+1\):
\[
\frac{3 - 2x}{4} = \frac{-2(x - 3/2)}{4} = \frac{x - 3/2}{-2}.
\]
Now every piece is visible in [REF: line-symm.2 | standard symmetric form]: the base point is \(P_0(3/2, -1, 7)\) and a direction vector is \(\mathbf{v} = \langle -2, 5, 0 \rangle\). Reading the denominator \(4\) directly without factoring out \(-2\) would give the wrong direction vector and the wrong sign.

=== SECTION 3: Non-uniqueness of parametrizations ===

A single geometric line \(L\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has infinitely many distinct vector and parametric equations. Why? Because two free choices enter [REF: line-param.1 | the vector equation] \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{v}\):
- You may replace the base point \(P_0\) by any other point \(Q_0\) lying on \(L\).
- You may replace the direction vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by any nonzero scalar multiple \(k\mathbf{v}\) (\(k \ne 0\)), which only changes the speed or orientation with which the parameter traces \(L\).

Therefore, two parametrizations \(\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{p}_1 + t\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2(s) = \mathbf{p}_2 + s\mathbf{v}_2\) represent the exact same geometric line if and only if two conditions hold simultaneously:
1. Their direction vectors are parallel: \(\mathbf{v}_2 = k\mathbf{v}_1\) for some \(k \ne 0\).
2. The base point of the second line lies on the first line: there exists a single real number \(t^*\) such that \(\mathbf{p}_2 = \mathbf{p}_1 + t^*\mathbf{v}_1\) (equivalently, \(\mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_1\) is also parallel to \(\mathbf{v}_1\)).

Checking only one of the two conditions is not enough. If \(\mathbf{v}_2 \parallel \mathbf{v}_1\) but \(\mathbf{p}_2\) does not lie on \(L_1\), the two lines are parallel and distinct. Conversely, if \(\mathbf{p}_2\) lies on \(L_1\) but \(\mathbf{v}_2\) is not parallel to \(\mathbf{v}_1\), the two lines intersect at the single point \(\mathbf{p}_2\) and then head in different directions.

=== SECTION 4: Relative positions of two lines in space and skew lines ===

In \(\mathbb{R}^2\), two distinct lines either are parallel or intersect at a point. In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), a genuinely new fourth possibility appears: two lines can fail to be parallel and still completely miss each other—like an east-west highway running along an overpass above a north-south road underneath it. Such lines are called skew lines.

[CALLOUT]
id: line-classify
label: Classification
title: Four relative positions of two lines in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
tone: key

Given two lines \(L_1: \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{p}_1 + t\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \mathbf{p}_2 + s\mathbf{v}_2\):

(1) First test whether \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\) are parallel (\(\mathbf{v}_2 = k\mathbf{v}_1\) for some \(k \ne 0\)):
- If \(\mathbf{v}_1 \parallel \mathbf{v}_2\) and \(\mathbf{p}_2 \in L_1\), the lines are coincident (the exact same line).
- If \(\mathbf{v}_1 \parallel \mathbf{v}_2\) and \(\mathbf{p}_2 \notin L_1\), the lines are parallel and distinct.
(2) If \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\) are NOT parallel, solve the system of three linear equations \(\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{r}_2(s)\) in the two independent parameters \(t\) and \(s\):
- If a pair \((t^*, s^*)\) satisfies all three coordinate equations, the lines are intersecting, with unique intersection point \(\mathbf{r}_1(t^*) = \mathbf{r}_2(s^*)\).
- If no pair \((t, s)\) satisfies all three coordinate equations, the lines are skew.
[/CALLOUT]

Why is it mandatory to assign two different parameter names—\(t\) for \(L_1\) and \(s\) for \(L_2\)—even if a problem prints \(t\) in both equations? Think of two airplanes flying along straight tracks. Setting \(\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{r}_2(t)\) with the same parameter \(t\) checks only whether the two planes collide at the exact same instant \(t\). Geometric intersection asks whether the two flight paths share a point in space at all, which can happen when the first plane passes that point at time \(t^*\) and the second passes it at a different time \(s^*\). Reusing one letter \(t\) misses every intersection with \(t^* \ne s^*\) and misclassifies intersecting lines as skew.

The diagram below illustrates the shadows in the \(xy\)-plane of two non-parallel 3D lines: \(L_1: \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \langle 0, 1, 2 \rangle + t\langle 2, 1, 1 \rangle\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \langle 4, 0, 3 \rangle + s\langle -1, 2, 1 \rangle\). In the \(xy\)-plane their shadows cross cleanly at \((2, 2)\) (reached at \(t = 1\) on \(L_1\) and \(s = 2\) on \(L_2\)). Yet comparing their heights at those parameter values gives \(z_1(1) = 3\) versus \(z_2(2) = 5\): the two lines pass directly over one another without touching, so they are skew. Changing the base height of \(L_2\) from \(3\) to \(1\) would lower \(z_2(2)\) to \(3 = z_1(1)\), turning the same pair of \(xy\)-shadows into intersecting lines meeting at \((2, 2, 3)\).

To carry out [REF: line-classify.2 | the 3-equation test] reliably:
1. Pick any two of the three coordinate equations that determine a unique solution \((t^*, s^*)\).
2. Substitute \((t^*, s^*)\) into the third coordinate equation.
3. If the third equation holds, the lines intersect at \(\mathbf{r}_1(t^*)\); if it fails, the lines are skew.

[TABLE]
Relative position | Directions \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\) | Solution of \(\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{r}_2(s)\) | Lie in a common plane?
Coincident | Parallel | Infinitely many pairs \((t, s)\) | Yes
Parallel and distinct | Parallel | No solution | Yes
Intersecting | Not parallel | Unique pair \((t^*, s^*)\) | Yes
Skew | Not parallel | No solution | No
[/TABLE]
