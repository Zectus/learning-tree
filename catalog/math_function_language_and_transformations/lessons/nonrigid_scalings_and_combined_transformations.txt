=== SECTION 1: Vertical and horizontal scalings ===

Rigid transformations slide and mirror a graph without changing its shape. What if we multiply the output or the input by a nonzero constant? That stretches or compresses the graph along one axis, producing a non-rigid scaling.

First, place a nonzero multiplier \(a\) OUTSIDE the function: \(y = a \cdot f(x)\). For every input \(x\), the height \(y\) is multiplied directly by \(a\):
- If \(|a| > 1\), points move farther from the \(x\)-axis (vertical stretch by a factor of \(|a|\)).
- If \(0 < |a| < 1\), points move closer to the \(x\)-axis (vertical compression by a factor of \(|a|\)).
- If \(a < 0\), the vertical scaling is accompanied by a reflection across the \(x\)-axis.
- Notice that \(x\)-intercepts \((r, 0)\) stay anchored because \(a \cdot 0 = 0\).

Second, place a nonzero multiplier \(b\) INSIDE the parentheses: \(y = f(bx)\). Just as horizontal shifts behaved opposite to the visible sign, horizontal scaling behaves reciprocally to the visible factor! Apply the input-matching bridge: to feed the original input \(x\) into \(f\) and get the same output \(y\), the new coordinate \(x'\) must satisfy
\[
b x' = x \implies x' = \frac{x}{b}.
\]
Therefore:
- If \(|b| > 1\) (say \(b = 2\)), then \(x' = x/2\): every point moves HALF as far from the \(y\)-axis, causing a horizontal COMPRESSION by a factor of \(\frac{1}{|b|}\).
- If \(0 < |b| < 1\) (say \(b = \frac{1}{3}\)), then \(x' = 3x\): every point moves THREE times as far from the \(y\)-axis, causing a horizontal STRETCH by a factor of \(\frac{1}{|b|}\).
- If \(b < 0\), the horizontal scaling is accompanied by a reflection across the \(y\)-axis.
- The \(y\)-intercept \((0, f(0))\) stays anchored because \(0/b = 0\).

[CALLOUT]
id: scaling-rules
label: Rule
title: Vertical and horizontal scalings
tone: key

Starting from \(y = f(x)\) with a typical point \((x, y)\):

(1) Vertical scaling \(y = a \cdot f(x)\) (\(a \ne 0\)): maps \((x, y) \mapsto (x, a y)\). Stretches vertically if \(|a| > 1\); compresses vertically if \(0 < |a| < 1\); reflects across the \(x\)-axis if \(a < 0\).
(2) Horizontal scaling \(y = f(bx)\) (\(b \ne 0\)): maps \((x, y) \mapsto (x/b, y)\). Compresses horizontally by \(1/|b|\) if \(|b| > 1\); stretches horizontally by \(1/|b|\) if \(0 < |b| < 1\); reflects across the \(y\)-axis if \(b < 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 2: Combining scalings, reflections, and shifts—and the factoring bridge ===

Now put all transformations together into the general affine transformation of any function \(f\):
\[
y = a \cdot f\bigl(b(x - h)\bigr) + k \qquad (a \ne 0,\; b \ne 0).
\]
Notice the crucial factored form inside the parentheses: \(b(x - h)\), NOT \(bx - c\)! Why is factoring \(b\) out of \(bx - c\) indispensable?

Suppose you want to graph \(g(x) = f(2x - 6)\). A very common mistake is to say "compress horizontally by \(1/2\) and shift right by \(6\)." Test that claim using the input-matching bridge! We want the argument of \(f\) to equal \(x\):
\[
2x' - 6 = x \implies 2x' = x + 6 \implies x' = \frac{x}{2} + 3.
\]
Look at the shift term: it is \(+3\), NOT \(+6\)! Rewriting the inside in factored form reveals why immediately:
\[
f(2x - 6) = f\bigl(2(x - 3)\bigr).
\]
Once \(b = 2\) is factored out, you read the true horizontal shift \(h = 3\) directly: compress horizontally by \(1/2\), THEN shift right by \(3\). (Equivalently, if you choose to shift BEFORE scaling, you must shift right by \(6\) FIRST—taking \(x \mapsto x + 6\)—and THEN compress by \(1/2\)—taking \(x + 6 \mapsto \frac{x + 6}{2} = \frac{x}{2} + 3\).)

The same order sensitivity occurs vertically: in \(y = a \cdot f(x) + k\), scaling/reflecting by \(a\) happens FIRST and shifting vertically by \(k\) happens SECOND, taking \(y \mapsto ay \mapsto ay + k\). Doing the vertical shift first would give \(a(y + k) = ay + ak \ne ay + k\).

[CALLOUT]
id: combined-transform-bridge
label: Theorem
title: Combined affine transformation and exact point-mapping rule
tone: key

Given \(y = a \cdot f(bx - c) + k\) with \(a \ne 0\) and \(b \ne 0\), first factor \(b\) out of the argument to write
\[
y = a \cdot f\bigl(b(x - h)\bigr) + k, \qquad \text{where } h = \frac{c}{b}.
\]
(1) Every point \((x, y)\) on \(y = f(x)\) maps to the exact point
\[
(x, y) \mapsto \left(\frac{x}{b} + h,\; ay + k\right).
\]
(2) Standard geometric sequence (scale/reflect before shift on each axis):
   - Horizontally: scale by \(1/|b|\) (and reflect across the \(y\)-axis if \(b < 0\)), then shift horizontally by \(h\).
   - Vertically: scale by \(|a|\) (and reflect across the \(x\)-axis if \(a < 0\)), then shift vertically by \(k\).
   (Horizontal operations and vertical operations are completely independent, so you may perform the horizontal group before or after the vertical group.)
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: combined-transform-bridge | this framework] to sketch
\[
g(x) = -2\sqrt{6 - 3x} + 1.
\]
Step 1 (Factor inside the radical):
\[
6 - 3x = -3(x - 2), \quad \text{so} \quad g(x) = -2\sqrt{-3(x - 2)} + 1.
\]
Here the parent is \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\), with \(a = -2\), \(b = -3\), \(h = 2\), and \(k = 1\).

Step 2 (Apply the point-mapping rule \((x, y) \mapsto \left(\frac{x}{-3} + 2,\; -2y + 1\right)\)):
- Parent anchor \((0, 0) \mapsto (2, 1)\). Check: \(g(2) = -2\sqrt{0} + 1 = 1\).
- Parent anchor \((1, 1) \mapsto \left(\frac{5}{3}, -1\right)\). Check: \(g(5/3) = -2\sqrt{6 - 5} + 1 = -1\).
- Parent anchor \((4, 2) \mapsto \left(\frac{2}{3}, -3\right)\). Check: \(g(2/3) = -2\sqrt{6 - 2} + 1 = -3\).
- Parent anchor \((9, 3) \mapsto (-1, -5)\). Check: \(g(-1) = -2\sqrt{9} + 1 = -5\).

Step 3 (Track domain and range):
- Parent domain \(x \ge 0\) maps under \(x' = -x/3 + 2\) to \(x' \le 2\), so \(\operatorname{Dom}(g) = (-\infty, 2]\).
- Parent range \(y \ge 0\) maps under \(y' = -2y + 1\) to \(y' \le 1\), so \(\operatorname{Range}(g) = (-\infty, 1]\).

=== SECTION 3: When vertical and horizontal scalings coincide—and when they do not ===

Look at the two formulas \(y = (2x)^2\) and \(y = 4x^2\). Algebraically they are identical! On the parent function \(f(x) = x^2\), a horizontal compression by a factor of \(1/2\) produces the exact same parabola as a vertical stretch by a factor of \(4\). Similarly:
- On \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\): \(\sqrt{4x} = 2\sqrt{x}\) (horizontal compression by \(1/4\) equals vertical stretch by \(2\)).
- On \(f(x) = |x|\): \(|3x| = 3|x|\) (horizontal compression by \(1/3\) equals vertical stretch by \(3\)).
- On \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\): \(\frac{1}{2x} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\) (horizontal compression by \(1/2\) equals vertical compression by \(1/2\)).

Because this algebraic coincidence occurs on every pure power, root, absolute-value, and reciprocal parent, it is tempting to think that horizontal and vertical scalings are always interchangeable. That is FALSE for general functions!

Compare \(f(2x)\) and \(a \cdot f(x)\) on a bounded or multi-feature function—for instance, \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}\) (whose peak height is \(1\) and whose domain is \((-\infty, \infty)\)) or an unlabeled polyline with corners at \((-2, 0)\), \((0, 2)\), and \((2, 0)\):
- Vertical scaling \(2f(x)\) doubles the peak height to \(4\) while keeping the \(x\)-intercepts fixed at \(-2\) and \(2\).
- Horizontal scaling \(f(2x)\) leaves the peak height fixed at \(2\) while pulling the \(x\)-intercepts inward to \(-1\) and \(1\).

No vertical stretch \(a \cdot f(x)\) can ever move an \(x\)-intercept, and no horizontal stretch \(f(bx)\) can ever change the global maximum height of a bounded curve! The table below makes this distinction clear.

[TABLE]
Function family | Effect of \(f(bx)\) (\(b > 0\)) | Equivalent to some \(a f(x)\)? | Reason
\(x^2\) | \((bx)^2 = b^2 x^2\) | Yes, \(a = b^2\) | Homogeneous power law
\(\sqrt{x}\) | \(\sqrt{bx} = \sqrt{b}\,\sqrt{x}\) | Yes, \(a = \sqrt{b}\) | Homogeneous root law
\(x^2 - 1\) | \(b^2 x^2 - 1\) | No (unless \(b = 1\)) | \(a(x^2 - 1) = a x^2 - a\) moves the vertex \((0, -1)\)
General unlabeled \(f\) | Scales \(x\)-coordinates by \(1/b\) | No | Vertical scaling fixes \(x\)-intercepts; horizontal scaling fixes peak heights
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 4: Reconstructing formulas and tracking asymptotes ===

When reconstructing a transformed formula from a graph, anchor features tell you the parameters systematically:
1. Identify the parent family from the overall shape (V-shape, parabola, S-curve, half-parabola, hyperbola).
2. Read the shift \((h, k)\) from the key landmark: vertex of a parabola or V-shape, starting point of a square root, inflection center of a cubic, or intersection of the vertical and horizontal asymptotes of a reciprocal curve.
3. Plug one additional clearly marked point \((x_1, y_1)\) on the graph into the template \(y = a \cdot f(x - h) + k\) to solve for the scaling parameter \(a\).

For example, suppose a graph has the shape of a reciprocal curve \(1/x\) with vertical asymptote \(x = 3\), horizontal asymptote \(y = -1\), and passes through \((5, 2)\).
- The asymptotes immediately fix \(h = 3\) and \(k = -1\), giving the template
\[
g(x) = \frac{a}{x - 3} - 1.
\]
- Substituting the point \((5, 2)\):
\[
2 = \frac{a}{5 - 3} - 1 \implies 3 = \frac{a}{2} \implies a = 6.
\]
Thus \(g(x) = \frac{6}{x - 3} - 1\). Its domain is \((-\infty, 3) \cup (3, \infty)\) and its range is \((-\infty, -1) \cup (-1, \infty)\).

What if a rational expression is written with a coefficient on \(x\) in the denominator, such as \(p(x) = \frac{3}{2x - 8} + 5\)? Factor \(2\) from the denominator:
\[
p(x) = \frac{3}{2(x - 4)} + 5 = \frac{3/2}{x - 4} + 5.
\]
Now you read instantly: vertical asymptote \(x = 4\), horizontal asymptote \(y = 5\), and vertical scale factor \(a = 3/2\) (or equivalently horizontal compression by \(1/2\) and vertical stretch by \(3\)).
