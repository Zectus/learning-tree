=== SECTION 1: Constant, identity, and absolute value families ===

Just as an architect recognizes basic structural beams before designing a bridge, a mathematician keeps a small library of elementary parent functions in memory. Almost every curve you will graph, shift, stretch, or invert in this course is built by modifying one of nine core algebraic parent functions. (Exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric families form separate libraries studied in their own dedicated lessons.) Knowing each parent's exact shape, anchor points, domain, range, symmetry, and monotonicity lets you sketch and analyze complex formulas in seconds.

We begin with the three straight-line parent families:
1. Constant function: \(f(x) = c\) (for a fixed real constant \(c\)). Every input outputs the same height \(c\), so the graph is a horizontal line. Its domain is \((-\infty, \infty)\), its range is the single value \(\{c\}\), it is constant on \((-\infty, \infty)\), and it is even because \(f(-x) = c = f(x)\). (Only \(c = 0\) is also odd.)
2. Identity function: \(f(x) = x\). Every input equals its own output, giving the \(45^\circ\) diagonal line through \((-1, -1)\), \((0, 0)\), and \((1, 1)\). Its domain and range are both \((-\infty, \infty)\), it is strictly increasing on \((-\infty, \infty)\), and it is odd because \(f(-x) = -x = -f(x)\).
3. Absolute value function: \(f(x) = |x|\). It measures the distance of \(x\) from \(0\), turning negative inputs positive while leaving nonnegative inputs unchanged. Its graph is a sharp V with vertex at \((0, 0)\) passing through \((-2, 2)\), \((-1, 1)\), \((1, 1)\), and \((2, 2)\). Its domain is \((-\infty, \infty)\), its range is \([0, \infty)\), it is strictly decreasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\), it has a global minimum of \(0\) at \(x = 0\), and it is even because \(|-x| = |x|\).

=== SECTION 2: Power and root families: \(x^2\), \(x^3\), \(\sqrt{x}\), and \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) ===

Next come four curved parent functions built from squaring, cubing, and their matching roots:
1. Squaring function: \(f(x) = x^2\). A U-shaped parabola opening upward through \((-2, 4)\), \((-1, 1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\), and \((2, 4)\). Domain \((-\infty, \infty)\), range \([0, \infty)\), even symmetry, strictly decreasing on \((-\infty, 0)\), strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\), global minimum \(0\) at \(x = 0\). As \(x \to \pm\infty\), \(f(x)\) grows arbitrarily large (written informally as \(f(x) \to \infty\)).
2. Cubing function: \(f(x) = x^3\). An S-shaped curve passing through \((-2, -8)\), \((-1, -1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\), and \((2, 8)\), flattening horizontally as it passes through the origin. Domain \((-\infty, \infty)\), range \((-\infty, \infty)\), odd symmetry, strictly increasing on \((-\infty, \infty)\) with no extrema. As \(x \to \infty\), \(f(x) \to \infty\); as \(x \to -\infty\), \(f(x) \to -\infty\).
3. Square root function: \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\). A half-parabola starting at \((0, 0)\) and rising more and more gently through \((1, 1)\), \((4, 2)\), and \((9, 3)\). Domain \([0, \infty)\), range \([0, \infty)\), neither even nor odd (its domain is not symmetric about \(0\)), strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\), global minimum \(0\) at \(x = 0\), unbounded above as \(x \to \infty\).
4. Cube root function: \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\). Defined for all real numbers because negative numbers have real cube roots; it passes through \((-8, -2)\), \((-1, -1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\), and \((8, 2)\), rising steeply as it crosses the origin. Domain \((-\infty, \infty)\), range \((-\infty, \infty)\), odd symmetry, strictly increasing on \((-\infty, \infty)\), unbounded in both directions.

Look closely at the first quadrant of that plot—especially between \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\) versus beyond \(x = 1\). Many students expect \(x^2\) to be larger than \(x\) and \(\sqrt{x}\) everywhere for \(x > 0\), because squaring "makes numbers bigger." That intuition holds only when \(x > 1\). When \(0 < x < 1\), multiplying by a positive number less than \(1\) makes the number smaller! For example, at \(x = \frac{1}{4}\):
\[
x^2 = \frac{1}{16} = 0.0625, \qquad x = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25, \qquad \sqrt{x} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5.
\]
All three curves intersect at \((0, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\), and their vertical order reverses cleanly at \(x = 1\).

All positive power and root parent curves pass through \((0, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\), but their vertical ranking flips across \(x = 1\):

(1) For \(0 < x < 1\): \(0 < x^3 < x^2 < x < \sqrt{x} < \sqrt[3]{x} < 1\).
(2) For \(x > 1\): \(1 < \sqrt[3]{x} < \sqrt{x} < x < x^2 < x^3\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: The reciprocal families \(1/x\) and \(1/x^2\) ===

The last two core parent functions are the reciprocal function \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\) and the reciprocal-squared function \(g(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}\). Both exclude \(x = 0\) from their domain, so their domains are \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\), splitting each graph into two separate branches.

To see what happens near \(x = 0\) and far out for large \(|x|\), compute a few values of \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\):
- As \(x\) approaches \(0\) from the right (\(x = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001\)), \(f(x) = 10, 100, 1000\) grows without bound toward \(+\infty\).
- As \(x\) approaches \(0\) from the left (\(x = -0.1, -0.01, -0.001\)), \(f(x) = -10, -100, -1000\) plunges toward \(-\infty\).
- As \(|x|\) grows large (\(x = \pm 10, \pm 100, \pm 1000\)), \(f(x) = \pm 0.1, \pm 0.01, \pm 0.001\) gets arbitrarily close to \(0\) without ever reaching \(0\).

For \(g(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}\), squaring makes every output strictly positive: as \(x\) approaches \(0\) from either side, \(g(x)\) shoots upward toward \(+\infty\); as \(|x|\) grows large, \(g(x)\) settles toward \(0\) from above.

[CALLOUT]
id: asymptotes-informal
label: Qualitative concept
title: Vertical and horizontal asymptotes (informal)
tone: key

(1) A vertical line \(x = c\) is a vertical asymptote of \(f\) if the outputs \(|f(x)|\) grow without bound as \(x\) approaches \(c\) from the left, from the right, or from both sides. Both \(1/x\) and \(1/x^2\) have the \(y\)-axis (\(x = 0\)) as a vertical asymptote.
(2) A horizontal line \(y = L\) is a horizontal asymptote of \(f\) if the outputs \(f(x)\) approach \(L\) arbitrarily closely as \(x\) grows large positively or negatively. Both \(1/x\) and \(1/x^2\) have the \(x\)-axis (\(y = 0\)) as a horizontal asymptote.
[/CALLOUT]

Formal limits belong to calculus, so here [REF: asymptotes-informal | asymptotes] serve as exact visual guide lines that frame the curve's extreme behavior. Plot \(1/x\) and \(1/x^2\) side by side to compare their symmetry and signs.

Compare the qualitative profiles of the two reciprocal families:
- \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\): Domain \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\), range \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\), odd symmetry, anchor points \((-1, -1)\) and \((1, 1)\), strictly decreasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and strictly decreasing on \((0, \infty)\), unbounded above and below.
- \(g(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}\): Domain \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\), range \((0, \infty)\), even symmetry, anchor points \((-1, 1)\) and \((1, 1)\), strictly increasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and strictly decreasing on \((0, \infty)\), bounded below by \(0\) (with no global minimum) and unbounded above.

Watch out for a classic phrasing trap with \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\): it is strictly decreasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and strictly decreasing on \((0, \infty)\), NOT strictly decreasing on its entire domain \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\). Indeed, \(-1 < 1\), yet \(f(-1) = -1 < 1 = f(1)\), which goes upward across the break at \(x = 0\).

=== SECTION 4: The complete parent toolkit and identifying families from clues ===

The reference table below gathers all nine core algebraic parent functions in one place. Use it not as a list to memorize mechanically, but as a diagnostic checklist: domain, range, symmetry, monotonicity, and asymptotes uniquely fingerprint every family.

[TABLE]
Parent function | Domain | Range | Symmetry | Monotonicity | Key points / Asymptotes
\(c\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \(\{c\}\) | Even (odd if \(c = 0\)) | Constant on \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \((0, c)\), \((1, c)\)
\(x\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | Odd | Inc. on \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \((-1, -1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\)
\(x^2\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \([0, \infty)\) | Even | Dec. \((-\infty, 0)\); Inc. \((0, \infty)\) | \((-1, 1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\)
\(x^3\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | Odd | Inc. on \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \((-1, -1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\)
\(\sqrt{x}\) | \([0, \infty)\) | \([0, \infty)\) | Neither | Inc. on \((0, \infty)\) | \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\), \((4, 2)\)
\(\sqrt[3]{x}\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | Odd | Inc. on \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \((-1, -1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\), \((8, 2)\)
\(\lvert x \rvert\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | \([0, \infty)\) | Even | Dec. \((-\infty, 0)\); Inc. \((0, \infty)\) | \((-1, 1)\), \((0, 0)\), \((1, 1)\); corner at \((0, 0)\)
\(1/x\) | \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\) | \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\) | Odd | Dec. \((-\infty, 0)\); Dec. \((0, \infty)\) | \((\pm 1, \pm 1)\); VA \(x = 0\), HA \(y = 0\)
\(1/x^2\) | \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\) | \((0, \infty)\) | Even | Inc. \((-\infty, 0)\); Dec. \((0, \infty)\) | \((\pm 1, 1)\); VA \(x = 0\), HA \(y = 0\)
[/TABLE]

How do you identify a parent function from qualitative clues alone? Narrow the candidates step by step:
1. Check the domain. If \(0\) is missing, it is \(1/x\) or \(1/x^2\). If negative numbers are missing, it is \(\sqrt{x}\). Otherwise the domain is \((-\infty, \infty)\).
2. Check the range and symmetry. An even function with range \([0, \infty)\) is either \(x^2\) or \(|x|\); to tell them apart, check whether the average rate of change on \([0, 1]\) equals the average rate of change on \([1, 2]\) (constant slope \(1\) for \(|x|\), versus slopes \(1\) and \(3\) for \(x^2\)).
3. Among the three odd, everywhere-increasing parents (\(x\), \(x^3\), and \(\sqrt[3]{x}\)), use [REF: unit-reversal | growth comparison]: on \((1, \infty)\), \(x^3\) curves steeply upward (average rate of change increases), \(x\) has constant slope \(1\), and \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) bends flatter (average rate of change decreases toward \(0\)).
