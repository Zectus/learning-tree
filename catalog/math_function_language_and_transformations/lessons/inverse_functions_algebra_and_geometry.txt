=== SECTION 1: Defining the inverse function and avoiding the reciprocal trap ===

If a function \(f\) converts Celsius temperature \(x\) to Fahrenheit via \(f(x) = \frac{9}{5}x + 32\), we naturally want the reverse rule that converts Fahrenheit back to Celsius: \(g(y) = \frac{5}{9}(y - 32)\). Feed any temperature through \(f\) and then through \(g\), and you get your original temperature back: \(g(f(x)) = x\). Feed any Fahrenheit reading through \(g\) and then through \(f\), and you get the original reading back: \(f(g(y)) = y\).

As we saw with injectivity, a function \(f\) can be reversed unambiguously into a function if and only if \(f\) is one-to-one—every output \(y \in \operatorname{Range}(f)\) comes from a single unique input \(x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f)\). That reversing function is the inverse function of \(f\), denoted \(f^{-1}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: inverse-def
label: Definition
title: Inverse function and cancellation identities
tone: key

Let \(f\) be a one-to-one function with domain \(D\) and range \(R\). Its inverse function \(f^{-1}\) has domain \(R\) and range \(D\), and is defined by the equivalence
\[
f^{-1}(y) = x \iff f(x) = y \qquad (x \in D,\; y \in R).
\]
Equivalently, \(f^{-1}\) is the unique function on \(R\) satisfying BOTH cancellation identities:

(1) \(f^{-1}(f(x)) = x\) for every \(x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f)\).
(2) \(f(f^{-1}(y)) = y\) for every \(y \in \operatorname{Dom}(f^{-1}) = \operatorname{Range}(f)\).

In particular, domain and range swap completely:
\[
\operatorname{Dom}(f^{-1}) = \operatorname{Range}(f) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \operatorname{Range}(f^{-1}) = \operatorname{Dom}(f).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Before doing a single calculation, pause on the most notorious notation trap in all of precalculus.

[CALLOUT]
id: not-reciprocal
label: Common pitfall
title: \(f^{-1}(x)\) is NOT the reciprocal \(\dfrac{1}{f(x)}\)
tone: caution

In function notation, the superscript \(-1\) on the function name \(f^{-1}\) denotes the inverse function under composition, NEVER the multiplicative reciprocal \(\frac{1}{f(x)}\). When you want the reciprocal of the output value \(f(x)\), write \(\bigl(f(x)\bigr)^{-1}\) or \(\frac{1}{f(x)}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Test [REF: not-reciprocal | that distinction] on \(f(x) = 2x + 3\):
- Its reciprocal is \(\frac{1}{f(x)} = \frac{1}{2x + 3}\).
- Its inverse function is \(f^{-1}(x) = \frac{x - 3}{2}\), because \(f(f^{-1}(x)) = 2\left(\frac{x - 3}{2}\right) + 3 = x\).
At \(x = 5\), \(f^{-1}(5) = 1\) (since \(f(1) = 5\)), whereas \(\frac{1}{f(5)} = \frac{1}{13}\)!

Without any algebraic formula at all, [REF: inverse-def | the equivalence \(f^{-1}(b) = a \iff f(a) = b\)] lets you evaluate \(f^{-1}\) directly from a table or graph. Consider the one-to-one table below:

[TABLE]
\(x\) | \(-2\) | \(0\) | \(1\) | \(4\) | \(7\)
\(f(x)\) | \(5\) | \(2\) | \(-1\) | \(3\) | \(8\)
[/TABLE]

To find \(f^{-1}(3)\), do NOT look for \(x = 3\) in the top row! Look for the output \(3\) in the bottom row \(f(x)\), and read its matching input above it: since \(f(4) = 3\), we have \(f^{-1}(3) = 4\). Similarly, \(f^{-1}(-1) = 1\) and \(f^{-1}(5) = -2\).

=== SECTION 2: Reflection geometry across the line \(y = x\) ===

Look at what [REF: inverse-def | the equivalence \(f^{-1}(b) = a \iff f(a) = b\)] says about points on graphs:
\[
(a, b) \text{ lies on the graph of } f \iff (b, a) \text{ lies on the graph of } f^{-1}.
\]
What geometric transformation swaps the coordinates \((a, b) \mapsto (b, a)\)? Connect \((a, b)\) and \((b, a)\) by a line segment:
1. Its midpoint is \(\left(\frac{a + b}{2}, \frac{a + b}{2}\right)\), which sits directly on the diagonal line \(y = x\).
2. When \(a \ne b\), the slope of the segment joining \((a, b)\) and \((b, a)\) is \(\frac{a - b}{b - a} = -1\), which is perpendicular to the slope \(+1\) of the line \(y = x\).

Therefore, the diagonal line \(y = x\) is the perpendicular bisector of the segment from \((a, b)\) to \((b, a)\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: reflection-yx
label: Geometric property
title: Symmetry across the diagonal \(y = x\)
tone: key

The graph of \(y = f^{-1}(x)\) is the exact mirror reflection of the graph of \(y = f(x)\) across the diagonal line \(y = x\).
[/CALLOUT]

Explore [REF: reflection-yx | this mirror geometry] in the equal-aspect graph below for \(f(x) = x^3 + 1\) and its inverse \(f^{-1}(x) = \sqrt[3]{x - 1}\). Notice how the anchor points \((-1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\), and \((1, 2)\) on \(f\) mirror across \(y = x\) to \((0, -1)\), \((1, 0)\), and \((2, 1)\) on \(f^{-1}\), and how the two curves cross right on \(y = x\) (near \(x \approx -1.325\), where \(f(x) = x\)).

Swapping \(x\) and \(y\) also explains how features transform from \(f\) to \(f^{-1}\):
- Every \(x\)-intercept \((r, 0)\) of \(f\) becomes a \(y\)-intercept \((0, r)\) of \(f^{-1}\), and vice versa.
- Every vertical asymptote \(x = c\) of \(f\) reflects across \(y = x\) into a horizontal asymptote \(y = c\) of \(f^{-1}\), and every horizontal asymptote \(y = L\) of \(f\) becomes a vertical asymptote \(x = L\) of \(f^{-1}\)!

=== SECTION 3: Computing algebraic inverses across core families ===

To find the formula for \(f^{-1}(x)\) algebraically from a one-to-one rule \(y = f(x)\):
1. Record \(\operatorname{Dom}(f)\) and \(\operatorname{Range}(f)\) first—because \(\operatorname{Dom}(f^{-1}) = \operatorname{Range}(f)\) will tell you any required domain restriction on the final formula!
2. Solve the equation \(y = f(x)\) for \(x\) in terms of \(y\).
3. Swap the labels \(x\) and \(y\) so the inverse function is written in standard input variable \(x\) as \(y = f^{-1}(x)\), and attach \(\operatorname{Dom}(f^{-1}) = \operatorname{Range}(f)\).

Let us carry out this procedure on three essential families.

Example 1 (Odd power / odd root). Find the inverse of \(f(x) = 2(x - 3)^3 + 5\).
Here \(\operatorname{Dom}(f) = (-\infty, \infty)\) and \(\operatorname{Range}(f) = (-\infty, \infty)\). Set \(y = 2(x - 3)^3 + 5\) and peel off operations from the outside in:
\[
y - 5 = 2(x - 3)^3 \implies \frac{y - 5}{2} = (x - 3)^3 \implies x - 3 = \sqrt[3]{\frac{y - 5}{2}} \implies x = 3 + \sqrt[3]{\frac{y - 5}{2}}.
\]
Swapping \(x\) and \(y\) gives
\[
f^{-1}(x) = 3 + \sqrt[3]{\frac{x - 5}{2}}, \qquad \operatorname{Dom}(f^{-1}) = (-\infty, \infty).
\]

Example 2 (Shifted square root—carrying the domain restriction!). Find the inverse of \(g(x) = \sqrt{x + 2} - 3\).
Step 1: \(\operatorname{Dom}(g) = [-2, \infty)\). Since \(\sqrt{x + 2} \ge 0\), we have \(\operatorname{Range}(g) = [-3, \infty)\).
Step 2: Set \(y = \sqrt{x + 2} - 3\), isolate the radical, and square:
\[
y + 3 = \sqrt{x + 2} \implies (y + 3)^2 = x + 2 \implies x = (y + 3)^2 - 2.
\]
Step 3: Swap \(x\) and \(y\) AND carry the domain restriction \(\operatorname{Dom}(g^{-1}) = \operatorname{Range}(g) = [-3, \infty)\):
\[
g^{-1}(x) = (x + 3)^2 - 2 \quad \text{for } x \ge -3.
\]
Why is the restriction \(x \ge -3\) mandatory? Without it, \((x + 3)^2 - 2\) is a full parabola that is not even one-to-one and outputs values at \(x = -5\) even though \(-5\) is NOT in \(\operatorname{Range}(g)\)! Check: \(g(g^{-1}(-5)) = g(2) = \sqrt{4} - 3 = -1 \ne -5\), violating [REF: inverse-def.2 | cancellation identity (2)]!

Example 3 (Linear fractional rational function). Find the inverse, domain, and range of
\[
h(x) = \frac{2x + 1}{x - 3}.
\]
Here \(\operatorname{Dom}(h) = (-\infty, 3) \cup (3, \infty)\). Set \(y = \frac{2x + 1}{x - 3}\) and collect all \(x\)-terms on one side:
\[
y(x - 3) = 2x + 1 \implies xy - 3y = 2x + 1 \implies xy - 2x = 3y + 1 \implies x(y - 2) = 3y + 1.
\]
Dividing by \(y - 2\) (valid for \(y \ne 2\)) gives \(x = \frac{3y + 1}{y - 2}\). Swapping \(x\) and \(y\):
\[
h^{-1}(x) = \frac{3x + 1}{x - 2}.
\]
Look at the bonus payoff! We did not even need to guess \(\operatorname{Range}(h)\) beforehand: the natural domain of \(h^{-1}\) is \(x \ne 2\), which immediately reveals \(\operatorname{Range}(h) = \operatorname{Dom}(h^{-1}) = (-\infty, 2) \cup (2, \infty)\), and \(\operatorname{Range}(h^{-1}) = \operatorname{Dom}(h) = (-\infty, 3) \cup (3, \infty)\)!

=== SECTION 4: Verifying inverse pairs by checking both compositions ===

How do you verify with certainty that two given functions \(f\) and \(g\) are inverses of each other? You must check BOTH cancellation identities in [REF: inverse-def | the definition]:
1. \(g(f(x)) = x\) for all \(x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f)\), AND
2. \(f(g(x)) = x\) for all \(x \in \operatorname{Dom}(g)\).

Why is checking ONLY ONE composition not enough? Consider \(f(x) = x^2\) on \((-\infty, \infty)\) and \(g(x) = \sqrt{x}\) on \([0, \infty)\).
- Check \(f(g(x))\): for every \(x \in [0, \infty)\), \(f(g(x)) = (\sqrt{x})^2 = x\)! That first composition passes completely!
- Yet check \(g(f(x))\): for \(x \in (-\infty, \infty)\), \(g(f(x)) = \sqrt{x^2} = |x|\), which fails at \(x = -3\) because \(g(f(-3)) = 3 \ne -3\)!

Checking both compositions catches mismatched domains and missing sign restrictions instantly. Let us verify \(h(x) = \frac{2x + 1}{x - 3}\) (\(x \ne 3\)) and \(h^{-1}(x) = \frac{3x + 1}{x - 2}\) (\(x \ne 2\)) from Section 3:
- For any \(x \ne 3\):
\[
h^{-1}(h(x)) = \frac{3\left(\frac{2x + 1}{x - 3}\right) + 1}{\left(\frac{2x + 1}{x - 3}\right) - 2} = \frac{3(2x + 1) + (x - 3)}{(2x + 1) - 2(x - 3)} = \frac{7x}{7} = x.
\]
- For any \(x \ne 2\):
\[
h(h^{-1}(x)) = \frac{2\left(\frac{3x + 1}{x - 2}\right) + 1}{\left(\frac{3x + 1}{x - 2}\right) - 3} = \frac{2(3x + 1) + (x - 2)}{(3x + 1) - 3(x - 2)} = \frac{7x}{7} = x.
\]
Both compositions simplify to \(x\) on the full domain of the inner function, confirming that \(h\) and \(h^{-1}\) are exact inverses.
