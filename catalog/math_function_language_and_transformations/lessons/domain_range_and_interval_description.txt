=== SECTION 1: Domain, range, and interval notation ===

When a formula or graph defines a function \(y = f(x)\), two questions arise immediately: which inputs \(x\) can legally be fed into \(f\), and which outputs \(y\) actually come out? The set of all valid inputs is the domain of \(f\), and the set of all resulting outputs is the range of \(f\).

For functions of a real variable, domains and ranges are almost always intervals—unbroken stretches of the number line—or combinations of intervals. Writing inequality phrases such as "all real numbers \(x\) with \(-2 \le x < 5\) or \(x > 8\)" repeatedly becomes cumbersome. Interval notation gives a compact, standard way to write these sets using brackets and parentheses.

[CALLOUT]
id: interval-notation
label: Convention
title: Interval notation and union
tone: key

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers. An interval is specified by its left and right endpoints, always written in increasing order from left to right:

(1) A square bracket \([\) or \(]\) means the endpoint is included (\(\le\) or \(\ge\)).
(2) A parenthesis \((\) or \()\) means the endpoint is excluded (\(<\) or \(>\)).
(3) The symbols \(-\infty\) and \(\infty\) denote unbounded extension to the left or right; because they are not real numbers, they always take parentheses, never square brackets.
(4) The union symbol \(\cup\) joins two or more intervals into one combined set: a number lies in \(A \cup B\) if it belongs to \(A\), to \(B\), or to both.
[/CALLOUT]

The table below translates every standard inequality form into interval notation.

[TABLE]
Inequality description | Interval notation | Endpoint inclusion
\(a < x < b\) | \((a, b)\) | Open: neither \(a\) nor \(b\) included
\(a \le x \le b\) | \([a, b]\) | Closed: both \(a\) and \(b\) included
\(a \le x < b\) | \([a, b)\) | Half-open: \(a\) included, \(b\) excluded
\(x \ge a\) | \([a, \infty)\) | Unbounded right, \(a\) included
\(x < b\) | \((-\infty, b)\) | Unbounded left, \(b\) excluded
All real numbers \(x\) | \((-\infty, \infty)\) | Unbounded in both directions
\(x \ne c\) | \((-\infty, c) \cup (c, \infty)\) | All reals except the single point \(c\)
[/TABLE]

Two details deserve care from the start. First, the expression \((2, 5)\) looks identical to the coordinates of a point in the plane, so context tells you whether \((2, 5)\) means a single point or the open interval \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : 2 < x < 5\}\). Second, never write the larger number first: \((5, 2)\) is invalid interval notation, and writing \([a, \infty]\) is always wrong because \(\infty\) cannot be included as a number.

=== SECTION 2: Reading domain and range from graphs ===

When a function is presented as a graph in the \(xy\)-plane, you do not need a formula to read its domain and range:
- To find the domain, scan horizontally from left to right and collect all \(x\)-coordinates that sit directly above or below at least one point on the graph. Geometrically, this is the shadow (projection) of the graph onto the \(x\)-axis.
- To find the range, scan vertically from bottom to top and collect all \(y\)-coordinates that lie directly left or right of at least one point on the graph. Geometrically, this is the shadow of the graph onto the \(y\)-axis.

Endpoints require special attention. A filled (solid) dot at \((a, b)\) means the point is on the graph, so \(a\) belongs to the domain and \(b\) belongs to the range. An open (hollow) circle at \((a, b)\) means the curve approaches \((a, b)\) arbitrarily closely without including that point; therefore \(a\) is excluded from the domain (unless another point on the graph shares the same \(x\)-coordinate—impossible for a function!), and \(b\) is excluded from the range unless the graph reaches height \(y = b\) somewhere else.

Consider the curve \(f(x) = (x - 1)^2 - 2\) restricted to the interval \(-1 \le x < 3\). Its lowest point is the vertex \((1, -2)\), its left endpoint is the included point \((-1, 2)\), and as \(x\) approaches \(3\) from the left the curve rises toward the excluded point \((3, 2)\). In the plot below, the dashed segments show how the graph projects onto the \(x\)-axis (domain) and \(y\)-axis (range). Note that the plotting format draws points with uniform markers, so the excluded endpoint \((3, 2)\) is labeled explicitly as open in the caption.

Look closely at the top of the range in that example. Even though the right endpoint \((3, 2)\) is open, the height \(y = 2\) is actually achieved at the closed left endpoint \((-1, 2)\). Meanwhile, the lowest height is not at either endpoint; it is at the interior vertex \((1, -2)\). Thus the domain is \([-1, 3)\) and the range is \([-2, 2]\).

This illustrates a crucial habit: never assume the range of a graph runs simply between the \(y\)-values of its two endpoints. Always scan for the true lowest and highest points across the entire curve, and check whether any interior gap or open circle removes a \(y\)-value that is not hit elsewhere.

=== SECTION 3: Natural domains of algebraic formulas ===

When a function is given solely by an algebraic formula without a specified domain, we adopt the natural (or implied) domain: the largest set of real numbers \(x\) for which the formula produces a well-defined real number. For elementary algebraic expressions involving polynomials, fractions, and roots, two real-number rules govern every restriction.

[CALLOUT]
id: natural-domain-rules
label: Rule
title: Finding natural domains of elementary algebraic functions
tone: key

Start with all real numbers \((-\infty, \infty)\) and enforce every applicable condition simultaneously:

(1) Nonzero denominators: whenever a formula contains a fraction \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\), require \(Q(x) \ne 0\).
(2) Nonnegative even radicands: whenever a formula contains an even root such as \(\sqrt{A(x)}\) or \(\sqrt[4]{A(x)}\), require \(A(x) \ge 0\).
(3) Unrestricted odd roots: an odd root such as \(\sqrt[3]{A(x)}\) is defined for every real number \(A(x)\) (positive, zero, or negative), so it introduces no restriction of its own.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: natural-domain-rules | these rules] to four progressively richer examples.

Example 1 (Rational function with a factorable quadratic denominator). Find the natural domain of
\[
f(x) = \frac{x + 2}{x^2 - x - 6}.
\]
Factor the denominator: \(x^2 - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2)\). By [REF: natural-domain-rules.1 | the denominator rule], we must exclude both \(x = 3\) and \(x = -2\). Notice that we must NOT cancel the common factor \((x + 2)\) before finding the domain: at \(x = -2\), the original formula produces \(0/0\), which is undefined. Removing \(-2\) and \(3\) from \((-\infty, \infty)\) splits the number line into three intervals:
\[
\operatorname{Dom}(f) = (-\infty, -2) \cup (-2, 3) \cup (3, \infty).
\]

Example 2 (Even root versus odd root in a denominator). Compare
\[
g(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x - 3}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad h(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x - 3}}.
\]
For \(g(x)\), [REF: natural-domain-rules.2 | the even-root rule] requires \(x - 3 \ge 0\), while [REF: natural-domain-rules.1 | the denominator rule] requires \(\sqrt{x - 3} \ne 0\), i.e., \(x - 3 \ne 0\). Combining \(x - 3 \ge 0\) and \(x - 3 \ne 0\) leaves the strict inequality \(x - 3 > 0\), so \(x > 3\), giving \(\operatorname{Dom}(g) = (3, \infty)\).
For \(h(x)\), the cube root imposes no sign restriction by [REF: natural-domain-rules.3 | the odd-root rule]; only division by zero is forbidden, so \(x - 3 \ne 0\), giving \(\operatorname{Dom}(h) = (-\infty, 3) \cup (3, \infty)\).

Example 3 (Even root in numerator plus a separate denominator). Find the natural domain of
\[
k(x) = \frac{\sqrt{5 - x}}{x - 1}.
\]
We need both conditions at once:
1. From the square root: \(5 - x \ge 0\), which gives \(x \le 5\), i.e., \((-\infty, 5]\).
2. From the denominator: \(x - 1 \ne 0\), which removes \(x = 1\).

Since \(1\) lies inside \((-\infty, 5]\), removing \(x = 1\) splits the interval into
\[
\operatorname{Dom}(k) = (-\infty, 1) \cup (1, 5].
\]
What if the denominator had been \(x - 7\) instead of \(x - 1\)? Since \(7\) is already outside \((-\infty, 5]\), the condition \(x \ne 7\) would remove nothing further, and the domain would simply remain \((-\infty, 5]\).

Example 4 (Even root of a factorable quadratic). Find the natural domain of
\[
m(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 9}.
\]
We require \(x^2 - 9 \ge 0\), or \((x - 3)(x + 3) \ge 0\). The boundary points where \(x^2 - 9 = 0\) are \(x = -3\) and \(x = 3\), which divide the number line into \((-\infty, -3)\), \((-3, 3)\), and \((3, \infty)\). Testing one value in each region—\(x = -4\) gives \((-4)^2 - 9 = 7 > 0\), \(x = 0\) gives \(-9 < 0\), and \(x = 4\) gives \(7 > 0\)—shows that \(x^2 - 9 \ge 0\) holds on \((-\infty, -3] \cup [3, \infty)\).

=== SECTION 4: Finding ranges and contextual domains ===

Determining the range from a formula is often trickier than finding the domain because you must identify which output values \(y\) can actually be reached. At this level, four elementary shapes account for almost every range problem:

1. Non-constant linear functions \(f(x) = mx + b\) (\(m \ne 0\)): For any target \(y\), solving \(mx + b = y\) gives \(x = (y - b)/m\), which always exists. So the range is \((-\infty, \infty)\). (A constant function \(f(x) = c\) has range \(\{c\}\), a single number.)
2. Quadratic functions \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\) (\(a \ne 0\)): The graph is a parabola whose vertex sits at \(x_v = -\frac{b}{2a}\) with height \(y_v = f(x_v)\). Completing the square or writing \(f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k\) shows why: since \((x - h)^2 \ge 0\) takes every value in \([0, \infty)\),
   - if \(a > 0\), the parabola opens upward and the range is \([k, \infty)\);
   - if \(a < 0\), the parabola opens downward and the range is \((-\infty, k]\).
3. Simple square-root expressions \(f(x) = c + d\sqrt{x - h}\) (\(d \ne 0\)): Since \(\sqrt{x - h}\) takes every value in \([0, \infty)\) as \(x\) runs over \([h, \infty)\), multiplying by \(d\) and adding \(c\) gives range \([c, \infty)\) when \(d > 0\) and \((-\infty, c]\) when \(d < 0\).
4. Simple reciprocal expressions \(f(x) = \frac{a}{x - h} + k\) (\(a \ne 0\)): The fraction \(\frac{a}{x - h}\) can equal any nonzero real number \(t\) (just set \(x = h + a/t\)), but can never equal \(0\). Hence \(f(x)\) can take every real value except \(k\), so the range is \((-\infty, k) \cup (k, \infty)\).

For example, consider the downward-opening quadratic \(q(x) = -2x^2 + 8x - 3\). Its vertex occurs at
\[
x_v = -\frac{8}{2(-2)} = 2, \qquad y_v = q(2) = -2(4) + 8(2) - 3 = 5.
\]
Equivalently, \(q(x) = -2(x - 2)^2 + 5\). Since \(-2(x - 2)^2 \le 0\) for all real \(x\), we have \(q(x) \le 5\), with equality at \(x = 2\). Thus \(\operatorname{Range}(q) = (-\infty, 5]\).

In applied problems, physical meaning imposes extra domain restrictions beyond the algebraic formula. Suppose you have \(40\text{ m}\) of fencing to enclose a rectangular garden against a straight brick wall, so fencing is needed only on the two sides of length \(x\) perpendicular to the wall and the one side of length \(40 - 2x\) parallel to it. The area function is
\[
A(x) = x(40 - 2x) = -2x^2 + 40x.
\]
As an abstract polynomial, \(A(x)\) has natural domain \((-\infty, \infty)\). Physically, however, both side lengths must be positive: \(x > 0\) and \(40 - 2x > 0\) (so \(x < 20\)). The practical domain is therefore \((0, 20)\). On this restricted domain, the vertex sits at \(x_v = -\frac{40}{2(-2)} = 10 \in (0, 20)\), giving maximum area \(A(10) = 10(20) = 200\text{ m}^2\). As \(x\) approaches \(0\) or \(20\), the area approaches \(0\) without reaching it, so the physical range is \((0, 200]\).
