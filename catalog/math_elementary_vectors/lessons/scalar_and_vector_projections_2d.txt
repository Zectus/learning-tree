=== SECTION 1: Scalar projection along an arbitrary direction ===

Resolving a vector \(\mathbf{u}\) along the horizontal and vertical axes gave us \(u_x = \|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta\) and \(u_y = \|\mathbf{u}\|\sin\theta\). Now suppose the natural direction of a problem is NOT horizontal or vertical—for example, a tilted ramp, a straight road, or a second vector \(\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}\). How much of \(\mathbf{u}\) points along the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\), and how do we split \(\mathbf{u}\) cleanly into one piece parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\) and one piece perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\)?

Place \(\mathbf{u}\) and the nonzero reference vector \(\mathbf{v}\) tail-to-tail at the origin, with included angle \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\), and drop a perpendicular from the head of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto the infinite line spanned by \(\mathbf{v}\). Think of shining a light straight down at \(90^\circ\) to the line of \(\mathbf{v}\): \(\mathbf{u}\) casts a shadow along that line.
- When \(0 \le \theta < 90^\circ\), the shadow points in the same direction as \(\mathbf{v}\), and by right-triangle trigonometry its length is \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta > 0\).
- When \(\theta = 90^\circ\), \(\mathbf{u}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\), so the shadow collapses to a point of length \(0 = \|\mathbf{u}\|\cos 90^\circ\).
- When \(90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ\), the shadow points in the OPPOSITE direction to \(\mathbf{v}\), with length \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos(180^\circ - \theta) = -\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta\), so the signed shadow coordinate along \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta < 0\).

In every case, the signed scalar projection (also called the component of \(\mathbf{u}\) along \(\mathbf{v}\)) is \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta\). To compute it directly from components without ever finding \(\theta\), substitute \(\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}\) from the dot product formula:
\[
\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta = \|\mathbf{u}\|\left(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}\right) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \left(\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\right) = \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}},
\]
where \(\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\) is the unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\). Notice that if \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\), \(\frac{\mathbf{0} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|} = 0\), so the formula works for all \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) as long as \(\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: scalar-proj
label: Definition
title: Scalar projection (component of \(\mathbf{u}\) along \(\mathbf{v}\))
tone: key

For any vector \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) and any nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2\), the scalar projection of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto \(\mathbf{v}\) is the signed real number
\[
\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}}.
\]
Its sign tells the orientation of the shadow: positive if \(\mathbf{u}\) leans with \(\mathbf{v}\) (acute angle), zero if \(\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}\), and negative if \(\mathbf{u}\) leans against \(\mathbf{v}\) (obtuse angle).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: scalar-proj | scalar projection] generalizes ordinary rectangular coordinates: taking \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{i}\) gives \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{i}} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{i} = u_x\), and taking \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{j}\) gives \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{j}} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{j} = u_y\).

=== SECTION 2: Vector projection and avoiding the two classic pitfalls ===

The scalar projection \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\) is only a signed number. To build the actual shadow vector—the vector projection of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto \(\mathbf{v}\), written \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\)—we multiply that signed scalar by the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\) pointing along \(\mathbf{v}\):
\[
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = (\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u})\,\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\right)\left(\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\right) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^2}\,\mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{v}.
\]
Notice how clean that last form is: you do not even need a square root to compute \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\), because \(\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = v_x^2 + v_y^2\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: vector-proj
label: Formula
title: Vector projection of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto \(\mathbf{v}\)
tone: key

For any \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) and any nonzero \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2\), the vector projection of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto \(\mathbf{v}\) is
\[
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\right)\mathbf{v} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^2}\right)\mathbf{v}.
\]
The vector \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\) always lies along the line spanned by \(\mathbf{v}\), and its length is \(|\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}|\).
[/CALLOUT]

[CALLOUT]
id: proj-pitfalls
label: Common pitfall
title: Two mistakes to guard against in vector projections
tone: caution

(1) Dividing by \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) instead of \(\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}\): geometrically, doubling \(\mathbf{v}\) to \(2\mathbf{v}\) does not change the line of \(\mathbf{v}\), so the shadow vector \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\) MUST stay unchanged. In \(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v}\), replacing \(\mathbf{v}\) by \(2\mathbf{v}\) multiplies the numerator by \(2 \cdot 2 = 4\) and the denominator by \(2 \cdot 2 = 4\), canceling completely. If you accidentally write \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) in the denominator, \(\mathbf{v}\) is NOT normalized, and the scale invariance breaks!
(2) Swapping \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\): \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\) is a multiple of the SUBSCRIPT vector \(\mathbf{v}\) (it sits ON the line of \(\mathbf{v}\)), whereas \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}} \mathbf{v}\) is a multiple of \(\mathbf{u}\) (it sits ON the line of \(\mathbf{u}\)). In general, \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} \ne \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}} \mathbf{v}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us run a concrete calculation for \(\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, 5 \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 2 \rangle\):
- Dot product: \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (2)(4) + (5)(2) = 18\).
- Denominators: \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 4^2 + 2^2 = 20\), so \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}\).
- Scalar projection: \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \frac{18}{2\sqrt{5}} = \frac{9}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{9\sqrt{5}}{5}\).
- Vector projection: \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \frac{18}{20}\langle 4, 2 \rangle = \frac{9}{10}\langle 4, 2 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{18}{5}, \frac{9}{5} \right\rangle\).

=== SECTION 3: Orthogonal decomposition and the Pythagorean check ===

Look back at the dashed orange segment in the graph above: it runs from the head of \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{u}\). By tail-to-tail subtraction, that vector is
\[
\mathbf{u}_\perp = \mathbf{u} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}.
\]
Adding \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel\) and \(\mathbf{u}_\perp\) gives \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel + \mathbf{u}_\perp = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{u} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{u}\) automatically. Now let us prove algebraically that \(\mathbf{u}_\perp\) really is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\) (and therefore to \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel\)):
\[
\mathbf{u}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{v} = \left(\mathbf{u} - \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{v}\right) \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} - \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0.
\]
Because \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel\) is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{v}\), we immediately obtain \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel \cdot \mathbf{u}_\perp = 0\) as well.

This splitting \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_\parallel + \mathbf{u}_\perp\) is also unique: if \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}\) for ANY vector \(\mathbf{a} = c\mathbf{v}\) parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\) and ANY vector \(\mathbf{b}\) orthogonal to \(\mathbf{v}\), dotting both sides with \(\mathbf{v}\) kills \(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\) and leaves \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v})\), uniquely forcing \(c = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\) and hence \(\mathbf{a} = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{u} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\).

Finally, because \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel \cdot \mathbf{u}_\perp = 0\), expanding \(\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 = (\mathbf{u}_\parallel + \mathbf{u}_\perp) \cdot (\mathbf{u}_\parallel + \mathbf{u}_\perp)\) eliminates the cross term and yields the Pythagorean identity
\[
\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}_\parallel\|^2 + \|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|^2.
\]
This gives both a fast way to find \(\|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|\) from \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\) and \(|\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}|\), and an instant check on every decomposition calculation.

[CALLOUT]
id: orthog-decomp
label: Theorem
title: Orthogonal decomposition relative to a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\)
tone: key

Every vector \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) decomposes uniquely as \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_\parallel + \mathbf{u}_\perp\), where:

(1) \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\right)\mathbf{v}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\) (or \(\mathbf{0}\)).
(2) \(\mathbf{u}_\perp = \mathbf{u} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{v}\) (\(\mathbf{u}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\) and \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel \cdot \mathbf{u}_\perp = 0\)).
(3) Pythagorean relation: \(\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}_\parallel\|^2 + \|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|^2\).
[/CALLOUT]

Check our earlier example \(\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, 5 \rangle\), \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 2 \rangle\), where \(\mathbf{u}_\parallel = \langle \frac{18}{5}, \frac{9}{5} \rangle\):
\[
\mathbf{u}_\perp = \langle 2, 5 \rangle - \left\langle \frac{18}{5}, \frac{9}{5} \right\rangle = \left\langle -\frac{8}{5}, \frac{16}{5} \right\rangle.
\]
Verify orthogonality: \(\mathbf{u}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{v} = -\frac{32}{5} + \frac{32}{5} = 0\). Verify Pythagoras: \(\|\mathbf{u}_\parallel\|^2 + \|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|^2 = \frac{324 + 81}{25} + \frac{64 + 256}{25} = \frac{405 + 320}{25} = \frac{725}{25} = 29 = 2^2 + 5^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\).
