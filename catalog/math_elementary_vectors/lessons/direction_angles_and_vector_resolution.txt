=== SECTION 1: Direction angles and resolving vectors into components ===

In real-world geometry and physics, vectors rarely arrive pre-packaged as coordinate pairs \(\langle v_x, v_y \rangle\). An air-traffic controller describes a wind as \(40\text{ km/h}\) blowing northeast; a mechanical drawing specifies a brace pulling with \(200\text{ N}\) at \(35^\circ\) above horizontal. Component arithmetic is still the cleanest way to add such vectors, so we need a reliable two-way bridge between magnitude–direction form and rectangular component form \(\langle v_x, v_y \rangle\).

Place any nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) in standard position with its tail at the origin \(O(0, 0)\). Its standard direction angle \(\theta\) is the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis to \(\mathbf{v}\), taken by convention in the interval \(0^\circ \le \theta < 360^\circ\) (or equivalently \(0 \le \theta < 2\pi\) in radians).

Start with the special case of a unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\) pointing at standard direction angle \(\theta\). Because \(\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}\| = 1\), its head lies on the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) at angle \(\theta\) from the positive \(x\)-axis. By the unit-circle definitions of cosine and sine, the coordinates of that point are \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), which gives the unit direction vector
\[
\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \langle \cos\theta,\; \sin\theta \rangle.
\]
Check its length: \(\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}\| = \sqrt{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta} = \sqrt{1} = 1\) for every angle \(\theta\).

Now take any nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) with magnitude \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) and standard direction angle \(\theta\). Since \(\mathbf{v}\) has length \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) in the same direction as the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \langle \cos\theta, \sin\theta \rangle\), scaling \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\) by \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) gives \(\mathbf{v}\) immediately:
\[
\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{v}\|\,\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \|\mathbf{v}\|\langle \cos\theta,\; \sin\theta \rangle = \langle \|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta,\; \|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta \rangle.
\]
Splitting \(\mathbf{v}\) into its horizontal component \(v_x = \|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta\) and vertical component \(v_y = \|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta\) is called resolving \(\mathbf{v}\) into rectangular components. Because cosine and sine carry the proper plus and minus signs in all four quadrants automatically, this single formula works for every \(\theta \in [0^\circ, 360^\circ)\).

[CALLOUT]
id: polar-to-rect
label: Formula
title: Magnitude–direction form to component form (vector resolution)
tone: key

If a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) has magnitude \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) and standard direction angle \(\theta \in [0^\circ, 360^\circ)\) measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, then:

(1) The unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \langle \cos\theta, \sin\theta \rangle\).
(2) The component form of \(\mathbf{v}\) is
\[
\mathbf{v} = \langle \|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta,\; \|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta \rangle = (\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta)\mathbf{i} + (\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta)\mathbf{j}.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

\]
Both components come out negative, matching Quadrant III.

=== SECTION 2: Converting components back to magnitude and direction angle ===

Now reverse the problem: given a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v} = \langle v_x, v_y \rangle\), how do we recover its magnitude \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) and standard direction angle \(\theta \in [0^\circ, 360^\circ)\)?

The magnitude is immediate: \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\). For the angle \(\theta\), dividing \(v_y = \|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta\) by \(v_x = \|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta\) (when \(v_x \ne 0\)) cancels \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) and yields
\[
\tan\theta = \frac{v_y}{v_x}.
\]
Here is where countless errors happen if you reach blindly for \(\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right)\). The inverse tangent function \(\arctan\) only ever outputs angles in the open interval \((-90^\circ, 90^\circ)\)—that is, Quadrants I and IV! Yet two vectors pointing in opposite directions have the exact same ratio \(\frac{v_y}{v_x}\). For example, \(\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, 1 \rangle\) (Quadrant I, \(\theta = 45^\circ\)) and \(\mathbf{w} = \langle -1, -1 \rangle\) (Quadrant III, \(\theta = 225^\circ\)) both satisfy \(\frac{v_y}{v_x} = 1\), so \(\arctan(1) = 45^\circ\) gives the wrong answer for \(\mathbf{w}\) by a full \(180^\circ\).

To get the true direction angle \(\theta \in [0^\circ, 360^\circ)\) every time, you MUST inspect the signs of \(v_x\) and \(v_y\) to identify the quadrant (or axis) before finalizing \(\theta\).

[CALLOUT]
id: rect-to-polar
label: Method
title: Component form to magnitude and standard direction angle
tone: key

Given a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v} = \langle v_x, v_y \rangle\):

(1) Compute the magnitude \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\).
(2) If \(v_x = 0\), read \(\theta\) directly from the vertical axis: \(\theta = 90^\circ\) if \(v_y > 0\), and \(\theta = 270^\circ\) if \(v_y < 0\).
(3) If \(v_y = 0\), read \(\theta\) directly from the horizontal axis: \(\theta = 0^\circ\) if \(v_x > 0\), and \(\theta = 180^\circ\) if \(v_x < 0\).
(4) Otherwise, compute \(\alpha = \arctan\left(\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right) \in (-90^\circ, 90^\circ)\) and adjust by quadrant:
- Quadrant I (\(v_x > 0, v_y > 0\)): \(\theta = \alpha\).
- Quadrant II (\(v_x < 0, v_y > 0\)) or Quadrant III (\(v_x < 0, v_y < 0\)): \(\theta = \alpha + 180^\circ\).
- Quadrant IV (\(v_x > 0, v_y < 0\)): \(\theta = \alpha + 360^\circ\).
[/CALLOUT]

[CALLOUT]
id: arctan-trap
label: Common pitfall
title: Never trust \(\arctan(v_y / v_x)\) without checking the quadrant
tone: caution

Whenever \(v_x < 0\) (Quadrants II and III), \(\arctan(v_y / v_x)\) points \(180^\circ\) away from the actual vector—you must add \(180^\circ\). Equivalently, you can compute the acute reference angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}} = \arctan\left(\frac{|v_y|}{|v_x|}\right)\) and place it in the right quadrant (\(180^\circ - \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}\) in Q II, \(180^\circ + \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}\) in Q III, \(360^\circ - \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}\) in Q IV).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Compass bearings and adding angled vectors ===

Navigation, aviation, and surveying use a different angle convention from standard mathematics. With East aligned along the positive \(x\)-axis and North along the positive \(y\)-axis, a compass bearing (or heading) is measured clockwise starting from due North:
- Due North is \(000^\circ\) (standard angle \(\theta = 90^\circ\)).
- Due East is \(090^\circ\) (standard angle \(\theta = 0^\circ\)).
- Due South is \(180^\circ\) (standard angle \(\theta = 270^\circ\)).
- Due West is \(270^\circ\) (standard angle \(\theta = 180^\circ\)).

Bearings are also written in quadrant form such as \(\text{N } 30^\circ \text{ E}\) (start at North and turn \(30^\circ\) toward East, which is \(030^\circ\) clockwise from North and \(\theta = 60^\circ\) in standard position) or \(\text{S } 40^\circ \text{ W}\) (start at South and turn \(40^\circ\) toward West, which is \(220^\circ\) clockwise from North and \(\theta = 230^\circ\) in standard position).

In general, if a vector has three-figure compass bearing \(\beta\) measured clockwise from North, its standard mathematical angle is
\[
\theta = 90^\circ - \beta \qquad (\text{adding } 360^\circ \text{ if negative to land in } [0^\circ, 360^\circ)).
\]
Using the cofunction identities \(\cos(90^\circ - \beta) = \sin\beta\) and \(\sin(90^\circ - \beta) = \cos\beta\), you can either convert \(\beta\) to \(\theta\) first and apply [REF: polar-to-rect | component resolution], or read directly that \(v_x = \|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\beta\) and \(v_y = \|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\beta\). Converting to a quick sketch with standard angle \(\theta\) is the safest way to avoid swapping sine and cosine.

With both conversions secured, we can add any collection of angled vectors cleanly by a three-step workflow:
1. Resolve each vector into rectangular components \((x, y)\) using [REF: polar-to-rect | vector resolution].
2. Add all \(x\)-components together to get \(R_x\), and add all \(y\)-components together to get \(R_y\).
3. If needed, convert the resultant \(\mathbf{R} = \langle R_x, R_y \rangle\) back to magnitude and direction using [REF: rect-to-polar | the inverse conversion].

Let us work through a concrete example: add vector \(\mathbf{a}\) of magnitude \(4\) at standard angle \(30^\circ\) to vector \(\mathbf{b}\) of magnitude \(3\) at standard angle \(120^\circ\).
Step 1: Resolve both vectors:
\[
\mathbf{a} = 4\langle \cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ \rangle = \langle 2\sqrt{3},\; 2 \rangle \approx \langle 3.464,\; 2.000 \rangle,
\]
\[
\mathbf{b} = 3\langle \cos 120^\circ, \sin 120^\circ \rangle = \left\langle -\frac{3}{2},\; \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \right\rangle \approx \langle -1.500,\; 2.598 \rangle.
\]
Step 2: Sum components:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \left\langle 2\sqrt{3} - \frac{3}{2},\; 2 + \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \right\rangle \approx \langle 1.964,\; 4.598 \rangle.
\]
Step 3: Recover magnitude and direction. Expanding the squares gives
\[
\|\mathbf{R}\|^2 = \left(2\sqrt{3} - \frac{3}{2}\right)^2 + \left(2 + \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = \left(12 - 6\sqrt{3} + \frac{9}{4}\right) + \left(4 + 6\sqrt{3} + \frac{27}{4}\right) = 16 + 9 = 25,
\]
so \(\|\mathbf{R}\| = 5\). (Notice why: \(120^\circ - 30^\circ = 90^\circ\), so \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) form a \(3\text{–}4\text{–}5\) right triangle!) Since \(R_x > 0\) and \(R_y > 0\) (Quadrant I), \(\theta_R = \arctan\left(\frac{4.598}{1.964}\right) \approx 66.9^\circ\).
