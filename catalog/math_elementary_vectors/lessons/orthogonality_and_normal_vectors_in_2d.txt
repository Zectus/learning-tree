=== SECTION 1: Orthogonality and 90-degree rotations in the plane ===

In the previous lesson, we saw that two nonzero vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) meet at a \(90^\circ\) angle if and only if \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\). Because \(\mathbf{0} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\) for every vector \(\mathbf{v}\) as well, mathematicians adopt one uniform algebraic word that covers both cases cleanly: we say two vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) are orthogonal (written \(\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}\)) whenever \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\). For nonzero vectors, "orthogonal" and "perpendicular" mean the exact same thing; by convention, the zero vector \(\mathbf{0}\) is orthogonal to every vector even though it has no geometric direction.

Now ask a practical construction question: given a single nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b \rangle\), how do we immediately write down a nonzero vector perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\)? We need \(\langle x, y \rangle\) satisfying \(ax + by = 0\). Look at the two simplest ways to make the products cancel identically:
\[
a(-b) + b(a) = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad a(b) + b(-a) = 0.
\]
Swapping the two components and negating one of them produces two orthogonal vectors of the exact same length \(\sqrt{(-b)^2 + a^2} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \|\mathbf{v}\|\):
- \(\langle -b, a \rangle\) is \(\mathbf{v}\) rotated \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise. (Check on \(\mathbf{i} = \langle 1, 0 \rangle\): swapping and negating the first gives \(\langle 0, 1 \rangle = \mathbf{j}\), a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise turn!)
- \(\langle b, -a \rangle = -\langle -b, a \rangle\) is \(\mathbf{v}\) rotated \(90^\circ\) clockwise.

[CALLOUT]
id: orthog-2d
label: Rule
title: Orthogonality test and 2D perpendicular construction
tone: key

Let \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2\).

(1) Orthogonality test: \(\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}\) if and only if \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_x v_x + u_y v_y = 0\).
(2) \(90^\circ\) rotations: for any nonzero \(\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b \rangle\), the vector \(\langle -b, a \rangle\) is obtained by rotating \(\mathbf{v}\) counterclockwise by \(90^\circ\), and \(\langle b, -a \rangle\) by rotating \(\mathbf{v}\) clockwise by \(90^\circ\). Every vector orthogonal to \(\mathbf{v}\) is a scalar multiple \(k\langle -b, a \rangle\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: orthog-2d | this vector rule] generalizes the classical negative-reciprocal slope rule from coordinate geometry. When \(a \ne 0\) and \(b \ne 0\), the slope along \(\langle a, b \rangle\) is \(m_1 = \frac{b}{a}\) and the slope along \(\langle -b, a \rangle\) is \(m_2 = \frac{a}{-b} = -\frac{a}{b}\), so \(m_1 m_2 = -1\). Yet the vector rule never breaks on horizontal or vertical lines: for the horizontal vector \(\langle 4, 0 \rangle\), [REF: orthog-2d.2 | the rotation rule] immediately produces the vertical vectors \(\langle 0, 4 \rangle\) and \(\langle 0, -4 \rangle\) with no division by zero.

=== SECTION 2: Normal vectors and dual forms of a 2D line ===

Earlier we described a line \(L\) by specifying a base point \(P_0(x_0, y_0)\) and a direction vector \(\mathbf{d}\) pointing ALONG the line, giving the parametric form \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{d}\). In \(\mathbb{R}^2\), there is a second, equally powerful way to pin down a line: specify one base point \(P_0\) and one nonzero normal vector \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b \rangle\) pointing PERPENDICULAR to the line.

Which points \(P(x, y)\) (with position vector \(\mathbf{r} = \langle x, y \rangle\)) lie on the line \(L\) through \(P_0\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{n}\)? Precisely those points for which the displacement \(\overrightarrow{P_0 P} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{n}\)! Using [REF: orthog-2d.1 | the orthogonality test], that single geometric condition becomes the point-normal equation of \(L\):
\[
\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0) = 0.
\]
Now expand that dot product in components, with \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x - x_0, y - y_0 \rangle\):
\[
a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) = 0 \iff ax + by = ax_0 + by_0.
\]
Setting the constant \(c = ax_0 + by_0 = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{r}_0\), we recover the familiar Cartesian equation \(ax + by = c\)—and we just uncovered a huge geometric insight! The coefficients \(a\) and \(b\) sitting in front of \(x\) and \(y\) in \(ax + by = c\) are literally the components of a normal vector \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b \rangle\) perpendicular to the line!

[CALLOUT]
id: normal-line
label: Theorem
title: Point-normal and Cartesian forms of a 2D line
tone: key

Let \(L\) be a line in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) passing through \(P_0(x_0, y_0)\) (\(\mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x_0, y_0 \rangle\)) with nonzero normal vector \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b \rangle\).

(1) Point-normal form: \(\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0) = 0\).
(2) Cartesian (standard) form: \(ax + by = c\), where \(c = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{r}_0 = ax_0 + by_0\).
(3) Reading geometry off \(ax + by = c\): \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b \rangle\) is perpendicular to \(L\), and \(\mathbf{d} = \langle -b, a \rangle\) (or \(\langle b, -a \rangle\)) is parallel to \(L\).
[/CALLOUT]

Putting [REF: orthog-2d.2 | the 90-degree rotation rule] together with [REF: normal-line | normal form] makes switching between parametric and Cartesian descriptions effortless:
- Parametric \(\to\) Cartesian: given \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{d}\), rotate \(\mathbf{d} = \langle d_x, d_y \rangle\) by \(90^\circ\) to get a normal \(\mathbf{n} = \langle -d_y, d_x \rangle\) (or \(\langle d_y, -d_x \rangle\)), then evaluate \(c = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{r}_0\) to write \(ax + by = c\).
- Cartesian \(\to\) Parametric: given \(ax + by = c\), pick any convenient point \(P_0(x_0, y_0)\) solving \(ax_0 + by_0 = c\) (such as an intercept), and rotate \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b \rangle\) by \(90^\circ\) to get a direction vector \(\mathbf{d} = \langle -b, a \rangle\), giving \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x_0, y_0 \rangle + t\langle -b, a \rangle\).

For example, take the Cartesian line \(2x + 3y = 12\). Reading the coefficients immediately gives the normal vector \(\mathbf{n} = \langle 2, 3 \rangle\) and a direction vector \(\mathbf{d} = \langle -3, 2 \rangle\). Since \(P_0(3, 2)\) satisfies \(2(3) + 3(2) = 12\), a parametric form is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3, 2 \rangle + t\langle -3, 2 \rangle\), illustrated below.

=== SECTION 3: Acute angles between lines and right-angle vector proofs ===

When two lines \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) intersect in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), they create two pairs of vertical angles: an acute angle \(\phi \in (0^\circ, 90^\circ]\) and its supplementary obtuse angle \(180^\circ - \phi\). By convention, the angle between two lines means the acute (or right) angle \(\phi \in [0^\circ, 90^\circ]\).

Suppose \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) have direction vectors \(\mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2\) and normal vectors \(\mathbf{n}_1, \mathbf{n}_2\). Depending on which way we orient \(\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(\mathbf{d}_2\) along their lines, \(\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2\) can be positive (giving \(\phi\)) or negative (giving \(180^\circ - \phi\)). Taking the absolute value removes the orientation choice and always selects \(\cos\phi \ge 0\), i.e., \(\phi \in [0^\circ, 90^\circ]\). Moreover, rotating both lines by \(90^\circ\) turns \(\mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2\) into \(\mathbf{n}_1, \mathbf{n}_2\) without altering the angle between them, so we can use whichever pair of vectors is already at hand:
\[
\cos\phi = \frac{|\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2|}{\|\mathbf{d}_1\|\,\|\mathbf{d}_2\|} = \frac{|\mathbf{n}_1 \cdot \mathbf{n}_2|}{\|\mathbf{n}_1\|\,\|\mathbf{n}_2\|}, \qquad \phi \in [0^\circ, 90^\circ].
\]
In particular, two lines \(a_1 x + b_1 y = c_1\) and \(a_2 x + b_2 y = c_2\) are perpendicular if and only if \(\mathbf{n}_1 \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 = a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 = 0\), and parallel (or identical) if and only if \(a_1 b_2 - b_1 a_2 = 0\).

Finally, combining position vectors with [REF: orthog-2d.1 | the orthogonality test] turns classical right-angle theorems of Euclidean geometry into two-line algebraic identities.

Worked Proof 1 (Thales' theorem): An angle inscribed in a semicircle is always a right angle.
Place the origin \(O\) at the center of a circle of radius \(R\). Let \(A\) and \(B\) be endpoints of a diameter, with position vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(-\mathbf{a}\) (since \(O\) is the midpoint of \(AB\)), and let \(P\) be any other point on the circle with position vector \(\mathbf{p}\). Because \(A\) and \(P\) both lie on the circle of radius \(R\), we have \(\|\mathbf{p}\| = \|\mathbf{a}\| = R\). Now express the two sides of \(\angle APB\) as \(\overrightarrow{AP} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{a}\) and \(\overrightarrow{BP} = \mathbf{p} - (-\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{a}\), and take their dot product:
\[
\overrightarrow{AP} \cdot \overrightarrow{BP} = (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{a}) \cdot (\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = \|\mathbf{p}\|^2 - \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 = R^2 - R^2 = 0.
\]
Since \(\overrightarrow{AP}\) and \(\overrightarrow{BP}\) are nonzero and their dot product is \(0\), they are perpendicular: \(\angle APB = 90^\circ\)!

Worked Proof 2 (Diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular):
Let \(OACB\) be a rhombus with one vertex at the origin \(O\) and adjacent sides \(\mathbf{u} = \overrightarrow{OA}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \overrightarrow{OB}\). By definition of a rhombus, all four sides have equal length, so \(\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\|\). The two diagonals are \(\overrightarrow{OC} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) and \(\overrightarrow{BA} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\). Taking their dot product gives
\[
(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) \cdot (\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 - \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 0,
\]
proving immediately that the diagonals meet at \(90^\circ\). Notice that the exact same algebra works in reverse: if a parallelogram's diagonals satisfy \((\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) \cdot (\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = 0\), then \(\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\|\), so the parallelogram must be a rhombus!
