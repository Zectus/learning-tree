=== SECTION 1: Mechanical work as a dot product ===

Suppose you pull a heavy sled \(12\text{ m}\) along a flat snowy trail using a rope tilted \(60^\circ\) above the ground. You pull with \(50\text{ N}\) of tension, yet not all of that \(50\text{ N}\) moves the sled forward: part of your pull lifts upward against the ground while only the horizontal shadow of \(\mathbf{F}\) along the trail actually drives the displacement \(\mathbf{d}\). In physics, mechanical work quantifies how effectively a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) pushes an object along a straight displacement \(\mathbf{d}\).

Only the signed scalar projection of \(\mathbf{F}\) onto the displacement \(\mathbf{d}\)—that is, \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{d}}\mathbf{F} = \|\mathbf{F}\|\cos\theta\), where \(\theta \in [0^\circ, 180^\circ]\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(\mathbf{d}\)—contributes along the path. Multiplying that effective force component by the distance traveled \(\|\mathbf{d}\|\) gives the work \(W\):
\[
W = (\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{d}}\mathbf{F})\,\|\mathbf{d}\| = (\|\mathbf{F}\|\cos\theta)\|\mathbf{d}\| = \|\mathbf{F}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}\|\cos\theta = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}.
\]
Recognizing that product as the dot product \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}\) immediately hands us two equivalent ways to compute work:
- From magnitudes and included angle: \(W = \|\mathbf{F}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}\|\cos\theta\).
- From Cartesian components: if \(\mathbf{F} = \langle F_x, F_y \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{d} = \langle d_x, d_y \rangle\), then \(W = F_x d_x + F_y d_y\)—no angle calculation required!

Notice two critical properties of \(W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}\). First, work is a SCALAR (a signed real number, measured in newton-meters or joules, \(1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\)), NOT a vector. Second, the sign of \(W\) carries direct physical meaning, inherited from the sign of \(\cos\theta\):
- Positive work (\(W > 0\), \(0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ\)): \(\mathbf{F}\) has a forward component aiding the motion.
- Zero work (\(W = 0\), \(\theta = 90^\circ\), or \(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\), or \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{0}\)): \(\mathbf{F}\) acts perpendicular to the displacement (for example, the upward normal force on a flat floor, or carrying a heavy box horizontally at constant height), or the object does not move at all.
- Negative work (\(W < 0\), \(90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ\)): \(\mathbf{F}\) has a backward component opposing the motion (for example, kinetic friction, braking, or gravity during an upward lift).

[CALLOUT]
id: work-def
label: Definition
title: Work done by a constant planar force
tone: key

When a constant force \(\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) acts on an object undergoing a straight-line displacement \(\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^2\), the work done by \(\mathbf{F}\) is the scalar
\[
W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = (\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{d}}\mathbf{F})\,\|\mathbf{d}\| = \|\mathbf{F}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}\|\cos\theta = F_x d_x + F_y d_y.
\]
In SI units, force in newtons (\(\text{N}\)) and displacement in meters (\(\text{m}\)) yield work in joules (\(\text{J}\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us try both formulas on concrete cases.
- Angle-magnitude case (our sled): \(\|\mathbf{F}\| = 50\text{ N}\), \(\|\mathbf{d}\| = 12\text{ m}\), \(\theta = 60^\circ\). Then \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{d}}\mathbf{F} = 50\cos 60^\circ = 25\text{ N}\), and
\[
W = (25\text{ N})(12\text{ m}) = 300\text{ J}.
\]
The upward component \(50\sin 60^\circ = 25\sqrt{3}\text{ N}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{d}\) and does \(0\text{ J}\) of work.
- Component case: a constant force \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 15, -4 \rangle\text{ N}\) pushes a cart from \(A(1, 2)\) to \(B(9, 8)\) (coordinates in meters). First form the displacement vector \(\mathbf{d} = \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 9 - 1, 8 - 2 \rangle = \langle 8, 6 \rangle\text{ m}\). Then [REF: work-def | the work formula] gives
\[
W = \langle 15, -4 \rangle \cdot \langle 8, 6 \rangle = 15(8) + (-4)(6) = 120 - 24 = 96\text{ J}.
\]

=== SECTION 2: Net work by distributivity of the dot product ===

In realistic settings, several constant forces \(\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \dots, \mathbf{F}_k\) act simultaneously while an object moves through the same straight displacement \(\mathbf{d}\). How does the work done by the resultant force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf{F}_i\) compare with the works \(W_i = \mathbf{F}_i \cdot \mathbf{d}\) done by each individual force?

Because the dot product distributes over vector addition, the answer is a one-line identity:
\[
W_{\mathrm{net}} = \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} \cdot \mathbf{d} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf{F}_i\right) \cdot \mathbf{d} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\mathbf{F}_i \cdot \mathbf{d}) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} W_i.
\]
You may choose whichever calculation is cleaner: either sum the force vectors first and take one dot product, or compute each individual scalar work (immediately dropping any force perpendicular to \(\mathbf{d}\)) and add the scalars!

[CALLOUT]
id: net-work-law
label: Theorem
title: Additivity of work for concurrent constant forces
tone: key

If constant forces \(\mathbf{F}_1, \dots, \mathbf{F}_k\) act across the same straight displacement \(\mathbf{d}\), the work done by their resultant \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf{F}_i\) equals the algebraic sum of the individual works:
\[
W_{\mathrm{net}} = \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} \cdot \mathbf{d} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\mathbf{F}_i \cdot \mathbf{d}) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} W_i.
\]
In particular, if the forces are in equilibrium (\(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \mathbf{0}\)) while the object moves at constant velocity across \(\mathbf{d}\), then \(W_{\mathrm{net}} = 0\)—the positive and negative individual works cancel out exactly.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us verify [REF: net-work-law | additivity of work] on a crate dragged \(5\text{ m}\) to the right (\(\mathbf{d} = \langle 5, 0 \rangle\text{ m}\)) under four constant forces: a diagonal pull \(\mathbf{P} = \langle 40, 30 \rangle\text{ N}\), gravity \(\mathbf{G} = \langle 0, -80 \rangle\text{ N}\), upward floor normal \(\mathbf{N} = \langle 0, 50 \rangle\text{ N}\), and horizontal friction \(\mathbf{f} = \langle -25, 0 \rangle\text{ N}\).
- Individual works:
  - \(W_P = \langle 40, 30 \rangle \cdot \langle 5, 0 \rangle = 200\text{ J}\),
  - \(W_G = 0\text{ J}\) and \(W_N = 0\text{ J}\) (both perpendicular to \(\mathbf{d}\)),
  - \(W_f = \langle -25, 0 \rangle \cdot \langle 5, 0 \rangle = -125\text{ J}\).
  Adding them yields \(W_{\mathrm{net}} = 200 + 0 + 0 - 125 = 75\text{ J}\).
- Resultant route: \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \langle 40 + 0 + 0 - 25,\; 30 - 80 + 50 + 0 \rangle = \langle 15, 0 \rangle\text{ N}\), so \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} \cdot \mathbf{d} = 15(5) + 0 = 75\text{ J}\). Both routes agree to the joule.

=== SECTION 3: Tilted-axis force decomposition via orthogonal projection ===

In our static equilibrium lesson, decomposing gravity on an inclined plane meant finding the ramp's tilt angle \(\alpha\), drawing a rotated coordinate frame, and using \(\sin\alpha\) and \(\cos\alpha\). What if the ramp or track is specified instead by a direction vector \(\mathbf{v}\) (for instance, a ramp rising \(3\text{ m}\) over a horizontal run of \(4\text{ m}\), so \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 3 \rangle\)), and an arbitrary force \(\mathbf{F}\) (gravity, or even an angled push) acts on the object?

Orthogonal projection handles ANY tilted direction directly in ordinary \((x, y)\) coordinates—no inverse tangent, no rotated axes, and no risk of swapping sine and cosine! Given any nonzero direction vector \(\mathbf{v}\) along the ramp and any planar force \(\mathbf{F}\):
\[
\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_\parallel + \mathbf{F}_\perp,
\]
where:
- \(\mathbf{F}_\parallel = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{F} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\right)\mathbf{v}\) is the vector component of \(\mathbf{F}\) PARALLEL to the ramp (with signed scalar component \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{F} = \frac{\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\)).
- \(\mathbf{F}_\perp = \mathbf{F} - \mathbf{F}_\parallel\) is the vector component of \(\mathbf{F}\) PERPENDICULAR (normal) to the ramp.

Furthermore, if the object undergoes a straight displacement \(\mathbf{d}\) along the ramp (so \(\mathbf{d}\) is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{v}\)), then \(\mathbf{F}_\perp \perp \mathbf{d}\) gives \(\mathbf{F}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{d} = 0\), and [REF: net-work-law | distributivity] confirms
\[
W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = (\mathbf{F}_\parallel + \mathbf{F}_\perp) \cdot \mathbf{d} = \mathbf{F}_\parallel \cdot \mathbf{d}.
\]
Only \(\mathbf{F}_\parallel\) does work along the ramp, while \(\mathbf{F}_\perp\) determines how hard the force presses into (or lifts off) the ramp surface!

[CALLOUT]
id: proj-ramp-decomp
label: Method
title: Projection-based force decomposition along a tilted direction \(\mathbf{v}\)
tone: key

To split any planar force \(\mathbf{F}\) relative to a track or ramp pointing along a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\):

(1) Parallel component along the ramp: \(\mathbf{F}_\parallel = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{F} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\right)\mathbf{v}\), with signed magnitude \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{F} = \frac{\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\).
(2) Normal component perpendicular to the ramp: \(\mathbf{F}_\perp = \mathbf{F} - \mathbf{F}_\parallel\), satisfying \(\mathbf{F}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\) and \(\|\mathbf{F}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{F}_\parallel\|^2 + \|\mathbf{F}_\perp\|^2\).
(3) Equilibrium link on a smooth ramp under gravity \(\mathbf{G}\): a parallel holding rope must exert \(\mathbf{T} = -\mathbf{G}_\parallel\), and the ramp exerts normal force \(\mathbf{N} = -\mathbf{G}_\perp\).
[/CALLOUT]

Work this out on a concrete ramp pointing along \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 3 \rangle\) (so \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 25\) and \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = 5\)) supporting a \(100\text{ N}\) crate, whose weight vector is \(\mathbf{G} = \langle 0, -100 \rangle\text{ N}\).
- Dot product: \(\mathbf{G} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0(4) + (-100)(3) = -300\).
- Signed component along \(\mathbf{v}\): \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{G} = \frac{-300}{5} = -60\text{ N}\) (exact—the minus sign tells us gravity pulls \(60\text{ N}\) DOWN the ramp, opposite to \(\mathbf{v}\)).
- Parallel vector component:
\[
\mathbf{G}_\parallel = \frac{-300}{25}\langle 4, 3 \rangle = -12\langle 4, 3 \rangle = \langle -48, -36 \rangle\text{ N}, \qquad \|\mathbf{G}_\parallel\| = 60\text{ N}.
\]
- Perpendicular vector component:
\[
\mathbf{G}_\perp = \mathbf{G} - \mathbf{G}_\parallel = \langle 0, -100 \rangle - \langle -48, -36 \rangle = \langle 48, -64 \rangle\text{ N}, \qquad \|\mathbf{G}_\perp\| = \sqrt{48^2 + (-64)^2} = 80\text{ N}.
\]
Check both identities: \(\mathbf{G}_\perp \cdot \mathbf{v} = 48(4) - 64(3) = 192 - 192 = 0\), and \(60^2 + 80^2 = 100^2\). By [REF: proj-ramp-decomp.3 | the equilibrium link], holding the crate still on this smooth ramp requires \(\mathbf{T} = -\mathbf{G}_\parallel = \langle 48, 36 \rangle\text{ N}\) (magnitude \(60\text{ N}\)), and the ramp pushes outward with normal force \(\mathbf{N} = -\mathbf{G}_\perp = \langle -48, 64 \rangle\text{ N}\) (magnitude \(80\text{ N}\))—all computed with exact integers and zero trigonometry!

Now suppose the crate is hauled \(10\text{ m}\) UP the ramp. Its displacement is \(\mathbf{d} = 10\hat{\mathbf{v}} = 10\left(\frac{\langle 4, 3 \rangle}{5}\right) = \langle 8, 6 \rangle\text{ m}\). The work done by gravity during this climb is
\[
W_G = \mathbf{G} \cdot \mathbf{d} = \langle 0, -100 \rangle \cdot \langle 8, 6 \rangle = -600\text{ J},
\]
which agrees with \((\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{G})\,\|\mathbf{d}\| = (-60\text{ N})(10\text{ m}) = -600\text{ J}\) and shows directly why gravity's work depends only on the vertical rise of \(6\text{ m}\) (\(-100\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = -600\text{ J}\)).
