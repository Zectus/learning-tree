=== SECTION 1: Concurrent forces, resultants, and equilibrants ===

A force is a push or pull with both a magnitude (measured in newtons, \(\text{N}\)) and a direction—making it a prototypical physical vector. When several forces \(\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \dots, \mathbf{F}_k\) act on the same point (for example, ropes tied to one steel ring, or cables meeting at a hook), we call them concurrent forces. How do they combine into one overall push, and what single extra force would cancel them completely?

Experiments and Newtonian mechanics establish that concurrent forces combine by ordinary vector addition. Their vector sum is called the resultant (or net) force:
\[
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 + \dots + \mathbf{F}_k.
\]
To compute \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\) when forces are given by magnitudes and angles, resolve each force into rectangular components \((F_{i,x}, F_{i,y})\) using \(\mathbf{F}_i = \|\mathbf{F}_i\|\langle \cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i \rangle\), sum the \(x\)-components and \(y\)-components separately to obtain
\[
F_{\mathrm{net},x} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} F_{i,x}, \qquad F_{\mathrm{net},y} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} F_{i,y},
\]
and then convert \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \langle F_{\mathrm{net},x}, F_{\mathrm{net},y} \rangle\) back to magnitude and quadrant-checked standard direction angle if desired.

Now suppose we want to add ONE balancing force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}}\) that brings the total force to zero: \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} + \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}} = \mathbf{0}\). Solving gives the equilibrant force:
\[
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}} = -\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}.
\]
Geometrically, the equilibrant has the exact same magnitude as the resultant (\(\|\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}}\| = \|\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\|\)) and points in the exact opposite direction (\(\theta_{\mathrm{eq}} = \theta_{\mathrm{net}} \pm 180^\circ\)).

[CALLOUT]
id: resultant-equilibrant
label: Definition
title: Resultant force and equilibrant
tone: key

For concurrent planar forces \(\mathbf{F}_1, \dots, \mathbf{F}_k\):

(1) The resultant (net) force is the vector sum \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf{F}_i = \left\langle \sum F_{i,x},\; \sum F_{i,y} \right\rangle\).
(2) The equilibrant is the unique single force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}} = -\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\) that balances all given forces so that \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} + \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}} = \mathbf{0}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us work through a concrete example. Two ropes pull a ring at the origin: \(\mathbf{F}_1\) pulls with \(60\text{ N}\) at standard angle \(30^\circ\), and \(\mathbf{F}_2\) pulls with \(40\text{ N}\) at standard angle \(150^\circ\).
- Resolve each force:
\[
\mathbf{F}_1 = 60\left\langle \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\; \frac{1}{2} \right\rangle = \langle 30\sqrt{3},\; 30 \rangle\text{ N} \approx \langle 51.96,\; 30 \rangle\text{ N},
\]
\[
\mathbf{F}_2 = 40\left\langle -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\; \frac{1}{2} \right\rangle = \langle -20\sqrt{3},\; 20 \rangle\text{ N} \approx \langle -34.64,\; 20 \rangle\text{ N}.
\]
- Sum components to get [REF: resultant-equilibrant.1 | the resultant]:
\[
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \langle 10\sqrt{3},\; 50 \rangle\text{ N} \approx \langle 17.32,\; 50 \rangle\text{ N}.
\]
Its magnitude is \(\|\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\| = \sqrt{300 + 2500} = \sqrt{2800} = 20\sqrt{7} \approx 52.92\text{ N}\). Since both components are positive (Quadrant I), its direction angle is \(\theta_{\mathrm{net}} = \arctan\left(\frac{50}{10\sqrt{3}}\right) \approx 70.9^\circ\).
- Take the opposite for [REF: resultant-equilibrant.2 | the equilibrant]:
\[
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}} = \langle -10\sqrt{3},\; -50 \rangle\text{ N},
\]
which has the same magnitude \(20\sqrt{7} \approx 52.92\text{ N}\) and points in Quadrant III at \(70.9^\circ + 180^\circ = 250.9^\circ\).

The plot below shows \(\mathbf{F}_1\), \(\mathbf{F}_2\), their resultant \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\), and the equilibrant \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{eq}}\) sitting directly opposite \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\) on the circle of radius \(20\sqrt{7}\text{ N}\).

=== SECTION 2: 2D static equilibrium and suspended-weight systems ===

In statics, we take Newton's first law of motion as our physical modeling premise: an object that remains at rest (in static equilibrium) under concurrent forces \(\mathbf{F}_1, \dots, \mathbf{F}_k\) experiences zero net force:
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}.
\]
Because a 2D vector equals \(\mathbf{0} = \langle 0, 0 \rangle\) if and only if BOTH of its components are zero, this single vector equation splits into two simultaneous scalar equations:
\[
\sum F_x = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sum F_y = 0.
\]
Two equations let us determine up to two unknown scalar quantities—such as the unknown tensions \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) in two supporting cables, or an unknown force magnitude and angle.

[CALLOUT]
id: eq-workflow
label: Method
title: Solving a 2D static equilibrium problem
tone: key

(1) Isolate the junction or object and draw a clean force diagram showing every force acting ON it (weight pointing straight down with magnitude \(W\), cable tensions pulling outward along each cable, and any contact/normal push perpendicular to a surface).
(2) Choose a convenient pair of perpendicular axes \((x, y)\) so that as many forces as possible lie directly along an axis.
(3) Resolve every remaining angled force into \(x\)- and \(y\)-components using its sign in each quadrant.
(4) Write \(\sum F_x = 0\) and \(\sum F_y = 0\) and solve the resulting \(2 \times 2\) system for the unknowns.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: eq-workflow | this workflow] to a classic problem: a \(100\text{ N}\) traffic signal hangs at rest from two cables meeting at a ring. Cable 1 rises to the left at \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal (standard angle \(150^\circ\)) with unknown tension \(T_1\); Cable 2 rises to the right at \(60^\circ\) above the horizontal (standard angle \(60^\circ\)) with unknown tension \(T_2\).

Three concurrent forces act on the ring:
- Downward weight: \(\mathbf{W} = \langle 0,\; -100 \rangle\text{ N}\).
- Tension in Cable 1: \(\mathbf{T}_1 = \langle -T_1\cos 30^\circ,\; T_1\sin 30^\circ \rangle = \left\langle -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}T_1,\; \frac{1}{2}T_1 \right\rangle\).
- Tension in Cable 2: \(\mathbf{T}_2 = \langle T_2\cos 60^\circ,\; T_2\sin 60^\circ \rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{2}T_2,\; \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}T_2 \right\rangle\).

Now enforce \(\sum F_x = 0\) and \(\sum F_y = 0\):
\[
-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}T_1 + \frac{1}{2}T_2 = 0 \implies T_2 = \sqrt{3}\,T_1,
\]
\[
\frac{1}{2}T_1 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}T_2 - 100 = 0.
\]
Substitute \(T_2 = \sqrt{3}\,T_1\) into the vertical equation:
\[
\frac{1}{2}T_1 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\left(\sqrt{3}\,T_1\right) = 100 \implies \frac{1}{2}T_1 + \frac{3}{2}T_1 = 2T_1 = 100 \implies T_1 = 50\text{ N}.
\]
Then \(T_2 = 50\sqrt{3} \approx 86.6\text{ N}\). Check both sums: horizontally, \(-50\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) + 50\sqrt{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 0\); vertically, \(50\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) + 50\sqrt{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = 25 + 75 = 100\text{ N}\), balancing the weight! Notice also the physical lesson: the steeper cable (Cable 2 at \(60^\circ\)) carries substantially MORE tension than the shallower cable (Cable 1 at \(30^\circ\)), because their horizontal pulls must match while the steeper cable has a smaller cosine and larger sine.

=== SECTION 3: Rotated axes on an inclined plane ===

Now consider a crate of weight \(W\) resting on a smooth (frictionless) ramp inclined at an acute angle \(\alpha\) above the horizontal, held in place by a rope pulling straight up along the ramp. Three forces act on the crate:
- Downward weight \(\mathbf{W}\) of magnitude \(W\).
- Rope tension \(\mathbf{T}\) pointing UP the ramp (at angle \(\alpha\) above horizontal).
- Normal force \(\mathbf{N}\) from the ramp surface pushing PERPENDICULAR to the ramp (at angle \(\alpha + 90^\circ\)).

If you insist on horizontal and vertical axes, BOTH unknowns \(T\) and \(N\) appear in BOTH equations \(\sum F_x = 0\) and \(\sum F_y = 0\), forcing coupled algebra. Instead, follow [REF: eq-workflow.2 | Step 2 of our workflow] and tilt your axes to match the ramp:
- Take the positive \(x'\)-axis pointing UP along the ramp.
- Take the positive \(y'\)-axis pointing OUTWARD perpendicular to the ramp.

In this rotated \((x', y')\) frame, \(\mathbf{T} = \langle T, 0 \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{N} = \langle 0, N \rangle\) each have only ONE nonzero component! The only vector left to resolve is the downward weight \(\mathbf{W}\). How does \(\mathbf{W}\) sit relative to the tilted axes? Starting from horizontal/vertical axes, rotating the positive \(x\)-axis counterclockwise by \(\alpha\) to form \(+x'\) simultaneously rotates the downward negative \(y\)-axis counterclockwise by the exact same angle \(\alpha\) to form the inward normal \(-y'\). Therefore, \(\mathbf{W}\) makes angle \(\alpha\) with the \(-y'\)-axis and points into the third tilted quadrant (both down-ramp and into-ramp):
\[
W_{x'} = -W\sin\alpha, \qquad W_{y'} = -W\cos\alpha.
\]

[CALLOUT]
id: ramp-trig-trap
label: Common pitfall
title: Do not blindly pair \(x\) with cosine and \(y\) with sine on a ramp
tone: caution

On a ramp tilted at angle \(\alpha\) to the horizontal, the weight vector \(\mathbf{W}\) makes angle \(\alpha\) with the NORMAL (\(-y'\)) axis, not the ramp (\(x'\)) axis. Hence the component along the ramp is \(W\sin\alpha\) and the component perpendicular to the ramp is \(W\cos\alpha\). Always test the two extreme angles to be certain: as \(\alpha \to 0^\circ\) (flat floor), \(W\sin\alpha \to 0\) (no sliding tendency) and \(W\cos\alpha \to W\) (full weight into the floor); as \(\alpha \to 90^\circ\) (vertical cliff), \(W\sin\alpha \to W\) and \(W\cos\alpha \to 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

Setting \(\sum F_{x'} = 0\) and \(\sum F_{y'} = 0\) now decouples the two unknowns instantly:
\[
\sum F_{x'} = T - W\sin\alpha = 0 \implies T = W\sin\alpha,
\]
\[
\sum F_{y'} = N - W\cos\alpha = 0 \implies N = W\cos\alpha.
\]
For example, holding a \(200\text{ N}\) crate on a smooth \(30^\circ\) ramp requires \(T = 200\sin 30^\circ = 100\text{ N}\) up the ramp, while the ramp pushes back with normal force \(N = 200\cos 30^\circ = 100\sqrt{3} \approx 173.2\text{ N}\). Notice that \(T^2 + N^2 = 100^2 + (100\sqrt{3})^2 = 40{,}000 = 200^2 = W^2\) because \(\mathbf{T}\) and \(\mathbf{N}\) are perpendicular and together equal \(-\mathbf{W}\).

Drag slider \(a\) (the ramp angle \(\alpha\) in radians) in the plot below to watch the holding tension \(\mathbf{T}\) and normal force \(\mathbf{N}\) adjust for a \(200\text{ N}\) crate (drawn in units of \(100\text{ N}\), so \(\|\mathbf{W}\| = 2\)). As the ramp steepens, \(\mathbf{T}\) grows toward \(2\) while \(\mathbf{N}\) shrinks toward \(0\), yet \(\mathbf{T} + \mathbf{N}\) always cancels \(\mathbf{W}\) exactly.
