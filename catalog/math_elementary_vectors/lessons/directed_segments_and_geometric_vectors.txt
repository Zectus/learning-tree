=== SECTION 1: Scalars, vectors, and directed line segments ===

Suppose a hiker tells you they walked \(4\text{ km}\) from camp. That single number tells you how far they traveled, yet it leaves you guessing where they ended up: they could be \(4\text{ km}\) north, \(4\text{ km}\) east, or anywhere on a circle of radius \(4\text{ km}\). To specify their new location relative to camp, you need two pieces of information together: a size (how far) and a direction (which way).

Mathematics separates quantities into two kinds on this exact basis. A scalar is a quantity specified completely by a single real number (with units when measured), such as temperature, mass, elapsed time, or walking distance. A vector is a quantity that has both a magnitude (a nonnegative size or length) and a direction, such as a straight-line displacement from one point to another, a velocity, or a push in a particular direction.

In the Euclidean plane, we represent a vector geometrically as a directed line segment—an arrow drawn from an initial point (its tail) to a terminal point (its head). If an arrow starts at point \(A\) and ends at point \(B\), we write \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) for that directed segment. Its length is the ordinary Euclidean distance between \(A\) and \(B\), and its direction is the straight ray from \(A\) toward \(B\). When we talk about a vector as an abstract quantity without naming specific endpoints, we write a single bold letter such as \(\mathbf{v}\) or \(\mathbf{u}\).

Here is the crucial convention that turns arrows into vectors. Suppose you walk \(3\text{ m}\) east and \(2\text{ m}\) north starting from a park bench, and your friend walks \(3\text{ m}\) east and \(2\text{ m}\) north starting from a bus stop across town. The two arrows sit in different places on the map, yet they represent the exact same displacement. If you slide one arrow straight across the plane without rotating or stretching it—a parallel translation—it lands right on top of the other.

[CALLOUT]
id: free-vector
label: Definition
title: Free vectors, zero vector, and opposite vector
tone: key

Two directed line segments \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) and \(\overrightarrow{CD}\) represent the same geometric vector, written \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{CD}\), if and only if they satisfy both conditions:

(1) Equal magnitude: segments \(AB\) and \(CD\) have the same length.
(2) Same direction: segments \(AB\) and \(CD\) lie on the same line or on parallel lines and point the same way.

Two special vectors accompany this definition:

(3) The zero vector \(\mathbf{0}\) is the degenerate directed segment whose tail and head coincide (\(\overrightarrow{AA} = \mathbf{0}\)). It has length \(0\) and no defined direction.
(4) The opposite (or negative) of a vector \(\mathbf{v}\), written \(-\mathbf{v}\), has the same length as \(\mathbf{v}\) and points in the exact reverse direction. In endpoint notation, \(\overrightarrow{BA} = -\overrightarrow{AB}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Because a vector keeps its identity under parallel translation, geometric vectors are often called free vectors: location is not part of a vector's identity. A common early mistake is to treat two translated copies of an arrow as different vectors just because they begin at different points. By [REF: free-vector | the equivalence rule], only length and direction matter.

To see this visually, the diagram below uses a background reference grid solely to help your eye compare lengths and directions (we will not attach coordinate formulas until the next lesson). Notice that the three copies labeled \(\mathbf{v}\) all share the same length and direction—each rises the same amount as it shifts right—so they all represent one and the same vector \(\mathbf{v}\). Meanwhile, \(-\mathbf{v}\) has the same length along a parallel line but points the opposite way, and \(\mathbf{w}\) has the same length as \(\mathbf{v}\) in a different direction, so \(\mathbf{w} \ne \mathbf{v}\).

Observe also how [REF: free-vector | vector equivalence] connects directly to parallelograms from Euclidean geometry. If four points \(A, B, C, D\) (not all on one line) satisfy \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{DC}\), then segments \(AB\) and \(DC\) are equal in length and parallel, so \(ABCD\) (walking around the boundary in order \(A \to B \to C \to D\)) is a parallelogram. Conversely, in any parallelogram \(ABCD\), opposite sides are parallel and congruent, giving \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{DC}\) and \(\overrightarrow{AD} = \overrightarrow{BC}\), while reversing the arrow on one side gives \(\overrightarrow{CD} = -\overrightarrow{AB}\).

=== SECTION 2: Geometric vector addition and subtraction ===

Suppose a sailboat makes two straight-line legs in succession: first displacement \(\mathbf{u}\) from harbor \(A\) to buoy \(B\), and then displacement \(\mathbf{v}\) from buoy \(B\) to island \(C\). What single straight-line displacement would have taken the boat directly from \(A\) to \(C\)? The arrow \(\overrightarrow{AC}\) captures the net effect of performing \(\mathbf{u}\) followed by \(\mathbf{v}\). That net arrow is how we define the sum \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\).

Because vectors are free to translate, we can add any two vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) even if they are initially drawn far apart. Simply slide a copy of \(\mathbf{v}\) without rotating it so that its tail lands on the head of \(\mathbf{u}\). The sum \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) is the arrow from the tail of \(\mathbf{u}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{v}\). This is the head-to-tail rule (also called the triangle rule).

Now ask what happens if we perform the two legs in the opposite order: \(\mathbf{v}\) first, then \(\mathbf{u}\). Place both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) so their tails meet at a common point \(O\). Drawing a copy of \(\mathbf{v}\) starting at the head of \(\mathbf{u}\), and a copy of \(\mathbf{u}\) starting at the head of \(\mathbf{v}\), forms a parallelogram whose opposite sides are parallel and equal in length. Both head-to-tail paths—\(\mathbf{u}\) followed by \(\mathbf{v}\), and \(\mathbf{v}\) followed by \(\mathbf{u}\)—end at the exact same opposite corner of the parallelogram!

That single parallelogram picture gives us two results at once:
1. The parallelogram rule: when \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are placed tail-to-tail, their sum \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) is the diagonal starting from their shared tail.
2. Commutativity of vector addition: \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\) for all planar vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\).

What about adding three vectors \(\mathbf{u}\), \(\mathbf{v}\), and \(\mathbf{w}\)? Chain them head-to-tail in sequence: \(\mathbf{u} = \overrightarrow{AB}\), \(\mathbf{v} = \overrightarrow{BC}\), \(\mathbf{w} = \overrightarrow{CD}\). Grouping the first two gives \((\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \overrightarrow{AC} + \overrightarrow{CD} = \overrightarrow{AD}\). Grouping the last two gives \(\mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = \overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BD} = \overrightarrow{AD}\). Both groupings trace out the same overall start-to-finish arrow \(\overrightarrow{AD}\), establishing associativity:
\[
(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}).
\]
The same head-to-tail chaining works for any multi-leg journey: \(\overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BC} + \overrightarrow{CD} = \overrightarrow{AD}\). If a journey returns to its starting point, as in \(\overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BC} + \overrightarrow{CA} = \overrightarrow{AA} = \mathbf{0}\), the vectors form a closed polygon and their sum is the zero vector. In particular, adding a vector to its opposite always brings you back to the tail: \(\mathbf{v} + (-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}\).

With opposite vectors in hand, geometric subtraction needs no separate mystery. Just as subtracting real numbers means adding the negative, we define the difference \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\) by
\[
\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v}).
\]
To see what \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\) looks like when \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) share a common tail \(O\), ask the reverse question: what vector must you add to \(\mathbf{v}\) to reach \(\mathbf{u}\)? Algebraically,
\[
\mathbf{v} + (\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}.
\]
Reading that head-to-tail equation geometrically: start at \(O\), travel along \(\mathbf{v}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{v}\), and then travel along \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\) to arrive at the head of \(\mathbf{u}\). Therefore, when \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are placed tail-to-tail, \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\) is the arrow pointing FROM the head of \(\mathbf{v}\) TO the head of \(\mathbf{u}\)—the second diagonal of the parallelogram!

[CALLOUT]
id: add-sub
label: Rule
title: Geometric addition and subtraction
tone: key

Let \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) be geometric vectors in the plane.

(1) Head-to-tail (triangle) rule: place the tail of \(\mathbf{v}\) at the head of \(\mathbf{u}\); then \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) runs from the tail of \(\mathbf{u}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{v}\).
(2) Parallelogram rule: place \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) tail-to-tail; then \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) is the diagonal from the shared tail to the opposite corner.
(3) Subtraction: \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v})\). When \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are placed tail-to-tail, \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\) runs from the head of \(\mathbf{v}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{u}\).
[/CALLOUT]

[CALLOUT]
id: sub-direction
label: Common pitfall
title: Do not reverse the arrow of \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\)
tone: caution

When \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) share a tail, the segment connecting their heads has two possible orientations. The arrow from the head of \(\mathbf{v}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{u}\) is \(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\), whereas the arrow from the head of \(\mathbf{u}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u} = -(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v})\). Always verify the direction by checking the head-to-tail sum: \(\mathbf{v} + (\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}\) means the arrow must point toward \(\mathbf{u}\)'s head.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Scalar multiplication and geometric parallelism ===

Repeatedly adding a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) to itself—such as \(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v}\)—produces an arrow in the same direction with three times the length. It is natural to write this as \(3\mathbf{v}\), and to extend the same idea to any real number \(k\), whether positive, negative, fractional, or zero.

[CALLOUT]
id: scalar-mult
label: Definition
title: Geometric scalar multiplication
tone: key

Let \(\mathbf{v}\) be a vector and let \(k\) be a real scalar. The scalar multiple \(k\mathbf{v}\) is defined as follows:

(1) If \(k > 0\), then \(k\mathbf{v}\) has length \(k\) times the length of \(\mathbf{v}\) and the same direction as \(\mathbf{v}\).
(2) If \(k < 0\), then \(k\mathbf{v}\) has length \(|k|\) times the length of \(\mathbf{v}\) and the opposite direction to \(\mathbf{v}\).
(3) If \(k = 0\) or \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\), then \(k\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\).

In every case, the length of \(k\mathbf{v}\) equals \(|k|\) times the length of \(\mathbf{v}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice that every scalar multiple \(k\mathbf{v}\) stays on the same straight line through the tail of \(\mathbf{v}\) (or on a line parallel to it after translation). This connects scalar multiplication directly to geometric parallelism.

[CALLOUT]
id: geom-parallel
label: Criterion
title: Geometric parallelism via scalar multiples
tone: key

Two nonzero vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are parallel if and only if one is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other—that is, \(\mathbf{u} = k\mathbf{v}\) for some real number \(k \ne 0\).

(1) If \(k > 0\), then \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) have the same direction.
(2) If \(k < 0\), then \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) have opposite directions (sometimes called anti-parallel, yet still parallel because they lie along parallel lines).
[/CALLOUT]

To see why [REF: geom-parallel | the parallelism criterion] works both ways: if \(\mathbf{u} = k\mathbf{v}\) with \(k \ne 0\), then by [REF: scalar-mult | the definition of scalar multiplication] \(\mathbf{u}\) lies along the same or a parallel line as \(\mathbf{v}\). Conversely, suppose nonzero \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) lie on parallel lines, and let \(r > 0\) be the ratio of \(\mathbf{u}\)'s length to \(\mathbf{v}\)'s length. If they point the same way, then \(\mathbf{u} = r\mathbf{v}\); if they point opposite ways, then \(\mathbf{u} = (-r)\mathbf{v}\). Either way, \(\mathbf{u}\) is a nonzero scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{v}\).

Scalar multiplication also interacts cleanly with vector addition through the distributive law
\[
k(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = k\mathbf{u} + k\mathbf{v}.
\]
Euclidean similar triangles explain why. Draw the triangle for \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) by placing \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) head-to-tail. For any \(k > 0\), scaling the two legs to \(k\mathbf{u}\) and \(k\mathbf{v}\) keeps the angle between them unchanged and scales both adjacent sides by the exact same factor \(k\). By SAS triangle similarity, the third side \(k\mathbf{u} + k\mathbf{v}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\), points the same way, and has \(k\) times its length—so it equals \(k(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})\). When \(k < 0\), scaling by \(|k|\) and reversing every arrow reverses the third side too, yielding the exact same equality. Likewise, combining two steps along the same line gives \((k + m)\mathbf{v} = k\mathbf{v} + m\mathbf{v}\), and scaling twice in a row gives \(k(m\mathbf{v}) = (km)\mathbf{v}\).

Let us put these geometric tools to work on a classic triangle configuration. Suppose in \(\triangle OAB\) we set \(\mathbf{a} = \overrightarrow{OA}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \overrightarrow{OB}\), and let \(M\) and \(N\) be the midpoints of sides \(OA\) and \(OB\). Then \(\overrightarrow{OM} = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}\) and \(\overrightarrow{ON} = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{b}\). By [REF: add-sub.3 | tail-to-tail subtraction] and distributivity,
\[
\overrightarrow{MN} = \overrightarrow{ON} - \overrightarrow{OM} = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{b} - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}) = \frac{1}{2}\overrightarrow{AB}.
\]
Because \(\overrightarrow{MN}\) is a positive scalar multiple of \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) with factor \(\frac{1}{2}\), we see in a single line of vector algebra that segment \(MN\) is parallel to base \(AB\), points from \(M\) to \(N\) in the same direction as \(A\) to \(B\), and has exactly half the length of \(AB\).
