=== SECTION 1: Rotating the coordinate axes ===

Every axis-aligned conic we have analyzed so far lacked a cross-product \(xy\)-term. Now consider the simple equation \(xy = 4\), or more generally the full second-degree equation
\[
Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0 \qquad (B \neq 0).
\]
Can a translation \(x = x' + h\), \(y = y' + k\) remove the \(xy\)-term? Substituting gives \(B(x' + h)(y' + k) = Bx'y' + Bkx' + Bhy' + Bhk\): the coefficient \(B\) on \(x'y'\) does not budge at all! Geometrically, a nonzero \(xy\)-term signals that the conic's symmetry axes are tilted relative to the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes. To align the coordinate axes with the conic, we must rotate the coordinate frame.

Keep the origin fixed at \((0, 0)\) and rotate the positive \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes counterclockwise through an angle \(\theta\) to form new perpendicular axes \(x'\) and \(y'\):
- One unit along the positive \(x'\)-axis lands at the unit-circle point \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) in the original plane.
- Because the positive \(y'\)-axis sits \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise from the positive \(x'\)-axis (at angle \(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\)), one unit along the positive \(y'\)-axis lands at \((\cos(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}), \sin(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2})) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\).

Now take any point \(P\) whose coordinates in the rotated frame are \((x', y')\). Starting from the origin, we reach \(P\) by moving \(x'\) units in the \(x'\)-direction \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and then \(y'\) units in the \(y'\)-direction \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\). Adding the horizontal and vertical displacements gives \(x\) and \(y\) directly!

[CALLOUT]
id: rotation-equations
label: Rule
title: Rotation of axes through counterclockwise angle \(\theta\)
tone: key

When the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes are rotated counterclockwise by angle \(\theta\) about the origin to form the \(x'y'\)-axes, original coordinates \((x, y)\) and rotated coordinates \((x', y')\) are related by
\[
x = x'\cos\theta - y'\sin\theta, \qquad y = x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta.
\]
Solving this \(2 \times 2\) linear system using \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\) (equivalently, rotating back by \(-\theta\)) yields the inverse equations:
\[
x' = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta, \qquad y' = -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Drag the angle slider \(a\) (in radians, representing \(\theta\)) below to rotate the dashed \(x'\)- and \(y'\)-axes counterclockwise around the origin. The point \(P\) keeps fixed rotated coordinates \((x', y') = (2, 1)\), so you can watch how stepping \(2\) units along \((\cos a, \sin a)\) and \(1\) unit along \((-\sin a, \cos a)\) locates \(P\) in the original \(xy\)-plane.

=== SECTION 2: Eliminating the xy-term and special-angle rotations ===

To find which angle \(\theta\) eliminates the \(x'y'\)-term from \(Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) (\(B \neq 0\)), we need two compact trigonometric bridges linking \(\theta\) and \(2\theta\).

[CALLOUT]
id: trig-bridges
label: Reference
title: Double-angle and acute half-angle identities
tone: note

For any angle \(\theta\), the double-angle identities relate products and squares of \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) to \(2\theta\):
(1) \(\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\).
(2) \(\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta\).

Solving the last two forms for \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) when \(\theta \in \bigl(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\) (so both \(\cos\theta > 0\) and \(\sin\theta > 0\)) yields the acute half-angle formulas:
(3) \(\cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}}, \qquad \sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Now substitute [REF: rotation-equations | the forward rotation formulas] \(x = x'\cos\theta - y'\sin\theta\) and \(y = x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta\) into \(Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\):
\[
A(x'\cos\theta - y'\sin\theta)^2 + B(x'\cos\theta - y'\sin\theta)(x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta) + C(x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta)^2 + D(x'\cos\theta - y'\sin\theta) + E(x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta) + F = 0.
\]
Expanding and grouping like terms produces the transformed equation
\[
A'x'^2 + B'x'y' + C'y'^2 + D'x' + E'y' + F' = 0,
\]
with new coefficients
\[
A' = A\cos^2\theta + B\sin\theta\cos\theta + C\sin^2\theta,
\]
\[
B' = B(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta) + (C - A)(2\sin\theta\cos\theta),
\]
\[
C' = A\sin^2\theta - B\sin\theta\cos\theta + C\cos^2\theta,
\]
along with \(D' = D\cos\theta + E\sin\theta\), \(E' = -D\sin\theta + E\cos\theta\), and \(F' = F\).

Apply [REF: trig-bridges.1 | the double-angle sine] and [REF: trig-bridges.2 | double-angle cosine] to \(B'\):
\[
B' = B\cos 2\theta + (C - A)\sin 2\theta.
\]
To eliminate the cross-product term, set \(B' = 0\):
\[
B\cos 2\theta = (A - C)\sin 2\theta.
\]
Can \(\sin 2\theta = 0\)? No: if \(\sin 2\theta = 0\), then \(\cos 2\theta = \pm 1\), forcing \(B = 0\) contrary to \(B \neq 0\). We may therefore divide safely by \(B\sin 2\theta\) to obtain the elimination condition.

[CALLOUT]
id: elimination-angle
label: Theorem
title: Angle that eliminates the \(xy\)-term
tone: key

Given \(Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) with \(B \neq 0\), the rotated cross-term coefficient \(B'\) vanishes if and only if the rotation angle \(\theta\) satisfies
\[
\cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B}.
\]
Choosing \(2\theta \in (0, \pi)\) uniquely fixes an acute counterclockwise rotation angle \(\theta \in \bigl(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\). In particular, whenever \(A = C\), we have \(\cot 2\theta = 0\), so \(2\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}\) (\(45^\circ\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Test [REF: elimination-angle | this condition] immediately on \(xy = 4\). Here \(A = 0\), \(B = 1\), and \(C = 0\), so \(A = C\) gives \(\theta = 45^\circ\), with \(\cos 45^\circ = \sin 45^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\). Substituting \(x = \frac{x' - y'}{\sqrt{2}}\) and \(y = \frac{x' + y'}{\sqrt{2}}\) turns \(xy = 4\) into
\[
\left(\frac{x' - y'}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{x' + y'}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = 4 \implies \frac{x'^2 - y'^2}{2} = 4 \implies \frac{x'^2}{8} - \frac{y'^2}{8} = 1.
\]
In the rotated frame, \(xy = 4\) is simply a rectangular hyperbola with \(a = b = 2\sqrt{2}\) opening along the \(x'\)-axis (the line \(y = x\)). Its vertices \((x', y') = (\pm 2\sqrt{2}, 0)\) translate back via \(x = \frac{x' - y'}{\sqrt{2}}, y = \frac{x' + y'}{\sqrt{2}}\) to \((2, 2)\) and \((-2, -2)\) in the original plane!

When \(\frac{A - C}{B}\) equals \(\pm \sqrt{3}\), \(\pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\), or \(0\), the rotation lands on one of the special angles \(15^\circ\), \(30^\circ\), \(45^\circ\), \(60^\circ\), or \(75^\circ\). Watch the sign of \(\cot 2\theta\) carefully! On \(7x^2 - 6\sqrt{3}xy + 13y^2 = 16\), we have \(A = 7\), \(B = -6\sqrt{3}\), and \(C = 13\), so
\[
\cot 2\theta = \frac{7 - 13}{-6\sqrt{3}} = \frac{-6}{-6\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.
\]
Because \(\cot 2\theta > 0\) and \(2\theta \in (0^\circ, 180^\circ)\), \(2\theta\) lies in Quadrant I: \(2\theta = 60^\circ\), so \(\theta = 30^\circ\), with \(\cos 30^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) and \(\sin 30^\circ = \frac{1}{2}\). Computing the rotated coefficients directly gives
\[
A' = 7\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) - 6\sqrt{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\right) + 13\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{21 - 18 + 13}{4} = 4,
\]
\[
C' = 7\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + 6\sqrt{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\right) + 13\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) = \frac{7 + 18 + 39}{4} = 16.
\]
Thus \(4x'^2 + 16y'^2 = 16\), or \(\frac{x'^2}{4} + \frac{y'^2}{1} = 1\)—an ellipse tilted by \(30^\circ\) with semi-major axis \(2\) along the \(x'\)-axis and semi-minor axis \(1\) along the \(y'\)-axis.

=== SECTION 3: Exact non-special rotations via reference triangles and half-angles ===

Most quadratic equations produce a rational value of \(\cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B}\) that does not correspond to a special angle—such as \(\frac{5}{12}\) or \(-\frac{3}{4}\). You never need a calculator or inverse trigonometric approximation! Because we only ever substitute \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) (never \(\theta\) itself), a signed reference triangle for \(2\theta\) combined with [REF: trig-bridges.3 | the acute half-angle formulas] delivers exact radical values every time:
(1) Compute \(\cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B}\) and reduce it to lowest terms.
(2) Remember that \(2\theta \in (0, \pi)\), so \(\sin 2\theta > 0\) ALWAYS. Therefore \(\cos 2\theta\) has the EXACT SAME SIGN as \(\cot 2\theta\): positive when \(\cot 2\theta > 0\) (Quadrant I), and negative when \(\cot 2\theta < 0\) (Quadrant II).
(3) Form a right triangle with adjacent leg \(\lvert p \rvert\), opposite leg \(\lvert q \rvert\) (where \(\cot 2\theta = \frac{p}{q}\)), and hypotenuse \(r = \sqrt{p^2 + q^2}\). Then \(\cos 2\theta = \pm \frac{\lvert p \rvert}{r}\), keeping the sign of \(\cot 2\theta\).
(4) Evaluate \(\cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}}\) and \(\sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}}\), both positive since \(\theta \in (0, \frac{\pi}{2})\).
(5) Substitute \(x = x'\cos\theta - y'\sin\theta\) and \(y = x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta\) into the equation, simplify, and sketch the conic relative to the rotated \(x'\)-axis (direction \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\)) and \(y'\)-axis (direction \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\)).

Work through a complete Quadrant II example: \(4x^2 + 4xy + 7y^2 = 24\).
Here \(A = 4\), \(B = 4\), and \(C = 7\), so
\[
\cot 2\theta = \frac{4 - 7}{4} = -\frac{3}{4}.
\]
The reference triangle has legs \(3\) and \(4\) and hypotenuse \(5\). Because \(\cot 2\theta < 0\), the angle \(2\theta\) lies in Quadrant II, giving
\[
\cos 2\theta = -\frac{3}{5}.
\]
Applying [REF: trig-bridges.3 | the acute half-angle formulas]:
\[
\cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (-3/5)}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{5}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}, \qquad \sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - (-3/5)}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{4}{5}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}.
\]
Now compute \(A'\) and \(C'\) (knowing \(B' = 0\) by construction):
\[
A' = 4\left(\frac{1}{5}\right) + 4\left(\frac{2}{5}\right) + 7\left(\frac{4}{5}\right) = \frac{4 + 8 + 28}{5} = 8,
\]
\[
C' = 4\left(\frac{4}{5}\right) - 4\left(\frac{2}{5}\right) + 7\left(\frac{1}{5}\right) = \frac{16 - 8 + 7}{5} = 3.
\]
The equation simplifies to \(8x'^2 + 3y'^2 = 24\), or
\[
\frac{x'^2}{3} + \frac{y'^2}{8} = 1.
\]
To sketch this ellipse in the original \(xy\)-plane, draw the positive \(x'\)-axis through the origin in the direction \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta) \parallel (1, 2)\) (slope \(2\)), draw the perpendicular positive \(y'\)-axis in the direction \((-2, 1)\) (slope \(-\frac{1}{2}\)), and plot the ellipse with semi-axis \(\sqrt{3}\) along \(x'\) and semi-major axis \(2\sqrt{2}\) along \(y'\).
