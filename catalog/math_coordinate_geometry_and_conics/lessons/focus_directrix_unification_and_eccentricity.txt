=== SECTION 1: One focus-directrix ratio for all non-circular conics ===

Earlier in this sequence, we defined parabolas, ellipses, and hyperbolas in two seemingly unrelated ways: a parabola was defined using ONE focus and a directrix line (\(PF = d(P, L)\)), whereas ellipses and hyperbolas were defined using TWO foci (\(PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a\) and \(\lvert PF_1 - PF_2 \rvert = 2a\)) and assigned an eccentricity \(e = \frac{c}{a}\). Yet the parabola's defining equation can be written as the ratio
\[
\frac{PF}{d(P, L)} = 1.
\]
What happens if we replace that constant ratio \(1\) with ANY positive number \(e > 0\)? Remarkably, the exact same focus-directrix ratio produces ellipses when \(0 < e < 1\), parabolas when \(e = 1\), and hyperbolas when \(e > 1\)—and the ratio \(e\) turns out to be the EXACT same eccentricity \(e = \frac{c}{a}\) we already know!

[CALLOUT]
id: focus-directrix-def
label: Definition
title: Unified focus-directrix characterization of conics
tone: key

Let \(F\) be a fixed point (a focus), let \(L\) be a fixed line not passing through \(F\) (its associated directrix), and let \(e > 0\) be a fixed positive constant (the eccentricity). The locus of all points \(P\) in the plane satisfying
\[
\frac{PF}{d(P, L)} = e \qquad \text{(equivalently, } PF = e \cdot d(P, L)\text{)}
\]
is a conic section with eccentricity \(e\):
(1) If \(0 < e < 1\), the locus is an ellipse.
(2) If \(e = 1\), the locus is a parabola.
(3) If \(e > 1\), the locus is a hyperbola.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us prove [REF: focus-directrix-def | this unified theorem] directly in Cartesian coordinates without guessing where the center or directrix ends up. Place the directrix \(L\) along the \(y\)-axis (\(x = 0\)) and place the focus on the positive \(x\)-axis at \(F(k, 0)\) with \(k > 0\) (so \(k\) is the perpendicular distance from the focus to the directrix). For any point \(P(x, y)\), we have \(PF = \sqrt{(x - k)^2 + y^2}\) and \(d(P, L) = \lvert x \rvert\). Squaring \(PF = e\,d(P, L)\) yields
\[
(x - k)^2 + y^2 = e^2 x^2 \implies (1 - e^2)x^2 - 2kx + y^2 + k^2 = 0.
\]
Look at the coefficient \(1 - e^2\) on \(x^2\):
- Case 1 (\(e = 1\)): The \(x^2\)-term vanishes! We get \(-2kx + y^2 + k^2 = 0\), or \(y^2 = 2k\bigl(x - \frac{k}{2}\bigr)\), a parabola with vertex at \(\bigl(\frac{k}{2}, 0\bigr)\) midway between \(F(k, 0)\) and \(x = 0\).
- Case 2 (\(e \neq 1\)): Divide through by \(1 - e^2\) and complete the square in \(x\):
\[
x^2 - \frac{2k}{1 - e^2}x + \frac{y^2}{1 - e^2} = -\frac{k^2}{1 - e^2},
\]
\[
\left(x - \frac{k}{1 - e^2}\right)^2 + \frac{y^2}{1 - e^2} = \frac{k^2}{(1 - e^2)^2} - \frac{k^2(1 - e^2)}{(1 - e^2)^2} = \frac{k^2 e^2}{(1 - e^2)^2}.
\]
Translate the origin to the center \(O'\bigl(\frac{k}{1 - e^2}, 0\bigr)\) by setting \(X = x - \frac{k}{1 - e^2}\) and \(Y = y\), and define the positive semi-axis
\[
a = \frac{ke}{\lvert 1 - e^2 \rvert} > 0.
\]
Dividing both sides by \(a^2 = \frac{k^2 e^2}{(1 - e^2)^2}\) produces
\[
\frac{X^2}{a^2} + \frac{Y^2}{a^2(1 - e^2)} = 1.
\]
Now read off BOTH conic families and their exact focus and directrix coordinates in the centered \((X, Y)\)-frame:
- When \(0 < e < 1\), \(1 - e^2 > 0\), so setting \(b^2 = a^2(1 - e^2)\) gives the ellipse \(\frac{X^2}{a^2} + \frac{Y^2}{b^2} = 1\), with focal distance \(c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = ae\).
- When \(e > 1\), \(1 - e^2 < 0\), so setting \(b^2 = a^2(e^2 - 1) > 0\) gives the hyperbola \(\frac{X^2}{a^2} - \frac{Y^2}{b^2} = 1\), with focal distance \(c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = ae\).
- Where do the focus and directrix sit relative to the center \(x_0 = \frac{k}{1 - e^2}\)?
  - The focus \(x = k\) sits at \(X_F = k - \frac{k}{1 - e^2} = -\frac{ke^2}{1 - e^2}\), so \(\lvert X_F \rvert = \frac{ke^2}{\lvert 1 - e^2 \rvert} = ae = c\).
  - The directrix \(x = 0\) sits at \(X_L = 0 - \frac{k}{1 - e^2} = -\frac{k}{1 - e^2}\), so \(\lvert X_L \rvert = \frac{k}{\lvert 1 - e^2 \rvert} = \frac{a}{e}\).
  - Notice \(X_F\) and \(X_L\) ALWAYS share the same sign! Thus the focus \((ae, 0)\) pairs with the directrix \(X = \frac{a}{e}\), and by left-right symmetry of the centered conic, the opposite focus \((-ae, 0)\) pairs with a second directrix \(X = -\frac{a}{e}\).

=== SECTION 2: Directrix locations and the circle limit as e approaches 0 ===

The centered derivation reveals a clean geometric rule for locating the directrices of any axis-aligned ellipse or hyperbola.

[CALLOUT]
id: centered-directrices
label: Rule
title: Foci and directrices of axis-aligned ellipses and hyperbolas
tone: key

For an ellipse or hyperbola centered at \((h, k)\) with semi-major or semi-transverse axis \(a\), focal distance \(c\), and eccentricity \(e = \frac{c}{a}\):
(1) Horizontal axis (\(\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} \pm \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1\)): foci are \((h \pm ae, k)\) and directrices are the vertical lines \(x = h \pm \frac{a}{e} = h \pm \frac{a^2}{c}\).
(2) Vertical axis (\(\frac{(y - k)^2}{a^2} \pm \frac{(x - h)^2}{b^2} = 1\)): foci are \((h, k \pm ae)\) and directrices are the horizontal lines \(y = k \pm \frac{a}{e} = k \pm \frac{a^2}{c}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Compare the relative positions of the center, focus, vertex, and directrix along the focal axis across all four conic types!

[TABLE]
Eccentricity \(e\) | Conic type | Ordering from center outward along the focal axis
\(e = 0\) (limit) | Circle | Foci merge at center (\(c = 0\)); directrices recede to infinity (\(\frac{a}{e} \to \infty\))
\(0 < e < 1\) | Ellipse | Center (\(0\)) \(<\) Focus (\(ae\)) \(<\) Vertex (\(a\)) \(<\) Directrix (\(\frac{a}{e}\))
\(e = 1\) | Parabola | Focus and directrix sit at equal distance \(p\) on opposite sides of the vertex
\(e > 1\) | Hyperbola | Center (\(0\)) \(<\) Directrix (\(\frac{a}{e}\)) \(<\) Vertex (\(a\)) \(<\) Focus (\(ae\))
[/TABLE]

For an ellipse (\(0 < e < 1\)), \(\frac{a}{e} > a > ae\): the directrices lie OUTSIDE the entire curve. How do circles fit into this unified family? Hold the semi-major axis \(a\) fixed and let \(e \to 0^+\):
- The foci \((\pm ae, 0)\) glide inward and coalesce at the center \((0, 0)\).
- The semi-minor axis \(b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2}\) approaches \(a\), rounding the ellipse into the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = a^2\).
- The directrices \(x = \pm \frac{a}{e}\) recede infinitely far from the origin (\(\frac{a}{e} \to \infty\)). Yet the focus-directrix product stays finite: for any point \(P(x, y)\) near the origin, \(d(P, L) = \frac{a}{e} - x\), so
\[
PF = e \cdot d(P, L) = e\left(\frac{a}{e} - x\right) = a - ex \xrightarrow{\;e \to 0^+\;} a.
\]
A circle does not have a finite directrix, because its directrix has vanished to infinity while \(e \to 0^+\) keeps the constant radius \(PF = a\) intact!

=== SECTION 3: Constructing conic equations from focus-directrix data ===

Given a focus \(F(x_F, y_F)\), a directrix \(L\), and an eccentricity \(e > 0\), you can always write the conic's Cartesian equation directly from [REF: focus-directrix-def | the defining equation] \(PF^2 = e^2\,[d(P, L)]^2\).

When the directrix is horizontal or vertical, completing the square recovers standard form cleanly—and checking the distance \(k = d(F, L) = \bigl\lvert \frac{a}{e} - ae \bigr\rvert = a\frac{\lvert 1 - e^2 \rvert}{e}\) gives a fast cross-check.

For example, find the standard equation of the conic with focus \(F(4, 1)\), directrix \(x = -2\), and eccentricity \(e = \frac{1}{2}\). Setting up \(PF^2 = e^2\,[d(P, L)]^2\):
\[
(x - 4)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = \frac{1}{4}(x + 2)^2.
\]
Multiply by \(4\) and collect terms:
\[
4(x^2 - 8x + 16) + 4(y - 1)^2 = x^2 + 4x + 4 \implies 3x^2 - 36x + 4(y - 1)^2 = -60.
\]
Complete the square in \(x\):
\[
3(x^2 - 12x + 36) + 4(y - 1)^2 = -60 + 108 = 48 \implies \frac{(x - 6)^2}{16} + \frac{(y - 1)^2}{12} = 1.
\]
Check with [REF: centered-directrices.1 | the centered directrix formula]: center \((6, 1)\), \(a = 4\), \(b^2 = 12\), \(c = 2\), \(e = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}\). The left focus is \((6 - 2, 1) = (4, 1)\) and the left directrix is \(x = 6 - \frac{4}{1/2} = -2\). Everything matches!

What if the directrix is an oblique line \(Ax + By + C = 0\)? Simply apply the point-to-line distance formula \(d(P, L) = \frac{\lvert Ax + By + C \rvert}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}\):
\[
(x - x_F)^2 + (y - y_F)^2 = e^2 \frac{(Ax + By + C)^2}{A^2 + B^2}.
\]
For instance, with focus \(F(1, 1)\), directrix \(x + y = 0\), and \(e = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) (an ellipse because \(e < 1\)):
\[
(x - 1)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{(x + y)^2}{1^2 + 1^2} = \frac{(x + y)^2}{4}.
\]
Multiplying by \(4\) and simplifying gives \(3x^2 - 2xy + 3y^2 - 8x - 8y + 8 = 0\)—a tilted ellipse whose \(xy\)-term arises automatically from the slanted directrix.
