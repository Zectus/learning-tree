=== SECTION 1: Two-focus difference definition and centered standard equations ===

Suppose two coastal listening stations at fixed locations \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) detect a sound pulse emitted from an unknown point \(P\). If the pulse arrives at \(F_2\) a fixed number of seconds before it reaches \(F_1\), then the difference between the two distances \(PF_1\) and \(PF_2\) is fixed, even though neither individual distance is known. What curve in the plane consists of all points whose distances to two fixed foci have a constant positive difference? That two-branch open curve is a hyperbola.

[CALLOUT]
id: hyperbola-def
label: Definition
title: Two-focus definition of a hyperbola
tone: key

Let \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) be two fixed points (the foci) separated by distance \(2c > 0\), and let \(2a\) be a positive constant strictly less than \(2c\) (so \(0 < a < c\)). The hyperbola with foci \(F_1, F_2\) and constant distance difference \(2a\) is the locus of all points \(P\) in the plane satisfying
\[
\lvert PF_1 - PF_2 \rvert = 2a.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Why must \(2a < 2c\)? By the triangle inequality applied to triangle \(P F_1 F_2\), the difference of two side lengths can never exceed the third side: \(\lvert PF_1 - PF_2 \rvert \le F_1 F_2 = 2c\), with equality only along the two rays extending outward from \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) on the line through them. Requiring \(0 < a < c\) guarantees a genuine two-branch curve. The branch with \(PF_1 - PF_2 = 2a\) wraps around \(F_2\) (points closer to \(F_2\)), while the branch with \(PF_2 - PF_1 = 2a\) wraps around \(F_1\) (points closer to \(F_1\)).

To translate [REF: hyperbola-def | the constant-difference definition] into coordinates, place the center midway between the foci at the origin \((0, 0)\) and put the foci on the \(x\)-axis at \(F_1(-c, 0)\) and \(F_2(c, 0)\). For any point \(P(x, y)\) on the hyperbola,
\[
\sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} - \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} = \pm 2a.
\]
Isolate the first radical on the left before squaring so that only one radical survives on the right:
\[
\sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} = \pm 2a + \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2}.
\]
Square both sides:
\[
x^2 + 2cx + c^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 \pm 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} + x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2.
\]
Cancel \(x^2\), \(c^2\), and \(y^2\) from both sides, gather the \(cx\)-terms, and divide through by \(4\):
\[
cx - a^2 = \pm a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2}.
\]
Square a second time to remove the remaining radical (the \(\pm\) sign disappears):
\[
c^2 x^2 - 2a^2 cx + a^4 = a^2\bigl(x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2\bigr).
\]
The \(-2a^2 cx\) terms cancel on both sides. Grouping \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) on the left and constants on the right yields
\[
(c^2 - a^2)x^2 - a^2 y^2 = a^2(c^2 - a^2).
\]
Because \(c > a > 0\), the quantity \(c^2 - a^2\) is strictly positive, so we define a positive length \(b > 0\) by
\[
b^2 = c^2 - a^2 \qquad \text{(equivalently, } c^2 = a^2 + b^2\text{)}.
\]
Substituting \(b^2 = c^2 - a^2\) gives \(b^2 x^2 - a^2 y^2 = a^2 b^2\), and dividing by \(a^2 b^2\) produces the centered standard equation with a horizontal transverse axis:
\[
\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1.
\]
Setting \(y = 0\) gives \(x = \pm a\), the two vertices \((\pm a, 0)\) connected by the transverse axis of length \(2a\). Setting \(x = 0\) yields \(-\frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\), which has no real solutions: the curve never crosses the \(y\)-axis, and the points \((0, \pm b)\) mark the endpoints of the conjugate axis of length \(2b\).

If the foci instead lie on the \(y\)-axis at \((0, \pm c)\), swapping \(x\) and \(y\) throughout the derivation produces the centered standard equation with a vertical transverse axis:
\[
\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1,
\]
with vertices \((0, \pm a)\) on the \(y\)-axis, conjugate axis endpoints \((\pm b, 0)\), and the same focal relation \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\).

[CALLOUT]
id: hyperbola-orientation
label: Common pitfall
title: Orientation and denominator size for hyperbolas
tone: caution

Unlike an ellipse (where the larger denominator always identifies the major axis and \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\)), a hyperbola's orientation is determined strictly by which squared term is POSITIVE:
(1) If the \(x^2\)-term is positive (\(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)), the transverse axis is horizontal and the branches open left and right.
(2) If the \(y^2\)-term is positive (\(\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1\)), the transverse axis is vertical and the branches open up and down.
(3) The denominator under the positive term is always \(a^2\), regardless of whether \(a < b\), \(a = b\), or \(a > b\).
(4) The focal distance satisfies \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), so \(c > a\) and \(c > b\): the foci always sit farther from the center than the vertices.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 2: Asymptotes, the central guide rectangle, translations, and eccentricity ===

What happens to the branches of \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) far from the origin? Solve explicitly for \(y\) in terms of \(x\) (requiring \(\lvert x \rvert \ge a\)):
\[
\frac{y^2}{b^2} = \frac{x^2}{a^2} - 1 \implies y = \pm \frac{b}{a}\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}.
\]
Factor \(x^2\) out of the square root (using \(\sqrt{x^2} = \lvert x \rvert\)):
\[
y = \pm \frac{b}{a}\lvert x \rvert \sqrt{1 - \frac{a^2}{x^2}}.
\]
As \(\lvert x \rvert\) grows arbitrarily large, the ratio \(\frac{a^2}{x^2}\) shrinks toward \(0\), so \(\sqrt{1 - \frac{a^2}{x^2}}\) approaches \(1\). Consequently, the branches approach two straight lines through the origin without ever crossing them:
\[
y = \pm \frac{b}{a}x.
\]
These two lines are the oblique asymptotes of the hyperbola. A fast algebraic check reproduces them immediately: replace the \(1\) on the right-hand side of \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) by \(0\) and solve \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} = \frac{y^2}{b^2}\), which gives \(y = \pm \frac{b}{a}x\). Applying the same argument to the vertical hyperbola \(\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1\) gives \(\frac{y^2}{a^2} = \frac{x^2}{b^2}\), so its asymptotes are
\[
y = \pm \frac{a}{b}x.
\]
Pay close attention to the slope ratio: in BOTH orientations, the asymptote slope equals the square root of the denominator under \(y\) divided by the square root of the denominator under \(x\) (rise over run).

This gives a reliable geometric blueprint for sketching any hyperbola: draw the central guide rectangle centered at the hyperbola's center with width \(2a\) and height \(2b\) (horizontal case) or width \(2b\) and height \(2a\) (vertical case). Its four corners lie at \((\pm a, \pm b)\) or \((\pm b, \pm a)\), so the diagonals extending through opposite corners ARE the asymptotes, and the vertices sit at the midpoints of the two sides perpendicular to the transverse axis. Moreover, the distance from the center to each corner of the rectangle is \(\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = c\), so the circle through the four corners passes directly through both foci.

Shifting the center from \((0, 0)\) to \((h, k)\) replaces \(x\) by \(x - h\) and \(y\) by \(y - k\) in the standard equation, the vertices, the foci, and the asymptotes.

[TABLE]
Feature | Horizontal transverse axis \(\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} - \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1\) | Vertical transverse axis \(\frac{(y - k)^2}{a^2} - \frac{(x - h)^2}{b^2} = 1\)
Center | \((h, k)\) | \((h, k)\)
Vertices | \((h \pm a, k)\) | \((h, k \pm a)\)
Foci (\(c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\)) | \((h \pm c, k)\) | \((h, k \pm c)\)
Asymptotes | \(y - k = \pm \frac{b}{a}(x - h)\) | \(y - k = \pm \frac{a}{b}(x - h)\)
Central guide rectangle | Width \(2a\), height \(2b\) | Width \(2b\), height \(2a\)
[/TABLE]

Just as eccentricity measures how stretched an ellipse is, the same ratio \(e = \frac{c}{a}\) measures how widely a hyperbola's branches open.

[CALLOUT]
id: hyperbola-eccentricity
label: Definition
title: Eccentricity of a hyperbola
tone: key

The eccentricity \(e\) of a hyperbola with semi-transverse axis \(a\) and focal distance \(c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\) is
\[
e = \frac{c}{a} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2}} > 1.
\]
(1) When \(e\) is just above \(1\), the ratio \(b/a\) is small and the branches fold tightly around the transverse axis.
(2) When \(e\) is large, \(b/a\) is large and the branches flare widely open.
(3) In the special case \(a = b\) (a rectangular or equilateral hyperbola), the asymptotes meet at right angles (slopes \(\pm 1\)) and \(e = \sqrt{2}\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Converting general form and spotting degenerate intersecting lines ===

Expanding either translated standard form produces an axis-aligned quadratic equation
\[
Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0
\]
in which \(A\) and \(C\) have OPPOSITE signs (\(AC < 0\)). To recover standard form, group the \(x\)-terms and \(y\)-terms, factor \(A\) out of the \(x\)-group and \(C\) out of the \(y\)-group, and complete both squares.

The single most common algebraic mistake occurs in the group with the NEGATIVE leading coefficient: factoring out a negative number flips the sign of the linear term inside the parentheses, and the square-completion constant added inside those parentheses actually SUBTRACTS from that side of the equation.

Follow every sign on \(9x^2 - 16y^2 - 36x - 64y - 172 = 0\):
\[
9(x^2 - 4x) - 16(y^2 + 4y) = 172.
\]
Notice that factoring \(-16\) out of \(-64y\) produces \(+4y\) inside the parentheses. Adding \(4\) inside the \(x\)-parentheses adds \(9 \cdot 4 = 36\) to the left-hand side; adding \(4\) inside the \(y\)-parentheses adds \(-16 \cdot 4 = -64\) to the left-hand side. Balancing both sides gives
\[
9(x - 2)^2 - 16(y + 2)^2 = 172 + 36 - 64 = 144.
\]
Dividing through by \(144\) yields the standard form:
\[
\frac{(x - 2)^2}{16} - \frac{(y + 2)^2}{9} = 1,
\]
a horizontal hyperbola centered at \((2, -2)\) with \(a = 4\), \(b = 3\), \(c = 5\), and asymptotes \(y + 2 = \pm \frac{3}{4}(x - 2)\).

What if the right-hand side \(R\) after completing the squares is negative or zero?

[CALLOUT]
id: hyperbola-degeneracy
label: Key distinction
title: Negative versus zero right-hand side after completing the square
tone: caution

Suppose completing the square yields \(P(x - h)^2 - Q(y - k)^2 = R\) with \(P, Q > 0\):
(1) If \(R > 0\), dividing by \(R\) yields a HORIZONTAL hyperbola \(\frac{(x - h)^2}{R/P} - \frac{(y - k)^2}{R/Q} = 1\).
(2) If \(R < 0\), the locus is NOT empty! Dividing by the negative number \(R\) flips both signs and yields a VERTICAL hyperbola \(\frac{(y - k)^2}{\lvert R \rvert / Q} - \frac{(x - h)^2}{\lvert R \rvert / P} = 1\).
(3) If \(R = 0\), the equation factors as a difference of squares: \(\bigl(\sqrt{P}(x - h) - \sqrt{Q}(y - k)\bigr)\bigl(\sqrt{P}(x - h) + \sqrt{Q}(y - k)\bigr) = 0\), producing a DEGENERATE pair of intersecting lines through \((h, k)\).
[/CALLOUT]

For example, \(4(x + 1)^2 - 9(y - 2)^2 = 0\) factors into \(\bigl(2(x + 1) - 3(y - 2)\bigr)\bigl(2(x + 1) + 3(y - 2)\bigr) = 0\), which is the pair of straight lines \(y - 2 = \pm \frac{2}{3}(x + 1)\) crossing at \((-1, 2)\). Visually, as \(R \to 0\), the vertices of the hyperbola shrink inward toward the center until the two branches collapse onto their own asymptotes.

=== SECTION 4: Difference-of-distance localization ===

Hyperbolas provide the mathematical foundation for hyperbolic navigation and acoustic localization (used in sonar, lightning-strike networks, and older radio navigation systems like LORAN). When two synchronized receivers sit at known points \(F_1(-c, 0)\) and \(F_2(c, 0)\) and record a signal traveling at constant speed \(v\), the measured arrival-time difference \(\Delta t\) determines the exact distance difference:
\[
\lvert PF_1 - PF_2 \rvert = v\,\Delta t = 2a.
\]
Crucially, knowing WHICH station received the pulse first removes the absolute value and pins the source to a single branch:
- If \(F_2(c, 0)\) receives the signal earlier than \(F_1(-c, 0)\), then \(PF_2 < PF_1\), so the source lies on the right branch (\(x \ge a\)).
- If \(F_1(-c, 0)\) receives the signal earlier than \(F_2(c, 0)\), then \(PF_1 < PF_2\), so the source lies on the left branch (\(x \le -a\)).

For example, suppose two underwater hydrophones are moored \(10\text{ km}\) apart at \(F_1(-5, 0)\) and \(F_2(5, 0)\) (so \(c = 5\text{ km}\)). An acoustic ping traveling through water at \(1.5\text{ km/s}\) reaches \(F_2\) exactly \(4\text{ s}\) before it reaches \(F_1\). The distance difference is
\[
PF_1 - PF_2 = (1.5\text{ km/s})(4\text{ s}) = 6\text{ km} = 2a \implies a = 3\text{ km}.
\]
Using \(b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\), the source must lie on the right branch (\(x \ge 3\)) of
\[
\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{16} = 1.
\]
If a second independent measurement places the source along the line \(y = 4\text{ km}\), substituting \(y = 4\) gives \(\frac{x^2}{9} - 1 = 1 \implies x^2 = 18\), and choosing the right branch selects the unique location \((3\sqrt{2}, 4)\).
