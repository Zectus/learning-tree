=== SECTION 1: Two-focus definition and derivation of the standard equation ===

Drive two tacks into a board at fixed points \(F_1\) and \(F_2\), tie a string longer than the distance between them to the two tacks, pull the string taut with a pencil, and trace all the way around. Because the string's total length never changes, every point \(P\) traced by the pencil satisfies the constant-sum distance condition \(PF_1 + PF_2 = \text{constant}\). That closed oval is an ellipse.

[CALLOUT]
id: ellipse-def
label: Definition
title: Two-focus definition of an ellipse
tone: key

Let \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) be two fixed points (the foci) separated by distance \(2c \ge 0\), and let \(2a\) be a constant strictly greater than \(2c\) (so \(a > c \ge 0\)). The ellipse with foci \(F_1, F_2\) and constant sum \(2a\) is the locus of all points \(P\) in the plane satisfying
\[
PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Why must \(2a > 2c\)? By the triangle inequality, \(PF_1 + PF_2 \ge F_1 F_2 = 2c\) for every point \(P\) in the plane, with equality only on the line segment joining \(F_1\) and \(F_2\). Requiring \(a > c\) guarantees a genuine two-dimensional curve enclosing both foci.

To turn [REF: ellipse-def | the string-sum definition] into coordinates, place the center midway between the foci at the origin \((0, 0)\) and align the foci along the \(x\)-axis at \(F_1(-c, 0)\) and \(F_2(c, 0)\). For any point \(P(x, y)\) on the ellipse,
\[
\sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} = 2a.
\]
Squaring directly with two radicals on the left leaves a messy cross-radical term, so first isolate one radical on the left:
\[
\sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} = 2a - \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2}.
\]
Square both sides:
\[
x^2 + 2cx + c^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 - 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} + x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2.
\]
Cancel the identical terms \(x^2\), \(c^2\), and \(y^2\) from both sides, collect the \(cx\)-terms, and divide by \(4\):
\[
4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} = 4a^2 - 4cx \implies a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} = a^2 - cx.
\]
Now square a second time to clear the remaining radical:
\[
a^2\bigl(x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2\bigr) = a^4 - 2a^2 cx + c^2 x^2.
\]
The \(-2a^2 cx\) terms cancel identically! Moving all variable terms to the left and constants to the right gives
\[
(a^2 - c^2)x^2 + a^2 y^2 = a^2(a^2 - c^2).
\]
Because \(a > c \ge 0\), the quantity \(a^2 - c^2\) is strictly positive, so we may define \(b > 0\) by
\[
b^2 = a^2 - c^2 \qquad \text{(equivalently, } c^2 = a^2 - b^2\text{)}.
\]
Substituting \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\) yields \(b^2 x^2 + a^2 y^2 = a^2 b^2\), and dividing through by \(a^2 b^2\) produces the centered standard equation:
\[
\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \qquad (a > b > 0).
\]
Notice the geometric meaning of \(a\) and \(b\) right off the intercepts:
- Setting \(y = 0\) gives \(x = \pm a\), the vertices \((\pm a, 0)\) at the ends of the major axis (length \(2a\)).
- Setting \(x = 0\) gives \(y = \pm b\), the co-vertices \((0, \pm b)\) at the ends of the minor axis (length \(2b\)).
- At a co-vertex \(B(0, b)\), symmetry gives \(B F_1 = B F_2 = a\), so the right triangle with legs \(b\) and \(c\) has hypotenuse \(a\), visually confirming \(b^2 + c^2 = a^2\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: ellipse-focal-relation
label: Common pitfall
title: Keeping \(a\) as the hypotenuse in \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\)
tone: caution

Do not confuse the ellipse relation \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) with the standard Pythagorean sum \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\). For an ellipse, the foci always lie INSIDE the curve, so \(c < a\). The semi-major axis \(a\) is the largest of the three lengths (\(a^2 = b^2 + c^2\)), and \(c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 2: Translated ellipses, orientation, and eccentricity ===

If the foci instead lie along the vertical line through the center, the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) exchange. Shifting the center from \((0, 0)\) to any point \((h, k)\) gives the two standard axis-aligned forms, where we ALWAYS keep the convention \(a > b > 0\):
- Horizontal major axis: \(\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1\) (larger denominator sits under \(x\)).
- Vertical major axis: \(\frac{(x - h)^2}{b^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{a^2} = 1\) (larger denominator sits under \(y\)).

Spotting the orientation of an ellipse is immediate: whichever variable has the LARGER denominator carries the major axis, vertices, and foci!

[TABLE]
Feature | Horizontal major axis \(\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1\) | Vertical major axis \(\frac{(x - h)^2}{b^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{a^2} = 1\)
Center | \((h, k)\) | \((h, k)\)
Vertices (major axis endpoints) | \((h \pm a, k)\) | \((h, k \pm a)\)
Co-vertices (minor axis endpoints) | \((h, k \pm b)\) | \((h \pm b, k)\)
Foci (\(c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}\)) | \((h \pm c, k)\) | \((h, k \pm c)\)
Major / minor axis lengths | \(2a\) horizontal, \(2b\) vertical | \(2a\) vertical, \(2b\) horizontal
[/TABLE]

How round or stretched is a given ellipse? That shape is measured by a single dimensionless number called the eccentricity.

[CALLOUT]
id: ellipse-eccentricity
label: Definition
title: Eccentricity of an ellipse
tone: key

The eccentricity \(e\) of an ellipse with semi-major axis \(a\) and focal distance \(c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}\) is
\[
e = \frac{c}{a} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}} \in [0, 1).
\]
(1) When \(e = 0\), we have \(c = 0\) and \(a = b\): the foci coalesce at the center and the ellipse is a circle.
(2) As \(e \to 1^-\), \(c\) approaches \(a\) and \(b/a = \sqrt{1 - e^2} \to 0\): the ellipse becomes increasingly elongated.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Converting general form by completing the square ===

Expanding either translated standard form and clearing denominators yields an equation of the type
\[
Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0,
\]
where \(A\) and \(C\) have the SAME sign (by multiplying by \(-1\) if needed, we assume \(A > 0\) and \(C > 0\)), and \(A \neq C\) for a non-circular ellipse.

To recover standard form, group the \(x\)- and \(y\)-terms, factor \(A\) out of the \(x\)-group and \(C\) out of the \(y\)-group, and complete both squares—remembering to multiply each added square-completion constant by \(A\) or \(C\) when balancing the right-hand side!

Watch every step on \(9x^2 + 4y^2 - 36x + 24y + 36 = 0\):
\[
9(x^2 - 4x) + 4(y^2 + 6y) = -36.
\]
Inside the first parentheses we add \(4\), which adds \(9 \cdot 4 = 36\) to the left-hand side; inside the second parentheses we add \(9\), which adds \(4 \cdot 9 = 36\) to the left-hand side. Balancing both sides gives
\[
9(x - 2)^2 + 4(y + 3)^2 = -36 + 36 + 36 = 36.
\]
Dividing through by \(36\) yields the standard equation:
\[
\frac{(x - 2)^2}{4} + \frac{(y + 3)^2}{9} = 1.
\]
Because the larger denominator \(9\) sits under \(y\), this is a vertical ellipse with center \((2, -3)\), \(a = 3\), \(b = 2\), and \(c = \sqrt{9 - 4} = \sqrt{5}\).

Just as with circles, completing the square also reveals degeneracies when the right-hand side \(R\) is not positive:
- If \(R = 0\), as in \(4(x - 1)^2 + 9(y + 2)^2 = 0\), the locus collapses to the single point \((1, -2)\).
- If \(R < 0\), as in \(4(x - 1)^2 + 9(y + 2)^2 = -5\), the locus is the empty set (no real points).

=== SECTION 4: Orbital distances and semi-elliptical arches ===

Two classic settings turn ellipse geometry directly into quantitative predictions.

First, in Kepler's model of planetary motion, a planet or comet traces an ellipse with its star situated at ONE focus (say \(F_1(-c, 0)\)), not at the center! Along the major axis, the closest point to the star (perihelion) is the nearer vertex \((-a, 0)\), at distance
\[
r_{\min} = a - c = a(1 - e),
\]
and the farthest point (aphelion) is the opposite vertex \((a, 0)\), at distance
\[
r_{\max} = a + c = a(1 + e).
\]
Notice that adding and subtracting these two equations immediately recovers \(a\) and \(c\) from observed extreme distances:
\[
a = \frac{r_{\max} + r_{\min}}{2}, \qquad c = \frac{r_{\max} - r_{\min}}{2}.
\]
For example, if a comet's perihelion is \(2\text{ AU}\) and its aphelion is \(18\text{ AU}\), then \(a = \frac{18 + 2}{2} = 10\text{ AU}\), \(c = \frac{18 - 2}{2} = 8\text{ AU}\), its eccentricity is \(e = \frac{8}{10} = 0.8\), and its semi-minor axis is \(b = \sqrt{10^2 - 8^2} = 6\text{ AU}\).

Second, semi-elliptical arches and tunnels correspond to the upper half (\(y \ge 0\)) of a centered horizontal ellipse \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\), where \(a\) is HALF the full base span and \(b\) is the center height. For instance, a tunnel \(20\text{ m}\) wide and \(8\text{ m}\) high at its center obeys \(\frac{x^2}{100} + \frac{y^2}{64} = 1\). At a point \(6\text{ m}\) from the center (\(x = 6\)):
\[
\frac{36}{100} + \frac{y^2}{64} = 1 \implies \frac{y^2}{64} = \frac{64}{100} \implies y = \frac{8 \cdot 8}{10} = 6.4\text{ m}.
\]
