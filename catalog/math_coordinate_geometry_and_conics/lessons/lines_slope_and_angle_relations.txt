=== SECTION 1: Slope as a line invariant and angle of inclination ===

What makes a straight line straight in the coordinate plane? Pick any two distinct points \(P_1(x_1, y_1)\) and \(P_2(x_2, y_2)\) on a non-vertical line \(L\), so \(x_1 \neq x_2\), and form the ratio of vertical change to horizontal change:
\[
m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}.
\]
Before calling \(m\) "the slope of the line," we must know that this ratio depends only on the line \(L\) and not on which pair of points we happened to pick.

Suppose \(P_1, P_2\) and \(Q_1, Q_2\) are two pairs of distinct points on the same non-vertical line \(L\). Construct the axis-aligned right triangles \(\triangle P_1 R P_2\) (with horizontal leg \(P_1 R\) and vertical leg \(R P_2\)) and \(\triangle Q_1 S Q_2\) (with horizontal leg \(Q_1 S\) and vertical leg \(S Q_2\)). Because both horizontal legs are parallel to the \(x\)-axis and the line \(L\) crosses them as a transversal, the corresponding acute angles at \(P_1\) and \(Q_1\) are equal. Both triangles also share a \(90^\circ\) angle, so \(\triangle P_1 R P_2 \sim \triangle Q_1 S Q_2\). Therefore the ratios of vertical leg to horizontal leg are equal in magnitude, and because both pairs rise or fall in the same direction along \(L\), their signed ratios match:
\[
\frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{y_2' - y_1'}{x_2' - x_1'}.
\]
Thus \(m\) is a genuine invariant of the line \(L\).

The slope also has a direct trigonometric interpretation. Every non-horizontal line \(L\) intersects the \(x\)-axis at some point; its angle of inclination \(\alpha \in [0, \pi)\) is the counterclockwise angle measured from the positive \(x\)-axis to the upward-pointing ray of \(L\) (with \(\alpha = 0\) for horizontal lines).
- If \(0 < \alpha < \frac{\pi}{2}\), the slope right triangle has positive horizontal run \(\Delta x > 0\) and positive vertical rise \(\Delta y > 0\), so \(m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \tan \alpha > 0\).
- If \(\alpha = 0\), then \(\Delta y = 0\) and \(m = \tan 0 = 0\) (a horizontal line).
- If \(\frac{\pi}{2} < \alpha < \pi\), the line falls from left to right. Taking a step to the left of length \(r > 0\) (so \(\Delta x = -r < 0\)) rises by \(r \tan(\pi - \alpha) > 0\), giving \(m = \frac{r \tan(\pi - \alpha)}{-r} = -\tan(\pi - \alpha) = \tan \alpha < 0\).
- If \(\alpha = \frac{\pi}{2}\), the line is vertical (\(x_2 - x_1 = 0\)), and both \(\tan(\pi/2)\) and the slope \(m\) are undefined.

=== SECTION 2: Four forms of the equation of a line ===

Because the slope between a fixed point \(P_1(x_1, y_1)\) on a non-vertical line \(L\) and any other point \(P(x, y)\) on \(L\) equals the constant \(m\), every point \(P(x, y) \neq P_1\) on \(L\) satisfies \(\frac{y - y_1}{x - x_1} = m\). Multiplying both sides by \(x - x_1\) gives
\[
y - y_1 = m(x - x_1),
\]
which is also satisfied by \(P_1(x_1, y_1)\) itself (both sides equal \(0\)). This is the point-slope form.

Specializing the anchor point produces two other heavily used forms:
1. Setting the anchor to the \(y\)-intercept \((0, b)\) gives the slope-intercept form \(y = mx + b\).
2. If the line crosses both axes away from the origin, at \((a, 0)\) and \((0, b)\) with \(a \neq 0\) and \(b \neq 0\), its slope is \(m = \frac{b - 0}{0 - a} = -\frac{b}{a}\). Substituting into \(y = mx + b\) gives \(y = -\frac{b}{a}x + b\); dividing by \(b\) and moving the \(x\)-term to the left yields the intercept form
\[
\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1.
\]

Notice, however, that point-slope, slope-intercept, and intercept forms all exclude vertical lines (because a vertical line has no slope \(m\) and no \(y\)-intercept \(b\)). Intercept form also excludes horizontal lines and every line through the origin. To represent every line in the plane—oblique, horizontal, and vertical—under one roof, we write the general linear equation:
\[
Ax + By + C = 0,
\]
where \(A\) and \(B\) are real constants not both zero.
- When \(B \neq 0\), solving for \(y\) gives \(y = -\frac{A}{B}x - \frac{C}{B}\), so the slope is \(m = -\frac{A}{B}\) (horizontal when \(A = 0\)).
- When \(B = 0\) and \(A \neq 0\), the equation reduces to \(x = -\frac{C}{A}\), a vertical line.

[CALLOUT]
id: line-forms
label: Reference
title: Standard algebraic forms of a planar line
tone: key

(1) Point-slope form: \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\) (all non-vertical lines).
(2) Slope-intercept form: \(y = mx + b\) (all non-vertical lines).
(3) Intercept form: \(\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1\) (lines with nonzero intercepts \(a, b\)).
(4) General linear form: \(Ax + By + C = 0\) with \((A, B) \neq (0, 0)\) (every line in the plane, including vertical lines \(x = c\)).
[/CALLOUT]

[TABLE]
Form | Equation | Direct geometric meaning | Lines excluded
Point-slope | \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\) | Slope \(m\), point \((x_1, y_1)\) | Vertical lines
Slope-intercept | \(y = mx + b\) | Slope \(m\), \(y\)-intercept \((0, b)\) | Vertical lines
Intercept | \(\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1\) | \(x\)-intercept \(a\), \(y\)-intercept \(b\) | Vertical, horizontal, and lines through \((0, 0)\)
General linear | \(Ax + By + C = 0\) | Slope \(-A/B\) when \(B \neq 0\); \(x = -C/A\) when \(B = 0\) | None
[/TABLE]

[CALLOUT]
id: vertical-line-pitfall
label: Common pitfall
title: Missing vertical lines when assuming \(y = mx + b\)
tone: caution

Whenever a problem asks you to find "all lines" through a given point satisfying a geometric condition, writing the unknown line as \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\) automatically assumes the line is not vertical. Always check the vertical line \(x = x_1\) separately (or use general form \(Ax + By + C = 0\)) so vertical solutions are never lost.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us practice converting fluently among all four forms using the line through \((-3, 4)\) and \((3, 0)\). Its slope is \(m = \frac{0 - 4}{3 - (-3)} = -\frac{2}{3}\).
- Point-slope using \((3, 0)\): \(y - 0 = -\frac{2}{3}(x - 3)\).
- Slope-intercept: \(y = -\frac{2}{3}x + 2\).
- General form (clearing the denominator \(3\)): \(2x + 3y - 6 = 0\).
- Intercept form (dividing \(2x + 3y = 6\) by \(6\)): \(\frac{x}{3} + \frac{y}{2} = 1\), immediately revealing \(x\)-intercept \(3\) and \(y\)-intercept \(2\).

=== SECTION 3: Parallel and perpendicular criteria ===

How do slopes tell us whether two lines are parallel or perpendicular?

For parallel lines, the answer comes directly from [REF: slope-inclination | angles of inclination]. Two distinct lines \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) in the plane are parallel if and only if they make equal corresponding angles with the \(x\)-axis, meaning \(\alpha_1 = \alpha_2\). Because \(\tan \alpha\) is one-to-one on \([0, \pi) \setminus \{\pi/2\}\), two non-vertical lines are parallel if and only if \(m_1 = m_2\). Two vertical lines (\(\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \pi/2\)) are also parallel to each other.

For perpendicular lines, suppose first that neither line is horizontal or vertical, and let \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) intersect at a point \(P\). Without loss of generality, let \(L_1\) rise to the right with slope \(m_1 > 0\). Starting from \(P\), move right \(1\) unit to \(Q\) and up \(m_1\) units to a point \(R\) on \(L_1\), forming right triangle \(\triangle PQR\) with legs \(PQ = 1\) and \(QR = m_1\). Now rotate \(\triangle PQR\) clockwise by \(90^\circ\) around \(P\):
- The horizontal leg \(PQ\) (pointing right by \(1\)) rotates into a vertical leg \(PQ'\) pointing down by \(1\).
- The vertical leg \(QR\) (pointing up by \(m_1\)) rotates into a horizontal leg \(Q'R'\) pointing right by \(m_1\).
- The hypotenuse \(PR\) along \(L_1\) rotates by \(90^\circ\) onto the hypotenuse \(PR'\) along the perpendicular line \(L_2\).

Reading the change from \(P\) to \(R'\) directly off the rotated legs gives horizontal run \(\Delta x = +m_1\) and vertical rise \(\Delta y = -1\). Therefore the slope of \(L_2\) is
\[
m_2 = \frac{-1}{m_1}, \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad m_1 m_2 = -1.
\]
Conversely, if \(m_1 m_2 = -1\) with \(m_1 > 0\), then \(m_2 = -1/m_1 < 0\). Building the two right triangles with legs \((1, m_1)\) and \((m_1, 1)\) as above produces congruent right triangles by SAS, so the two acute angles at \(P\) flanking the \(x\)-axis direction are complementary. Adding the remaining angle shows that the angle between \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) is exactly \(90^\circ\). Finally, if one line is horizontal (\(m = 0\)), its perpendicular is vertical (slope undefined).

[CALLOUT]
id: parallel-perp
label: Theorem
title: Parallel and perpendicular slope criteria
tone: key

Let \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) be two lines in the plane.
(1) Parallel: \(L_1 \parallel L_2\) if and only if they have equal slopes (\(m_1 = m_2\)), or both lines are vertical.
(2) Perpendicular: \(L_1 \perp L_2\) if and only if \(m_1 m_2 = -1\), or one line is horizontal (\(m = 0\)) and the other is vertical.
[/CALLOUT]

Notice a handy general-form version of [REF: parallel-perp.2 | perpendicularity]: two lines \(A_1 x + B_1 y + C_1 = 0\) and \(A_2 x + B_2 y + C_2 = 0\) are perpendicular if and only if
\[
A_1 A_2 + B_1 B_2 = 0.
\]
Indeed, when \(B_1, B_2 \neq 0\), substituting \(m_1 = -A_1/B_1\) and \(m_2 = -A_2/B_2\) into \(m_1 m_2 = -1\) gives \(\frac{A_1 A_2}{B_1 B_2} = -1 \iff A_1 A_2 + B_1 B_2 = 0\); and when \(B_1 = 0\) (\(A_1 \neq 0\), vertical), \(A_1 A_2 = 0\) forces \(A_2 = 0\) (horizontal).

=== SECTION 4: Intersections and triangle centers ===

Any two non-parallel lines \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) meet at a unique intersection point \((x, y)\), found by solving their two equations simultaneously as a \(2 \times 2\) linear system. Putting [REF: line-forms | line equations], [REF: parallel-perp | perpendicularity], and midpoints together lets us locate the three classical centers of any coordinate triangle \(\triangle ABC\):
- Circumcenter \(O\): the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the sides. Each perpendicular bisector passes through a side's midpoint and is perpendicular to that side.
- Orthocenter \(H\): the intersection of the altitudes. Each altitude passes through a vertex and is perpendicular to the opposite side.
- Centroid \(G\): the intersection of the medians. Each median connects a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Let us compute all three centers for the concrete triangle with vertices \(A(0, 0)\), \(B(6, 0)\), and \(C(2, 4)\).

First, the circumcenter \(O\). Side \(AB\) is horizontal with midpoint \((3, 0)\), so its perpendicular bisector is the vertical line \(x = 3\). Side \(AC\) has midpoint \((1, 2)\) and slope \(\frac{4 - 0}{2 - 0} = 2\), so its perpendicular bisector has slope \(-\frac{1}{2}\):
\[
y - 2 = -\frac{1}{2}(x - 1).
\]
Intersecting with \(x = 3\) gives \(y = 2 - \frac{1}{2}(3 - 1) = 1\), so \(O = (3, 1)\). Checking distances confirms \(OA^2 = 3^2 + 1^2 = 10\), \(OB^2 = (-3)^2 + 1^2 = 10\), and \(OC^2 = 1^2 + (-3)^2 = 10\): \(O\) is indeed equidistant from all three vertices.

Second, the orthocenter \(H\). The altitude from \(C(2, 4)\) to horizontal side \(AB\) is the vertical line \(x = 2\). The altitude from \(B(6, 0)\) to side \(AC\) (slope \(2\)) has slope \(-\frac{1}{2}\):
\[
y - 0 = -\frac{1}{2}(x - 6).
\]
Intersecting with \(x = 2\) gives \(y = -\frac{1}{2}(2 - 6) = 2\), so \(H = (2, 2)\). As an independent check, side \(BC\) has slope \(\frac{4 - 0}{2 - 6} = -1\), so the altitude from \(A(0, 0)\) has slope \(1\), namely \(y = x\)—and \(H(2, 2)\) lies right on \(y = x\).

Third, the centroid \(G\). The midpoint of \(AB\) is \(M_C(3, 0)\), so median \(C M_C\) passes through \((2, 4)\) and \((3, 0)\), giving slope \(-4\) and equation \(y = -4(x - 3) = -4x + 12\). The midpoint of \(AC\) is \(M_B(1, 2)\), so median \(B M_B\) passes through \((6, 0)\) and \((1, 2)\), giving slope \(-\frac{2}{5}\) and equation \(y = -\frac{2}{5}(x - 6)\). Equating the two \(y\)-expressions:
\[
-4x + 12 = -\frac{2}{5}x + \frac{12}{5} \implies -\frac{18}{5}x = -\frac{48}{5} \implies x = \frac{8}{3},
\]
and \(y = -4\!\left(\frac{8}{3}\right) + 12 = \frac{4}{3}\). Thus \(G = \left(\frac{8}{3}, \frac{4}{3}\right)\). Notice that \(\frac{0 + 6 + 2}{3} = \frac{8}{3}\) and \(\frac{0 + 0 + 4}{3} = \frac{4}{3}\): the centroid is the average of the three vertices (a fact we will prove in general in a later lesson).
