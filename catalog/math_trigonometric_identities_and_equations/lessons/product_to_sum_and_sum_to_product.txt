=== SECTION 1: Deriving the product-to-sum identities ===

In trigonometry, two opposite tasks come up repeatedly:
- Sometimes you have a PRODUCT of two sinusoids at different frequencies, such as \(\sin(5x)\cos(2x)\), and you want to rewrite it as a SUM of individual sinusoids (for example, to integrate it or read its frequency components).
- Other times you have a SUM or DIFFERENCE of two sinusoids, such as \(\sin(5x) + \sin x\), and you want to FACTOR it into a product (for example, to set each factor equal to \(0\) when solving an equation, or to cancel a factor in a fraction).

Both transformations come directly from adding and subtracting the four sum and difference identities you already know. Write the sine pair one above the other:
\[
\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta
\]
\[
\sin(\alpha - \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta - \cos\alpha\sin\beta
\]
Add the two equations: the \(\cos\alpha\sin\beta\) terms cancel, leaving \(\sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin(\alpha - \beta) = 2\sin\alpha\cos\beta\). Dividing by \(2\) gives
\[
\sin\alpha\cos\beta = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin(\alpha - \beta)\bigr].
\]
Subtract the second equation from the first instead: the \(\sin\alpha\cos\beta\) terms cancel, leaving \(\sin(\alpha + \beta) - \sin(\alpha - \beta) = 2\cos\alpha\sin\beta\), so
\[
\cos\alpha\sin\beta = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\sin(\alpha + \beta) - \sin(\alpha - \beta)\bigr].
\]

Now do the exact same addition and subtraction with the cosine pair:
\[
\cos(\alpha - \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta
\]
\[
\cos(\alpha + \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta
\]
Adding gives \(\cos(\alpha - \beta) + \cos(\alpha + \beta) = 2\cos\alpha\cos\beta\), hence
\[
\cos\alpha\cos\beta = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\cos(\alpha - \beta) + \cos(\alpha + \beta)\bigr].
\]
Subtracting the second from the first gives \(\cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta) = 2\sin\alpha\sin\beta\), hence
\[
\sin\alpha\sin\beta = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta)\bigr].
\]
Watch the order inside the last identity carefully: \(\cos(\alpha - \beta)\) comes FIRST and \(\cos(\alpha + \beta)\) is subtracted from it! If you reverse that order to \(\cos(\alpha + \beta) - \cos(\alpha - \beta)\), you get \(-2\sin\alpha\sin\beta\).

[CALLOUT]
id: product-to-sum
label: Theorem
title: Product-to-sum identities
tone: key

For all real angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\):

(1) \(\sin\alpha\cos\beta = \dfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin(\alpha - \beta)\bigr]\)
(2) \(\cos\alpha\sin\beta = \dfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\sin(\alpha + \beta) - \sin(\alpha - \beta)\bigr]\)
(3) \(\cos\alpha\cos\beta = \dfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\cos(\alpha - \beta) + \cos(\alpha + \beta)\bigr]\)
(4) \(\sin\alpha\sin\beta = \dfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta)\bigr]\)
[/CALLOUT]

Watch [REF: product-to-sum.1 | the first product-to-sum identity] compute an exact value that would otherwise need two separate angle-addition expansions: evaluate \(\sin(75^\circ)\cos(15^\circ)\).
Set \(\alpha = 75^\circ\) and \(\beta = 15^\circ\). Then \(\alpha + \beta = 90^\circ\) and \(\alpha - \beta = 60^\circ\):
\[
\sin(75^\circ)\cos(15^\circ) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\sin(90^\circ) + \sin(60^\circ)\bigr] = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = \frac{2 + \sqrt{3}}{4}.
\]
The same technique simplifies symbolic products immediately. For example, using [REF: product-to-sum.4 | the sine-sine identity] on \(\sin(4x)\sin(x)\):
\[
\sin(4x)\sin(x) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\cos(4x - x) - \cos(4x + x)\bigr] = \frac{1}{2}\cos(3x) - \frac{1}{2}\cos(5x).
\]

=== SECTION 2: The substitution bridge to sum-to-product identities ===

Each equation in [REF: product-to-sum | the product-to-sum identities] already relates a sum or difference of two trig functions (evaluated at \(\alpha + \beta\) and \(\alpha - \beta\)) to a product. To run those four equations in reverse—starting from arbitrary angles \(u\) and \(v\) in \(\sin u \pm \sin v\) or \(\cos u \pm \cos v\)—we simply rename the sum and difference angles:
\[
u = \alpha + \beta, \qquad v = \alpha - \beta.
\]
Solve this linear system for \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) by adding and subtracting the two equations:
- Adding gives \(u + v = 2\alpha\), so \(\alpha = \dfrac{u + v}{2}\) (the average of \(u\) and \(v\)).
- Subtracting gives \(u - v = 2\beta\), so \(\beta = \dfrac{u - v}{2}\) (the half-difference of \(u\) and \(v\)).

Now substitute \(\alpha + \beta = u\), \(\alpha - \beta = v\), \(\alpha = \frac{u + v}{2}\), and \(\beta = \frac{u - v}{2}\) into all four equations from [REF: product-to-sum | Section 1], multiplying each by \(2\):
- From \(2\sin\alpha\cos\beta = \sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin(\alpha - \beta)\):
\[
\sin u + \sin v = 2\sin\!\left(\frac{u + v}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{u - v}{2}\right).
\]
- From \(2\cos\alpha\sin\beta = \sin(\alpha + \beta) - \sin(\alpha - \beta)\):
\[
\sin u - \sin v = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{u + v}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{u - v}{2}\right).
\]
- From \(2\cos\alpha\cos\beta = \cos(\alpha + \beta) + \cos(\alpha - \beta)\):
\[
\cos u + \cos v = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{u + v}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{u - v}{2}\right).
\]
- Finally, be EXTRA careful with the fourth one! From [REF: product-to-sum.4 | the sine-sine product identity], \(2\sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta) = \cos v - \cos u\). Multiplying both sides by \(-1\) to get \(\cos u - \cos v\) on the left produces a crucial leading minus sign:
\[
\cos u - \cos v = -2\sin\!\left(\frac{u + v}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{u - v}{2}\right).
\]

[CALLOUT]
id: sum-to-product
label: Theorem
title: Sum-to-product identities
tone: key

For all real angles \(u\) and \(v\):

(1) \(\sin u + \sin v = 2\sin\!\left(\dfrac{u + v}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\dfrac{u - v}{2}\right)\)
(2) \(\sin u - \sin v = 2\cos\!\left(\dfrac{u + v}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\dfrac{u - v}{2}\right)\)
(3) \(\cos u + \cos v = 2\cos\!\left(\dfrac{u + v}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\dfrac{u - v}{2}\right)\)
(4) \(\cos u - \cos v = -2\sin\!\left(\dfrac{u + v}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\dfrac{u - v}{2}\right)\)
[/CALLOUT]

Notice a fast sanity check for the minus sign in [REF: sum-to-product.4 | \(\cos u - \cos v\)]: take \(u = \frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(v = 0\). On the left, \(\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}) - \cos 0 = 0 - 1 = -1 < 0\). On the right, both \(\sin(\frac{\pi}{4})\) and \(\sin(\frac{\pi}{4})\) are positive, so you MUST have the minus sign in front to get \(-2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = -1\).

=== SECTION 3: Factoring sums, proving quotient identities, and acoustic beats ===

Sum-to-product identities shine in three settings: factoring sums of different frequencies, collapsing trigonometric quotients, and explaining beat phenomena in physics and music.

First, factoring a sum of sinusoids turns an addition problem into a product of factors—the exact form you need later when solving equations set equal to \(0\). Take \(\sin(5x) + \sin x\): here \(\frac{5x + x}{2} = 3x\) and \(\frac{5x - x}{2} = 2x\), so by [REF: sum-to-product.1 | the sine-sum identity],
\[
\sin(5x) + \sin x = 2\sin(3x)\cos(2x).
\]
What if you want to check where \(\sin(5x) + \sin x = 0\)? In the additive form \(\sin(5x) + \sin x\), the frequencies \(5x\) and \(x\) are locked together; in the factored form \(2\sin(3x)\cos(2x)\), the expression vanishes precisely when \(\sin(3x) = 0\) or \(\cos(2x) = 0\).

Second, watch sum-to-product collapse a quotient identity cleanly: verify
\[
\frac{\sin(3x) - \sin x}{\cos(3x) + \cos x} = \tan x
\]
wherever \(\cos(3x) + \cos x \neq 0\) and \(\cos x \neq 0\).
Apply [REF: sum-to-product.2 | the sine-difference identity] to the numerator and [REF: sum-to-product.3 | the cosine-sum identity] to the denominator, noting \(\frac{3x + x}{2} = 2x\) and \(\frac{3x - x}{2} = x\):
\[
\text{LHS} = \frac{2\cos(2x)\sin x}{2\cos(2x)\cos x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x = \text{RHS}.
\]
Both the \(2\) and the common factor \(\cos(2x)\) cancel on the common domain (since \(\cos(3x) + \cos x = 2\cos(2x)\cos x \neq 0\) guarantees \(\cos(2x) \neq 0\)), leaving \(\tan x\) in two lines without ever expanding \(\sin(3x)\) or \(\cos(3x)\).

Third, [REF: sum-to-product.3 | the cosine-sum identity] explains the physical phenomenon of beats. Suppose two slightly out-of-tune musical notes vibrate as \(\cos(11t)\) and \(\cos(9t)\). When they sound together, the combined wave is \(\cos(11t) + \cos(9t)\). Applying [REF: sum-to-product.3 | the cosine-sum identity] with \(u = 11t\) and \(v = 9t\) gives
\[
\cos(11t) + \cos(9t) = 2\cos(10t)\cos(t).
\]
Read the two factors physically:
- \(\cos(10t)\) is a rapid carrier wave oscillating at the average frequency \(10\).
- \(2\cos(t)\) is a slow amplitude envelope oscillating at the half-difference frequency \(1\).

Because \(|\cos(10t)| \le 1\), the combined wave stays trapped between the dashed curves \(y = 2\cos(t)\) and \(y = -2\cos(t)\), pulsing loudly when \(|\cos(t)| = 1\) and falling silent at the beat nodes where \(\cos(t) = 0\) (at \(t = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \dots\)).
