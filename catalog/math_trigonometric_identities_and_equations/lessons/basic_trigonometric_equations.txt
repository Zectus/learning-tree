=== SECTION 1: Existence criteria and why one inverse value is never enough ===

To solve a trigonometric equation such as \(\sin x = c\), \(\cos x = c\), or \(\tan x = c\), we seek ALL real angles \(x\) (or all angles in a specified interval such as \([0, 2\pi)\)) that make the equation true.

Before doing any calculation, check whether ANY solution exists at all! Because \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) always lie in \([-1, 1]\), while \(\csc x\) and \(\sec x\) always satisfy \(|\csc x| \ge 1\) and \(|\sec x| \ge 1\):
- \(\sin x = c\) and \(\cos x = c\) have solutions if and only if \(|c| \le 1\) (i.e., \(-1 \le c \le 1\)). For example, \(\cos x = 1.4\) and \(\sin x = -2\) immediately have NO real solutions (solution set \(\emptyset\)).
- \(\csc x = c\) and \(\sec x = c\) have solutions if and only if \(|c| \ge 1\). Always convert them immediately to \(\sin x = \frac{1}{c}\) or \(\cos x = \frac{1}{c}\). For instance, \(\sec x = 0.5\) has no real solutions because \(\cos x = 2\) is impossible.
- \(\tan x = c\) and \(\cot x = c\) have solutions for EVERY real number \(c \in \mathbb{R}\). For \(\cot x = c\), rewrite as \(\tan x = \frac{1}{c}\) when \(c \neq 0\); when \(c = 0\), remember directly that \(\cot x = 0 \iff \cos x = 0\), which occurs at \(x = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)).

When a solution DOES exist, inverse trigonometric functions give you a crucial starting point—one principal angle \(\theta_0\)—but they NEVER give you the full solution set by themselves! Look at \(\sin x = 0.4\) on the unit circle. A calculator returns \(\theta_0 = \arcsin(0.4) \approx 0.4115\) radians in Quadrant I. Yet the horizontal line \(y = 0.4\) cuts the unit circle at TWO points: one at \(\theta_0\) in Quadrant I and a symmetric point across the \(y\)-axis at \(\pi - \theta_0 \approx 2.7301\) radians in Quadrant II! And adding any integer multiple of \(2\pi\) lands on those exact same two unit-circle points infinitely many times.

Reporting only \(\theta_0 = \arcsin(c)\) or \(\theta_0 = \arccos(c)\) misses HALF of all solutions on every period. Learning the exact symmetry partner for each function eliminates that error permanently.

=== SECTION 2: General solution families for sine, cosine, and tangent ===

Each of the three primary functions has its own unit-circle symmetry and fundamental period, producing a clean blueprint that turns one principal angle \(\theta_0\) into every solution on \(\mathbb{R}\) (and every solution in \([0, 2\pi)\)).

1. Sine equation \(\sin x = c\) (\(|c| \le 1\)):
Let \(\theta_0 = \arcsin(c) \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]\). Because \(\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta\), the two unit-circle angles sharing the same \(y\)-coordinate \(c\) are \(\theta_0\) and \(\pi - \theta_0\). Adding integer multiples of the period \(2\pi\) gives every real solution:
\[
x = \theta_0 + 2\pi k \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = (\pi - \theta_0) + 2\pi k \qquad (k \in \mathbb{Z}).
\]
(Note: at the extremes \(c = 1\) or \(c = -1\), \(\theta_0\) and \(\pi - \theta_0\) coincide modulo \(2\pi\), collapsing to a single family \(\frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k\) or \(-\frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k\).)
Watch negative \(c\) when listing solutions in \([0, 2\pi)\): if \(c = -\frac{1}{2}\), then \(\theta_0 = -\frac{\pi}{6} \notin [0, 2\pi)\). Add \(2\pi\) to shift it into \([0, 2\pi)\): \(\theta_0 + 2\pi = \frac{11\pi}{6}\), while the partner \(\pi - \left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{7\pi}{6}\) is already in \([0, 2\pi)\). So on \([0, 2\pi)\), \(\sin x = -\frac{1}{2}\) has the two solutions \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\) and \(\frac{11\pi}{6}\).

2. Cosine equation \(\cos x = c\) (\(|c| \le 1\)):
Let \(\theta_0 = \arccos(c) \in [0, \pi]\). Because cosine is even (\(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\)), the two unit-circle angles sharing the same \(x\)-coordinate \(c\) are \(\theta_0\) and \(-\theta_0\) (equivalently \(2\pi - \theta_0\) inside \([0, 2\pi)\)). Thus the general solution on \(\mathbb{R}\) is
\[
x = \theta_0 + 2\pi k \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = -\theta_0 + 2\pi k \qquad (k \in \mathbb{Z}),
\]
often written compactly as \(x = \pm\theta_0 + 2\pi k\). On \([0, 2\pi)\), the two solutions (for \(|c| < 1\)) are \(\theta_0\) and \(2\pi - \theta_0\). (At \(c = 1\) the single solution in \([0, 2\pi)\) is \(0\); at \(c = -1\) it is \(\pi\).)

3. Tangent equation \(\tan x = c\) (\(c \in \mathbb{R}\)):
Let \(\theta_0 = \arctan(c) \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\). Because \(\tan x\) has period \(\pi\)—its values repeat every HALF-turn across the origin—ALL real solutions sit in ONE single family:
\[
x = \theta_0 + \pi k \qquad (k \in \mathbb{Z}).
\]
On \([0, 2\pi)\), this yields two solutions separated by \(\pi\): if \(c \ge 0\), they are \(\theta_0\) and \(\theta_0 + \pi\); if \(c < 0\), \(\theta_0\) is negative, so shifting by \(\pi\) and \(2\pi\) gives \(\theta_0 + \pi\) (Quadrant II) and \(\theta_0 + 2\pi\) (Quadrant IV).

=== SECTION 3: Reciprocal equations and non-special inverse-trig solutions ===

Many basic equations require one quick isolation step or reciprocal conversion before applying [REF: general-solutions | the general-solution formulas].

Example 1 (Reciprocal function): Solve \(\sec x + \sqrt{2} = 0\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and on \([0, 2\pi)\).
Isolate \(\sec x\): \(\sec x = -\sqrt{2}\). Since \(|-\sqrt{2}| \ge 1\), solutions exist. Take reciprocals:
\[
\cos x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.
\]
The principal angle is \(\theta_0 = \arccos\!\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = \frac{3\pi}{4}\). By [REF: general-solutions.2 | the cosine general solution],
\[
x = \frac{3\pi}{4} + 2\pi k \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = -\frac{3\pi}{4} + 2\pi k \qquad (k \in \mathbb{Z}).
\]
On \([0, 2\pi)\), the two solutions are \(x = \frac{3\pi}{4}\) and \(x = 2\pi - \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{5\pi}{4}\).

Example 2 (Non-special exact and numeric solutions): Solve \(5\sin x - 2 = 0\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and on \([0, 2\pi)\).
Isolate \(\sin x\): \(\sin x = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4\). Since \(|0.4| \le 1\), solutions exist, even though \(0.4\) is not a special unit-circle ratio. Leave the principal angle in exact inverse-trig form: \(\theta_0 = \arcsin(0.4)\). By [REF: general-solutions.1 | the sine general solution], the exact solutions on \(\mathbb{R}\) are
\[
x = \arcsin(0.4) + 2\pi k \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = \pi - \arcsin(0.4) + 2\pi k \qquad (k \in \mathbb{Z}).
\]
On \([0, 2\pi)\), the exact solutions are \(x = \arcsin(0.4) \approx 0.4115\) and \(x = \pi - \arcsin(0.4) \approx 2.7301\).

Example 3 (Negative non-special sine value on \([0, 2\pi)\)): Solve \(\sin x = -0.3\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).
Here \(\theta_0 = \arcsin(-0.3) \approx -0.3047\) is negative, so it lies outside \([0, 2\pi)\). Apply the two families and pick the integer \(k\) that lands in \([0, 2\pi)\):
- From \(\pi - \theta_0\): \(\pi - \arcsin(-0.3) = \pi + \arcsin(0.3) \approx 3.4463 \in [0, 2\pi)\) (Quadrant III).
- From \(\theta_0 + 2\pi\): \(2\pi + \arcsin(-0.3) = 2\pi - \arcsin(0.3) \approx 5.9785 \in [0, 2\pi)\) (Quadrant IV).
Notice how writing \(\arcsin(-0.3) = -\arcsin(0.3)\) makes both exact expressions completely transparent!

Equation | Existence condition | Exact solutions in \([0, 2\pi)\) (for \(0 < c < 1\) or \(c > 0\)) | Exact solutions in \([0, 2\pi)\) for \(-c\) (negative RHS)
\(\sin x = c\) | \(\lvert c \rvert \le 1\) | \(\arcsin c,\; \pi - \arcsin c\) | \(\pi + \arcsin c,\; 2\pi - \arcsin c\)
\(\cos x = c\) | \(\lvert c \rvert \le 1\) | \(\arccos c,\; 2\pi - \arccos c\) | \(\arccos(-c),\; 2\pi - \arccos(-c)\)
\(\tan x = c\) | All \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) | \(\arctan c,\; \pi + \arctan c\) | \(\pi - \arctan c,\; 2\pi - \arctan c\)
[/TABLE]
