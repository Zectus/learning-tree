=== SECTION 1: Sine and cosine inequalities on the unit circle ===

Finding when a periodic wave equals a threshold is an equation; finding the entire set of angles where the wave stays ABOVE or BELOW that threshold is a trigonometric inequality. Because \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) oscillate endlessly, their solution sets are not isolated numbers—they are arcs on the unit circle, which unwrap into periodic unions of intervals on \(\mathbb{R}\).

The most reliable workflow for every basic trigonometric inequality is the boundary-and-test-arc method. Watch how it works on \(\sin x > \frac{1}{2}\):
1. Locate the boundary angles by solving the associated equality \(\sin x = \frac{1}{2}\) on one fundamental period \([0, 2\pi)\): \(x = \frac{\pi}{6}\) and \(x = \frac{5\pi}{6}\).
2. Read the valid arc directly on the unit circle: since \(\sin x\) is the \(y\)-coordinate on the unit circle, \(\sin x > \frac{1}{2}\) is the entire upper arc lying strictly above the horizontal line \(y = \frac{1}{2}\), running counterclockwise from \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) to \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\). Because the inequality is strict (\(>\)), the two endpoints are excluded, giving the open interval \(\left(\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).
3. Extend periodically to all of \(\mathbb{R}\) by adding \(2\pi k\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)):
\[
\bigcup_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \left(\frac{\pi}{6} + 2\pi k,\; \frac{5\pi}{6} + 2\pi k\right).
\]

Now watch the wrap-around subtlety when the valid arc crosses the positive \(x\)-axis (\(0 / 2\pi\))! Solve \(\cos x \ge \frac{1}{2}\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and on \([0, 2\pi)\).
- On the unit circle, \(\cos x\) is the horizontal coordinate, so \(\cos x \ge \frac{1}{2}\) is the single contiguous right-hand arc running counterclockwise from \(-\frac{\pi}{3}\) through \(0\) up to \(+\frac{\pi}{3}\) (endpoints INCLUDED because of \(\ge\)).
- On all of \(\mathbb{R}\), writing that contiguous arc directly produces a single clean periodic family:
\[
\bigcup_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \left[-\frac{\pi}{3} + 2\pi k,\; \frac{\pi}{3} + 2\pi k\right].
\]
- On the bounded interval \([0, 2\pi)\), however, the cut at \(0 / 2\pi\) splits that single arc into TWO pieces:
\[
\left[0, \frac{\pi}{3}\right] \cup \left[\frac{5\pi}{3}, 2\pi\right).
\]
Check the brackets at the cut carefully: \(0\) gets a CLOSED bracket (written \([0, \dots\)) because \(0 \in [0, 2\pi)\) AND \(\cos 0 = 1 \ge \frac{1}{2}\); \(2\pi\) gets an OPEN parenthesis (written \(\dots, 2\pi)\)) because \(2\pi\) is outside \([0, 2\pi)\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: boundary-arc-method
label: Workflow
title: The boundary-and-test-arc method for trigonometric inequalities
tone: key

(1) Solve the associated equality on one fundamental period to locate exact boundary angles (and record any vertical asymptotes for \(\tan, \cot, \sec,\) or \(\csc\)).
(2) Shade the valid arcs on the unit circle (or intervals on a one-period graph) and assign open parentheses or closed brackets according to strict (\(<\), \(>\)) versus non-strict (\(\le\), \(\ge\)) signs—asymptotes are ALWAYS open.
(3) Check wrap-around across \(0 / 2\pi\) when restricting to \([0, 2\pi)\) (testing \(x = 0\) explicitly!), and add integer multiples of the period for the general solution on \(\mathbb{R}\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 2: Tangent inequalities and multiple-angle inequalities ===

Two extensions build directly on [REF: boundary-arc-method | the boundary-and-test-arc method]: handling vertical asymptotes in tangent inequalities, and rescaling periodic intervals for multiple-angle inequalities.

Case 1 (Tangent inequalities and asymptote walls): Solve \(\tan x < 1\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and on \([0, 2\pi)\).

On its fundamental period \(\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\), \(\tan x\) increases strictly from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\) and equals \(1\) at \(x = \frac{\pi}{4}\). Therefore \(\tan x < 1\) holds on the entire open interval \(\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{4}\right)\)—stopping at the vertical asymptote \(x = -\frac{\pi}{2}\), which acts as an impassable open wall! Because \(\tan x\) has period \(\pi\), the general solution on \(\mathbb{R}\) is
\[
\bigcup_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \left(-\frac{\pi}{2} + \pi k,\; \frac{\pi}{4} + \pi k\right).
\]
To intersect with \([0, 2\pi)\), list the pieces for \(k = 0, 1, 2\) and trim to \([0, 2\pi)\):
- \(k = 0\): \(\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \cap [0, 2\pi) = \left[0, \frac{\pi}{4}\right)\) (closed at \(0\) since \(\tan 0 = 0 < 1\)).
- \(k = 1\): \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{4}\right)\).
- \(k = 2\): \(\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}, \frac{9\pi}{4}\right) \cap [0, 2\pi) = \left(\frac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi\right)\).
Complete solution on \([0, 2\pi)\): \(\left[0, \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \cup \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{4}\right) \cup \left(\frac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi\right)\). Notice that the asymptotes \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\) stay strictly open!

Case 2 (Multiple-angle inequalities): Solve \(\cos(2x) \le -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and on \([0, 2\pi)\).

Just as in multiple-angle equations, set \(u = 2x\) and write the COMPLETE periodic family for \(u\) BEFORE dividing by \(2\):
1. On one period \([0, 2\pi)\), \(\cos u \le -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) on the closed arc \(\left[\frac{5\pi}{6}, \frac{7\pi}{6}\right]\).
2. Add \(2\pi k\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)):
\[
\frac{5\pi}{6} + 2\pi k \le 2x \le \frac{7\pi}{6} + 2\pi k.
\]
3. Divide all three parts by \(2\) (which is positive, so the \(\le\) signs stay unchanged):
\[
\frac{5\pi}{12} + \pi k \le x \le \frac{7\pi}{12} + \pi k \qquad (k \in \mathbb{Z}).
\]
4. Extract every interval lying in \([0, 2\pi)\) (\(k = 0\) and \(k = 1\)):
\[
\left[\frac{5\pi}{12}, \frac{7\pi}{12}\right] \cup \left[\frac{17\pi}{12}, \frac{19\pi}{12}\right].
\]
(Had you divided \(\left[\frac{5\pi}{6}, \frac{7\pi}{6}\right]\) by \(2\) without including \(+2\pi k\), you would have missed the entire second interval!) The shaded regions below show both solution intervals on \([0, 2\pi)\) where \(y = \cos(2x)\) dips at or below \(y = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\).

=== SECTION 3: Factored and quadratic trigonometric inequalities ===

For quadratic or product trigonometric inequalities, move every term to one side, factor, solve the resulting algebraic inequality for the trigonometric function (or analyze quadrant signs), and then translate back to \(x\) on the unit circle.

[CALLOUT]
id: trig-ineq-pitfalls
label: Common Pitfall
title: Two critical rules for algebraic trigonometric inequalities
tone: caution

(1) Never multiply or divide an inequality by a variable trigonometric factor such as \(\sin x\) or \(\cos x\). Because it is positive on half the circle, negative on the other half (which reverses the inequality!), and zero at two points, dividing by it both loses valid intervals and produces false ones. Always move everything to one side and factor!
(2) Never cross a vertical asymptote of \(\tan x, \cot x, \sec x,\) or \(\csc x\); asymptotes always split solution sets into open-ended intervals.
[/CALLOUT]

Worked example 1 (Quadratic-in-sine inequality): Solve \(2\sin^2 x - \sin x > 0\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).

Factor out \(\sin x\) (respecting [REF: trig-ineq-pitfalls.1 | rule (1)]—do NOT divide by \(\sin x\)!):
\[
\sin x (2\sin x - 1) > 0.
\]
Substitute \(u = \sin x\): the quadratic inequality \(u(2u - 1) > 0\) holds whenever BOTH factors have the same sign, meaning
\[
u < 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad u > \frac{1}{2}.
\]
Now read BOTH conditions on \([0, 2\pi)\):
- \(\sin x > \frac{1}{2}\) gives \(\left(\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\).
- \(\sin x < 0\) gives \((\pi, 2\pi)\) (the entire open lower semicircle, where \(\sin x < 0\) and \(2\sin x - 1 < -1 < 0\), so their product is strictly positive!).
Uniting the two disjoint regions gives the complete solution set on \([0, 2\pi)\):
\[
\left(\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{5\pi}{6}\right) \cup (\pi, 2\pi).
\]
Notice how dividing by \(\sin x\) would have left only \(2\sin x - 1 > 0\) and completely missed the entire interval \((\pi, 2\pi)\)!

Worked example 2 (Product sign check): Solve \(\sin x \cos x < 0\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).

A product is negative precisely when its factors have opposite signs:
- Quadrant II \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right)\): \(\sin x > 0\) and \(\cos x < 0\).
- Quadrant IV \(\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi\right)\): \(\sin x < 0\) and \(\cos x > 0\).
Hence the solution set is \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right) \cup \left(\frac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi\right)\). (You can also confirm this via the double-angle identity \(\frac{1}{2}\sin(2x) < 0 \iff \sin(2x) < 0\).)
