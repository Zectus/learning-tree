=== SECTION 1: Strategic double-angle selection ===

When a trigonometric equation mixes different arguments—such as \(\cos(2x) + \sin x = 0\) or \(\sin(2x) - \cos x = 0\)—blindly rewriting terms can trap you in a tangle of mixed functions. The key is STRATEGIC identity selection: inspect the rest of the equation FIRST, then pick the identity that turns the entire equation into either a single-function polynomial or an immediate common-factor product.

Recall that \(\cos(2x)\) has three equivalent forms: \(\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x\), \(2\cos^2 x - 1\), and \(1 - 2\sin^2 x\).
- If the rest of the equation contains \(\sin x\), choose \(\cos(2x) = 1 - 2\sin^2 x\) so the entire equation becomes a pure quadratic in \(\sin x\).
- If the rest of the equation contains \(\cos x\), choose \(\cos(2x) = 2\cos^2 x - 1\) so the entire equation becomes a pure quadratic in \(\cos x\).
- Choosing \(\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x\) in either situation forces an unnecessary extra Pythagorean substitution.

[CALLOUT]
id: double-angle-strategy
label: Strategy
title: Matching double-angle and half-angle substitutions to the equation
tone: key

(1) \(\cos(2x)\) paired with \(\sin x\): substitute \(\cos(2x) = 1 - 2\sin^2 x\) to form a quadratic in \(\sin x\).
(2) \(\cos(2x)\) paired with \(\cos x\): substitute \(\cos(2x) = 2\cos^2 x - 1\) to form a quadratic in \(\cos x\).
(3) \(\sin(2x)\) paired with \(\sin x\) or \(\cos x\): substitute \(\sin(2x) = 2\sin x \cos x\), move all terms to one side, and factor out the common factor (NEVER divide both sides by \(\sin x\) or \(\cos x\)).
(4) Mixed \(x\) and \(\frac{x}{2}\): view \(x = 2\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\) and apply a double-angle cosine identity to \(\cos x\) (such as \(\cos x = 1 - 2\sin^2(x/2)\) or \(2\cos^2(x/2) - 1\)) so you avoid square roots and extraneous-root squaring completely.
[/CALLOUT]

Worked example 1 (Quadratic reduction via \(\cos(2x)\)): Solve \(\cos(2x) + \sin x = 0\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).

Using [REF: double-angle-strategy.1 | rule (1)], replace \(\cos(2x) = 1 - 2\sin^2 x\):
\[
1 - 2\sin^2 x + \sin x = 0 \implies 2\sin^2 x - \sin x - 1 = 0.
\]
Factor as a quadratic in \(u = \sin x\):
\[
(2\sin x + 1)(\sin x - 1) = 0 \iff \sin x = -\frac{1}{2} \quad \text{or} \quad \sin x = 1.
\]
On \([0, 2\pi)\):
- \(\sin x = 1\) gives \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\).
- \(\sin x = -\frac{1}{2}\) gives \(x = \frac{7\pi}{6}\) and \(x = \frac{11\pi}{6}\).
The complete solution set on \([0, 2\pi)\) is \(\left\{\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{7\pi}{6}, \frac{11\pi}{6}\right\}\), matching the three \(x\)-intercepts plotted below.

Worked example 2 (Common-factor split via \(\sin(2x)\)): Solve \(\sin(2x) - \cos x = 0\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).

By [REF: double-angle-strategy.3 | rule (3)], rewrite \(\sin(2x) = 2\sin x \cos x\) and factor out \(\cos x\):
\[
2\sin x \cos x - \cos x = 0 \implies \cos x (2\sin x - 1) = 0.
\]
- \(\cos x = 0\) gives \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}\).
- \(2\sin x - 1 = 0 \iff \sin x = \frac{1}{2}\) gives \(x = \frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{5\pi}{6}\).
Complete solution set on \([0, 2\pi)\): \(\left\{\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{6}, \frac{3\pi}{2}\right\}\). (Had you divided both sides by \(\cos x\), you would have silently erased \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\)!)

=== SECTION 2: Sum-to-product factoring and uniting solution families ===

Double-angle formulas do not directly handle mixed-frequency equations such as \(\sin(5x) + \sin x = 0\) or \(\cos(3x) - \cos x = 0\). Whenever a sum or difference of two sines or two cosines equals \(0\), the sum-to-product identities factor the left-hand side directly into a product of two multiple-angle factors, which you solve via the multiple-angle method.

Worked example 1 (Disjoint solution families): Solve \(\sin(5x) + \sin x = 0\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).

Apply \(\sin u + \sin v = 2\sin\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}\right)\) with \(u = 5x\) and \(v = x\):
\[
2\sin(3x)\cos(2x) = 0 \iff \sin(3x) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \cos(2x) = 0.
\]
Solve each multiple-angle equation completely on \(\mathbb{R}\) first, then extract all roots in \([0, 2\pi)\):
- \(\sin(3x) = 0 \implies 3x = \pi k \implies x = \dfrac{\pi k}{3}\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)). For \(k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\), this gives \(6\) roots:
\[
0, \;\frac{\pi}{3}, \;\frac{2\pi}{3}, \;\pi, \;\frac{4\pi}{3}, \;\frac{5\pi}{3}.
\]
- \(\cos(2x) = 0 \implies 2x = \frac{\pi}{2} + \pi n \implies x = \dfrac{\pi}{4} + \dfrac{\pi n}{2}\) (\(n \in \mathbb{Z}\)). For \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\), this gives \(4\) roots:
\[
\frac{\pi}{4}, \;\frac{3\pi}{4}, \;\frac{5\pi}{4}, \;\frac{7\pi}{4}.
\]
None of the odd multiples of \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) match any multiple of \(\frac{\pi}{3}\), so the two families are disjoint and yield \(6 + 4 = 10\) distinct solutions on \([0, 2\pi)\).

Worked example 2 (Overlapping solution families!): Solve \(\cos(3x) - \cos x = 0\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and on \([0, 2\pi)\).

Apply \(\cos u - \cos v = -2\sin\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}\right)\):
\[
-2\sin(2x)\sin x = 0 \iff \sin(2x) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \sin x = 0.
\]
- \(\sin(2x) = 0 \implies 2x = \pi k \implies x = \dfrac{\pi k}{2}\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)). On \([0, 2\pi)\): \(\left\{0, \frac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}\right\}\).
- \(\sin x = 0 \implies x = \pi n\) (\(n \in \mathbb{Z}\)). On \([0, 2\pi)\): \(\{0, \pi\}\).

Look closely before counting! Every solution \(x = \pi n\) of the second family is ALREADY contained in the first family \(x = \frac{\pi k}{2}\) (simply take even integers \(k = 2n\)). Therefore the complete general solution simplifies cleanly to a single family \(x = \frac{\pi k}{2}\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)), giving \(4\) distinct solutions on \([0, 2\pi)\)—NOT \(6\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: union-check
label: Common Pitfall
title: Removing duplicate roots when uniting solution families
tone: caution

After factoring a trigonometric equation into two (or more) multiple-angle factors, always compare the resulting solution families before counting or reporting the union. One family can be partially or completely absorbed inside another (for example, \(\{x = \pi n\} \subset \{x = \frac{\pi k}{2}\}\)). Report every distinct solution once without omitting periods or double-counting overlaps.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Half-angle, tangent, and domain-checked equations ===

Two remaining situations call for extra care: equations pairing \(x\) with \(\frac{x}{2}\), and equations containing \(\tan, \cot, \sec,\) or \(\csc\) where hidden denominators restrict the domain.

Worked example 1 (Half-angle equation without radicals): Solve \(\cos x + \sin\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) = 1\) on \([0, 2\pi)\).

Using [REF: double-angle-strategy.4 | rule (4)], write \(\cos x = 1 - 2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\) instead of introducing a \(\pm\sqrt{\cdot}\) half-angle radical:
\[
1 - 2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + \sin\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) = 1 \implies \sin\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\!\left(1 - 2\sin\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\right) = 0.
\]
Let \(u = \frac{x}{2}\). As \(x\) spans \([0, 2\pi)\), \(u\) spans \([0, \pi)\):
- \(\sin u = 0\) on \([0, \pi)\) gives \(u = 0 \implies x = 0\).
- \(\sin u = \frac{1}{2}\) on \([0, \pi)\) gives \(u = \frac{\pi}{6}\) or \(u = \frac{5\pi}{6} \implies x = \frac{\pi}{3}\) or \(x = \frac{5\pi}{3}\).
All three steps are completely reversible, giving the verified solution set \(\left\{0, \frac{\pi}{3}, \frac{5\pi}{3}\right\}\).

Worked example 2 (Domain exclusions and extraneous roots): Solve \(\tan(2x) = \sec(2x) - 1\) on \([0, \pi)\).

1. Record the ORIGINAL domain restrictions first: both \(\tan(2x) = \frac{\sin(2x)}{\cos(2x)}\) and \(\sec(2x) = \frac{1}{\cos(2x)}\) require \(\cos(2x) \ne 0\), so \(2x \ne \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}\), meaning \(x \ne \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{3\pi}{4}\).
2. Multiply both sides by \(\cos(2x)\) (a potentially non-reversible step!) to get
\[
\sin(2x) = 1 - \cos(2x).
\]
3. Square both sides (another non-reversible step!) and use \(\sin^2(2x) = 1 - \cos^2(2x)\):
\[
1 - \cos^2(2x) = 1 - 2\cos(2x) + \cos^2(2x) \implies 2\cos^2(2x) - 2\cos(2x) = 0 \implies 2\cos(2x)\bigl(\cos(2x) - 1\bigr) = 0.
\]
4. Candidates on \([0, \pi)\):
- \(\cos(2x) = 0 \implies x = \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{3\pi}{4}\). REJECT BOTH—they violate the domain \(\cos(2x) \ne 0\)!
- \(\cos(2x) = 1 \implies 2x = 0 \implies x = 0\). Check in the original equation: \(\tan 0 = 0\) and \(\sec 0 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0\). Valid!
So \(x = 0\) is the ONLY solution on \([0, \pi)\).

Worked example 3 (Avoid artificially narrowing the domain): Solve \(\tan(2x) = \cot x\) on \([0, \pi)\).

The original domain requires \(\cos(2x) \ne 0\) (\(x \ne \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{3\pi}{4}\)) and \(\sin x \ne 0\) (\(x \ne 0\)). Notice that \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\) IS IN THE DOMAIN because \(\tan(\pi) = 0\) and \(\cot(\frac{\pi}{2}) = 0\) are both well defined! Converting directly to sines and cosines preserves \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\):
\[
\frac{\sin(2x)}{\cos(2x)} = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} \implies \sin(2x)\sin x - \cos(2x)\cos x = 0 \implies -\cos(2x + x) = 0 \implies \cos(3x) = 0.
\]
Solving \(3x = \frac{\pi}{2} + \pi k\) gives \(x = \frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{6}\) on \([0, \pi)\). Checking all three confirms that none violates the domain—all three are valid! (Had you rewritten \(\cot x = \frac{1}{\tan x}\) and \(\tan(2x) = \frac{2\tan x}{1 - \tan^2 x}\), you would have accidentally excluded \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\) where \(\tan x\) is undefined!)

[CALLOUT]
id: domain-extran-check
label: Common Pitfall
title: Domain checks and preserving valid roots
tone: caution

(1) Whenever an equation contains \(\tan, \cot, \sec,\) or \(\csc\), list the original denominator exclusions immediately and reject any candidate root that makes an original term undefined.
(2) Whenever you multiply by a variable expression or square both sides, substitute every candidate back into the ORIGINAL equation to filter out extraneous roots.
(3) Converting to \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) is safer than substituting \(\cot x = \frac{1}{\tan x}\), which can artificially drop valid roots where \(\cos x = 0\).
[/CALLOUT]
