=== SECTION 1: Organizing the fundamental identity toolkit ===

When you simplify a polynomial or rational expression in algebra, you rewrite it into an equivalent form that is easier to read, factor, or evaluate. Trigonometric expressions work the same way, except the six trigonometric functions are tightly linked to one another by unit-circle geometry. An expression like \(\frac{\sec x - \cos x}{\tan x}\) looks complicated at first glance, yet on its domain it collapses all the way down to \(\sin x\). Making that collapse routine requires organizing the basic relationships you already know from unit-circle definitions into a single working toolkit.

An identity is an equation between two expressions that holds for every value of the variable where both sides are defined. The first four groups of fundamental identities follow directly from the unit-circle definitions of the six functions and from symmetry across the axes and across the line \(y = x\).

[CALLOUT]
id: basic-identities
label: Reference
title: Reciprocal, quotient, even/odd, and cofunction identities
tone: key

For all angles \(x\) (in radians) where both sides are defined:

(1) Reciprocal identities: \(\csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}\), \(\sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}\), \(\cot x = \frac{1}{\tan x}\), and equivalently \(\sin x = \frac{1}{\csc x}\), \(\cos x = \frac{1}{\sec x}\), \(\tan x = \frac{1}{\cot x}\).
(2) Quotient identities: \(\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) and \(\cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\).
(3) Even/odd identities: \(\cos(-x) = \cos x\) and \(\sec(-x) = \sec x\) (even); \(\sin(-x) = -\sin x\), \(\csc(-x) = -\csc x\), \(\tan(-x) = -\tan x\), and \(\cot(-x) = -\cot x\) (odd).
(4) Cofunction identities: \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos x\), \(\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \sin x\), \(\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cot x\), \(\cot\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \tan x\), \(\sec\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \csc x\), and \(\csc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \sec x\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how each group handles a different kind of clutter in an expression. [REF: basic-identities.1 | The reciprocal identities] and [REF: basic-identities.2 | quotient identities] let you trade any combination of \(\tan x\), \(\cot x\), \(\sec x\), and \(\csc x\) for expressions built solely out of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\). [REF: basic-identities.3 | The even/odd identities] eliminate negative arguments like \(-x\), while [REF: basic-identities.4 | the cofunction identities] remove shifts of the form \(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\).

Watch these four groups work together on a concrete expression:
\[
\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)\tan(-x)}{\csc(-x)}.
\]
First clear the shifted and negative arguments. By [REF: basic-identities.4 | the cofunction identity], \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos x\). By [REF: basic-identities.3 | the odd-function identities], \(\tan(-x) = -\tan x\) and \(\csc(-x) = -\csc x\). The two negative signs cancel:
\[
\frac{\cos x \cdot (-\tan x)}{-\csc x} = \frac{\cos x \tan x}{\csc x}.
\]
Now rewrite \(\tan x\) and \(\csc x\) in terms of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) using [REF: basic-identities.2 | the quotient identity] and [REF: basic-identities.1 | the reciprocal identity]:
\[
\frac{\cos x \cdot \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}}{\frac{1}{\sin x}} = \sin x \cdot \sin x = \sin^2 x.
\]
Here \(\sin^2 x\) is standard shorthand for \((\sin x)^2\) (never \(\sin(x^2)\)). Keep track of domain validity too: in the original expression, \(\tan(-x)\) requires \(\cos x \neq 0\) and \(\csc(-x)\) requires \(\sin x \neq 0\), so the simplified form \(\sin^2 x\) agrees with the original expression at every \(x\) where neither \(\sin x\) nor \(\cos x\) is \(0\) (that is, \(x \neq \frac{k\pi}{2}\) for any integer \(k\)).

[TABLE]
Identity family | Main role in simplification | Example replacement
Reciprocal | Remove \(\sec x\), \(\csc x\), \(\cot x\) or turn fractions into single functions | \(\frac{1}{\sec x} \to \cos x\)
Quotient | Express \(\tan x\) and \(\cot x\) via \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) | \(\cos x \tan x \to \sin x\)
Even/odd | Clear negative angle inputs \(-x\) | \(\sin(-x)\cos(-x) \to -\sin x \cos x\)
Cofunction | Clear \(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\) angle shifts | \(\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) \to \cot x\)
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 2: The three Pythagorean identities and their rearranged forms ===

On the unit circle, every angle \(x\) corresponds to a point \((\cos x, \sin x)\) at distance \(1\) from the origin, which gives the fundamental Pythagorean identity
\[
\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1.
\]
This equation holds for every real number \(x\). From this single identity, two more Pythagorean identities follow directly by dividing every term by \(\cos^2 x\) or by \(\sin^2 x\).

First, suppose \(\cos x \neq 0\) (meaning \(x \neq \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\) for integers \(k\)). Dividing both sides of \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\) by \(\cos^2 x\) gives
\[
\frac{\sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} + \frac{\cos^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x}.
\]
Applying [REF: basic-identities.2 | the quotient identity] \(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x\) and [REF: basic-identities.1 | the reciprocal identity] \(\frac{1}{\cos x} = \sec x\) produces
\[
\tan^2 x + 1 = \sec^2 x, \qquad \text{usually written } 1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x.
\]
Second, suppose \(\sin x \neq 0\) (meaning \(x \neq k\pi\) for integers \(k\)). Dividing both sides of \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\) by \(\sin^2 x\) gives
\[
\frac{\sin^2 x}{\sin^2 x} + \frac{\cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{1}{\sin^2 x},
\]
which simplifies via \(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x\) and \(\frac{1}{\sin x} = \csc x\) to
\[
1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x.
\]
Notice why remembering the division step is better than trying to memorize signs blindly: you can re-derive both equations in two seconds, and you automatically see their exact domains—\(1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x\) holds precisely where \(\cos x \neq 0\), while \(1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x\) holds precisely where \(\sin x \neq 0\).

[CALLOUT]
id: pythagorean-identities
label: Theorem
title: The three Pythagorean identities and key rearrangements
tone: key

(1) \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\) (for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\)), rearranging to \(\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x\) and \(\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x\).
(2) \(1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x\) (for \(\cos x \neq 0\)), rearranging to \(\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1\) and \(\sec^2 x - \tan^2 x = 1\).
(3) \(1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x\) (for \(\sin x \neq 0\)), rearranging to \(\cot^2 x = \csc^2 x - 1\) and \(\csc^2 x - \cot^2 x = 1\).
[/CALLOUT]

In practice, trigonometric expressions rarely hand you \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x\) already grouped side by side. More often you meet one of the rearranged differences, or its negative. Watch the signs closely:
- \(1 - \cos^2 x = \sin^2 x\), so \(\cos^2 x - 1 = -\sin^2 x\).
- \(\sec^2 x - 1 = \tan^2 x\), so \(1 - \sec^2 x = -\tan^2 x\).
- \(\csc^2 x - \cot^2 x = 1\), so \(\cot^2 x - \csc^2 x = -1\).

For example, consider simplifying
\[
\frac{1 - \sec^2 x}{\tan x} + \tan x \cos^2 x.
\]
In the first term, recognize \(1 - \sec^2 x = -(\sec^2 x - 1) = -\tan^2 x\) from [REF: pythagorean-identities.2 | the secant-tangent Pythagorean identity]. Provided \(\tan x \neq 0\) and \(\cos x \neq 0\), we get
\[
\frac{-\tan^2 x}{\tan x} + \tan x \cos^2 x = -\tan x + \tan x \cos^2 x.
\]
Now factor out \(-\tan x\):
\[
-\tan x(1 - \cos^2 x) = -\tan x \sin^2 x.
\]
Because \(1 - \cos^2 x = \sin^2 x\) by [REF: pythagorean-identities.1 | the sine-cosine Pythagorean identity], a two-term fraction collapses into a single product \(-\tan x \sin^2 x\) (or \(-\frac{\sin^3 x}{\cos x}\)).

=== SECTION 3: Algebraic moves for simplifying trigonometric expressions ===

Every factoring and fraction technique from polynomial algebra applies directly when a trigonometric function plays the role of the variable. Rather than guessing identities at random, look at the algebraic structure of the expression first. Four moves handle almost every simplification you will encounter.

Move 1: Factor out a greatest common trigonometric factor (GCF).
Look at \(\sec^2 x \sin x - \sin x\). Both terms contain \(\sin x\), so factor it out before touching any identity:
\[
\sec^2 x \sin x - \sin x = \sin x(\sec^2 x - 1) = \sin x \tan^2 x.
\]
Factoring exposed \(\sec^2 x - 1\), which [REF: pythagorean-identities.2 | the second Pythagorean identity] immediately converts to \(\tan^2 x\).

Move 2: Factor differences of squares and quadratic trinomials.
Consider \(\cos^4 x - \sin^4 x\). Treating \(\cos^2 x\) and \(\sin^2 x\) as the two squared quantities, apply \(A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)\):
\[
\cos^4 x - \sin^4 x = (\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x)(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x).
\]
By [REF: pythagorean-identities.1 | the fundamental Pythagorean identity], the second factor equals \(1\), leaving
\[
\cos^4 x - \sin^4 x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x.
\]
If you want the result in terms of \(\cos x\) alone, substitute \(\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x\) to obtain \(2\cos^2 x - 1\).

Quadratic trinomials work the same way. To simplify
\[
\frac{\sin^2 x + 2\sin x - 3}{\sin^2 x - 1},
\]
think of \(u = \sin x\). The numerator factors as \((u + 3)(u - 1)\) and the denominator as \((u - 1)(u + 1)\):
\[
\frac{(\sin x + 3)(\sin x - 1)}{(\sin x - 1)(\sin x + 1)} = \frac{\sin x + 3}{\sin x + 1},
\]
valid wherever \(\sin x \neq 1\) and \(\sin x \neq -1\) (that is, \(\cos x \neq 0\)).

Move 3: Combine fractions over a common denominator.
Whenever you see a sum or difference of two trigonometric fractions, adding them often creates a Pythagorean combination in the numerator or denominator. Simplify
\[
\frac{1}{1 - \sin x} + \frac{1}{1 + \sin x}.
\]
The common denominator is \((1 - \sin x)(1 + \sin x) = 1 - \sin^2 x\):
\[
\frac{(1 + \sin x) + (1 - \sin x)}{(1 - \sin x)(1 + \sin x)} = \frac{2}{1 - \sin^2 x} = \frac{2}{\cos^2 x} = 2\sec^2 x,
\]
valid whenever \(\cos x \neq 0\) (which is precisely the condition \(1 - \sin x \neq 0\) and \(1 + \sin x \neq 0\)).

Move 4: Convert everything to \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\).
When an expression mixes several functions and no immediate factoring or Pythagorean pattern stands out, converting every function into \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) reduces six symbols to two and exposes hidden cancellations. Return to the opening example \(\frac{\sec x - \cos x}{\tan x}\):
\[
\frac{\sec x - \cos x}{\tan x} = \frac{\frac{1}{\cos x} - \cos x}{\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}} = \frac{\frac{1 - \cos^2 x}{\cos x}}{\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}} = \frac{1 - \cos^2 x}{\sin x} = \frac{\sin^2 x}{\sin x} = \sin x,
\]
for all \(x\) with \(\sin x \neq 0\) and \(\cos x \neq 0\).

=== SECTION 4: Domain validity and illegal algebra pitfalls ===

Algebraic simplification can change the apparent domain of a trigonometric expression, and careless algebra can destroy equivalence completely. Both issues are worth inspecting directly so they never slip past you.

First, track excluded values whenever a factor cancels from a denominator. Consider
\[
f(x) = \frac{\sin^2 x}{1 - \cos x}.
\]
Rewriting the numerator with [REF: pythagorean-identities.1 | the sine-cosine Pythagorean identity] and factoring as a difference of squares gives
\[
\frac{\sin^2 x}{1 - \cos x} = \frac{1 - \cos^2 x}{1 - \cos x} = \frac{(1 - \cos x)(1 + \cos x)}{1 - \cos x} = 1 + \cos x \qquad (\cos x \neq 1).
\]
The expression \(1 + \cos x\) is defined at \(x = 0\), where it equals \(2\), whereas the original expression \(\frac{\sin^2 x}{1 - \cos x}\) gives \(\frac{0}{0}\) and is undefined at every \(x = 2k\pi\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)). Therefore \(\frac{\sin^2 x}{1 - \cos x} = 1 + \cos x\) is an identity only on the domain \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \neq 2k\pi\}\). Any time you cancel a trigonometric factor from numerator and denominator—or replace \(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) with \(\tan x\)—keep note of the values where a denominator vanished in the original expression.

Second, guard against two pervasive algebraic errors that routinely ruin trigonometric simplifications.

[CALLOUT]
id: algebra-pitfalls
label: Common Pitfall
title: Two illegal moves in trigonometric algebra
tone: caution

(1) Squares do not distribute across sums or differences: \((\sin x + \cos x)^2 = \sin^2 x + 2\sin x \cos x + \cos^2 x = 1 + 2\sin x \cos x\), which is NOT equal to \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\). Likewise, \(\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 x} = |\cos x|\), NOT \(1 - \sin x\).
(2) Terms cannot be canceled across addition or subtraction: in \(\frac{\sin x + \cos x}{\sin x}\), you cannot cancel \(\sin x\) to get \(\cos x\). Instead split the fraction into \(\frac{\sin x}{\sin x} + \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = 1 + \cot x\) (for \(\sin x \neq 0\)).
[/CALLOUT]

The graph below makes [REF: algebra-pitfalls.1 | the squaring error] unmistakable. While \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x\) stays pinned at the constant horizontal line \(y = 1\), the true expansion \((\sin x + \cos x)^2 = 1 + 2\sin x\cos x\) oscillates between \(0\) and \(2\). At \(x = \frac{\pi}{4}\), for instance, \((\sin \frac{\pi}{4} + \cos \frac{\pi}{4})^2 = (\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2})^2 = (\sqrt{2})^2 = 2\), double the value \(1\). At \(x = \frac{3\pi}{4}\), \(\sin \frac{3\pi}{4} + \cos \frac{3\pi}{4} = 0\), so the square drops all the way to \(0\).

When you expand a squared sum or difference of trigonometric functions, keeping the middle cross term often produces clean cancellations rather than clutter. For example,
\[
(\sin x + \cos x)^2 + (\sin x - \cos x)^2 = (1 + 2\sin x\cos x) + (1 - 2\sin x\cos x) = 2.
\]
Here the cross terms \(+2\sin x\cos x\) and \(-2\sin x\cos x\) cancel legitimately only after both squares are expanded properly.
