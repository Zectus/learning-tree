=== SECTION 1: Where weak induction stalls and the Principle of Strong Induction ===

Ordinary (weak) mathematical induction proves \(P(k + 1)\) using only the single immediately preceding statement \(P(k)\). Sometimes, however, the natural way to build object \(k + 1\) does not come from \(k\) at all—it reaches further back to \(k - 1\), \(k - 2\), or even to arbitrary smaller integers between \(n_0\) and \(k\).

Consider the postage-stamp problem: suppose you have an unlimited supply of \(3\)-cent and \(5\)-cent stamps. Can you form every integer amount \(n \ge 8\) cents? Check the first few amounts:
\[
8 = 3 + 5, \qquad 9 = 3 + 3 + 3, \qquad 10 = 5 + 5, \qquad 11 = 8 + 3, \qquad 12 = 9 + 3, \qquad 13 = 10 + 3.
\]
Look at what happened starting at \(11\): to make \(11\), we did not modify a representation of \(10\); we took our representation of \(8 = 11 - 3\) and added one \(3\)-cent stamp. To make \(12\), we added a \(3\)-cent stamp to \(9\). In general, to prove \(P(k + 1)\)—"the amount \(k + 1\) can be written as \(3a + 5b\) with integers \(a, b \ge 0\)"—we want to invoke \(P(k - 2)\), three steps earlier, not \(P(k)\).

Strong induction (also called complete induction) grants us access to all earlier statements from \(n_0\) up to \(k\) at once.

[CALLOUT]
id: strong-induction
label: Principle
title: Principle of Strong (Complete) Induction
tone: key

Let \(n_0 \in \mathbb{Z}\) be a fixed starting integer, and let \(P(n)\) be a statement for every integer \(n \ge n_0\). Suppose that:

(1) Base case(s): \(P(n_0)\) is true (and, if the inductive step only applies once \(k + 1 \ge n_0 + b\), all explicit base cases \(P(n_0), P(n_0 + 1), \dots, P(n_0 + b - 1)\) are true).
(2) Strong inductive step: For every integer \(k \ge n_0 + b - 1\) (with \(b \ge 1\)), if \(P(j)\) is true for all integers \(j\) satisfying \(n_0 \le j \le k\), then \(P(k + 1)\) is true.

Then \(P(n)\) is true for every integer \(n \ge n_0\).
[/CALLOUT]

Why does [REF: strong-induction.1 | the base-case clause] sometimes require multiple explicit base cases? Because the strong inductive hypothesis only covers integers \(j\) with \(n_0 \le j \le k\). If your argument for \(P(k + 1)\) looks back by a fixed lag \(L\)—meaning it invokes \(P(k + 1 - L)\)—then that earlier index lies inside the valid range \([n_0, k]\) only when \(k + 1 - L \ge n_0\), i.e., when \(k + 1 \ge n_0 + L\). Every integer \(n\) from \(n_0\) up to \(n_0 + L - 1\) cannot be reached by looking back \(L\) steps, so all \(L\) of those starting values must be verified explicitly as base cases.

Let us complete the postage-stamp proof using [REF: strong-induction | strong induction] with \(n_0 = 8\) and lag \(L = 3\).

Claim: Every integer \(n \ge 8\) can be written as \(n = 3a + 5b\) for some integers \(a, b \ge 0\).

Proof: Let \(P(n)\) be the statement that \(n = 3a + 5b\) for some integers \(a, b \ge 0\).

Base cases (\(n = 8, 9, 10\)):
- \(n = 8 = 3(1) + 5(1)\),
- \(n = 9 = 3(3) + 5(0)\),
- \(n = 10 = 3(0) + 5(2)\).
Thus \(P(8)\), \(P(9)\), and \(P(10)\) all hold.

Strong inductive step: Fix an integer \(k \ge 10\) and assume \(P(j)\) holds for every integer \(j\) with \(8 \le j \le k\). Consider \(k + 1\). Because \(k \ge 10\), the integer \(j = (k + 1) - 3 = k - 2\) satisfies \(8 \le k - 2 \le k\). By the strong inductive hypothesis applied at \(j = k - 2\), there exist integers \(a, b \ge 0\) such that \(k - 2 = 3a + 5b\). Adding \(3\) to both sides gives
\[
k + 1 = (k - 2) + 3 = 3(a + 1) + 5b,
\]
with \(a + 1 \ge 1 > 0\) and \(b \ge 0\). Thus \(P(k + 1)\) holds. By [REF: strong-induction | strong induction], \(P(n)\) is true for all \(n \ge 8\).

Notice that checking only \(n = 8\) and \(n = 9\) would leave a fatal gap: at \(k = 9\), trying to prove \(P(10)\) by looking back \(3\) steps reaches \(10 - 3 = 7 < 8\), which is outside the domain of the theorem (and indeed \(7\) cannot be formed from \(3\)-cent and \(5\)-cent stamps).

Strong induction with a fixed lag is equally essential for multi-step sequences. Define the Fibonacci sequence by \(F_1 = 1\), \(F_2 = 1\), and \(F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}\) for all \(n \ge 3\). Let us prove \(F_n < 2^n\) for all \(n \ge 1\). Because the recurrence for \(F_{k+1} = F_k + F_{k-1}\) looks back \(2\) steps, we check two base cases:
- \(n = 1\): \(F_1 = 1 < 2 = 2^1\),
- \(n = 2\): \(F_2 = 1 < 4 = 2^2\).
Now fix \(k \ge 2\) and assume \(F_j < 2^j\) for all \(1 \le j \le k\). Since \(k \ge 2\), both \(k\) and \(k - 1\) lie in \(\{1, \dots, k\}\), so the strong inductive hypothesis applies to both:
\[
F_{k+1} = F_k + F_{k-1} < 2^k + 2^{k-1} < 2^k + 2^k = 2^{k+1}.
\]
Hence \(F_n < 2^n\) for every integer \(n \ge 1\).

=== SECTION 2: Arbitrary smaller splits and prime factorization ===

In fixed-lag problems like postage stamps or Fibonacci bounds, you know in advance how far back the step reaches. In another large class of problems, the inductive step breaks \(k + 1\) into one or more strictly smaller pieces whose exact sizes cannot be predicted in advance—they could lie anywhere between \(n_0\) and \(k\). Strong induction handles this effortlessly because its hypothesis covers the entire interval \([n_0, k]\).

The flagship example is the existence of prime factorization. Recall that an integer \(p \ge 2\) is prime if its only positive divisors are \(1\) and \(p\); an integer \(n \ge 2\) that is not prime is composite, meaning \(n = ab\) for some integers \(a, b\) with \(2 \le a < n\) and \(2 \le b < n\).

[CALLOUT]
id: prime-factorization
label: Theorem
title: Existence of prime factorization
tone: key

Every integer \(n \ge 2\) is either a prime number or a product of prime numbers.
[/CALLOUT]

Proof: Let \(P(n)\) be the statement that \(n\) is prime or a product of primes, for \(n \ge 2\).

Base case (\(n = 2\)): The integer \(2\) is prime, so \(P(2)\) holds.

Strong inductive step: Fix an integer \(k \ge 2\) and assume \(P(j)\) holds for every integer \(j\) satisfying \(2 \le j \le k\). Consider \(k + 1\). There are two cases:
- Case 1: \(k + 1\) is prime. Then \(P(k + 1)\) holds immediately.
- Case 2: \(k + 1\) is composite. By definition of compositeness, there exist integers \(a\) and \(b\) such that \(k + 1 = ab\) and \(2 \le a \le k\), \(2 \le b \le k\). Because both \(a\) and \(b\) lie in the range \([2, k]\), the strong inductive hypothesis yields both \(P(a)\) and \(P(b)\): each of \(a\) and \(b\) is either prime or a product of primes. Multiplying their prime factorizations together writes \(k + 1 = ab\) as a product of primes, so \(P(k + 1)\) holds.

In both cases \(P(k + 1)\) holds. By [REF: strong-induction | strong induction], every integer \(n \ge 2\) is either prime or a product of primes.

Pause and notice two crucial things about this proof. First, only ONE base case (\(n = 2\)) was needed! Why? Because for every integer \(k \ge 2\), if \(k + 1\) is composite, its proper factors \(a\) and \(b\) automatically satisfy \(2 \le a, b \le k\)—they can never dip below \(n_0 = 2\). Second, ordinary induction would be completely helpless here: factoring \(k + 1 = 15 = 3 \cdot 5\) tells you nothing about \(k = 14\); you need the result for \(3\) and \(5\), which sit far back in the range \([2, 14]\). (This proof establishes existence of a prime factorization; proving uniqueness—the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic—requires additional divisibility properties of primes and belongs to number theory.)

=== SECTION 3: The Well-Ordering Principle and minimal counterexample proofs ===

Induction climbs upward from a base case. There is a twin principle that looks in the opposite direction—downward to the smallest element of a nonempty set of natural numbers—and turns out to have the exact same logical power. Throughout this section, let \(\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: wop
label: Principle
title: Well-Ordering Principle (WOP)
tone: key

Every nonempty subset \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) has a least element; that is, there exists \(m \in S\) such that \(m \le s\) for every \(s \in S\).
[/CALLOUT]

Do not take [REF: wop | the Well-Ordering Principle] for granted as a property of all ordered number sets—it is special to \(\mathbb{N}\) (and any set bounded below in \(\mathbb{Z}\), such as \(\{n \in \mathbb{Z} : n \ge n_0\}\)). In \(\mathbb{Z}\), the nonempty subset of negative integers \(\{-1, -2, -3, \dots\}\) has no least element. In the positive real numbers \(\mathbb{R}^+\), the open interval \((0, 1)\) is nonempty and bounded below by \(0\), yet it has no least element: for any \(x \in (0, 1)\), the smaller number \(x/2\) still belongs to \((0, 1)\). The same fails in the positive rationals \(\mathbb{Q}^+\). It is the discrete step size of \(\mathbb{N}\) together with its lower bound \(0\) that guarantees a minimum.

In proofs, [REF: wop | WOP] is usually deployed in one of two ways: either directly, by picking the smallest element of a carefully constructed set of natural numbers, or indirectly, as a minimal-counterexample proof by contradiction.

First, let us see a direct application of [REF: wop | WOP] that proves the existence part of the Integer Division Algorithm.

[CALLOUT]
id: division-alg
label: Theorem
title: Division algorithm (existence)
tone: key

For every integer \(a\) and every positive integer \(d \ge 1\), there exist integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that
\[
a = dq + r \qquad \text{and} \qquad 0 \le r < d.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Proof: Fix \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(d \ge 1\). Define the set of nonnegative remainders obtainable by subtracting integer multiples of \(d\) from \(a\):
\[
S = \{a - dq : q \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ and } a - dq \ge 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{N}.
\]
First check that \(S\) is nonempty—this check is mandatory before invoking [REF: wop | WOP]! If \(a \ge 0\), taking \(q = 0\) gives \(a - d(0) = a \ge 0\), so \(a \in S\). If \(a < 0\), taking \(q = a\) gives \(a - da = a(1 - d) \ge 0\) (since \(a < 0\) and \(1 - d \le 0\)), so \(a(1 - d) \in S\). Thus \(S\) is always a nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{N}\).

By [REF: wop | the Well-Ordering Principle], \(S\) has a least element \(r \in S\). Since \(r \in S\), we have \(r \ge 0\) and \(r = a - dq\) for some \(q \in \mathbb{Z}\), meaning \(a = dq + r\). It remains to prove \(r < d\). Suppose for contradiction that \(r \ge d\). Then
\[
r - d = (a - dq) - d = a - d(q + 1) \ge 0.
\]
Since \(q + 1 \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(r - d \ge 0\), the number \(r - d\) belongs to \(S\). Yet \(d \ge 1\) implies \(r - d < r\), contradicting the minimality of \(r\) in \(S\). Therefore \(0 \le r < d\).

Second, let us see the minimal-counterexample pattern on a classic result: proving \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational. Suppose for contradiction that \(\sqrt{2} = a/b\) for positive integers \(a, b\). Then the set
\[
C = \{b \in \mathbb{Z}^+ : \text{there exists } a \in \mathbb{Z}^+ \text{ with } a^2 = 2b^2\}
\]
is a nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{N}\). By [REF: wop | WOP], \(C\) has a least element \(b_0\), with a matching \(a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}^+\) satisfying \(a_0^2 = 2b_0^2\). Then \(a_0^2\) is even, which forces \(a_0\) to be even (the square of an odd integer is odd), so \(a_0 = 2a_1\) for some \(a_1 \in \mathbb{Z}^+\). Substituting gives \(4a_1^2 = 2b_0^2\), hence \(b_0^2 = 2a_1^2\). By the same reasoning, \(b_0\) is even, so \(b_0 = 2b_1\) with \(b_1 \in \mathbb{Z}^+\). Substituting again gives \(4b_1^2 = 2a_1^2\), hence \(a_1^2 = 2b_1^2\). Therefore \(b_1 \in C\). Yet \(0 < b_1 = b_0/2 < b_0\), contradicting the choice of \(b_0\) as the least element of \(C\). Hence \(C = \emptyset\), and \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

Notice how clean that was: we did not even need to invoke "reduce the fraction \(a/b\) to lowest terms" beforehand—[REF: wop | WOP] automatically handed us the minimal denominator \(b_0\), and producing a strictly smaller element \(b_1 \in C\) gave an immediate contradiction.

=== SECTION 4: Logical equivalence of weak induction, strong induction, and WOP ===

We now have three principles on \(\mathbb{N}\): ordinary (weak) induction, [REF: strong-induction | strong induction], and [REF: wop | the Well-Ordering Principle]. At first glance, strong induction looks strictly more powerful than weak induction because its inductive hypothesis assumes far more, and WOP looks like a completely different kind of axiom. Remarkably, all three are logically equivalent: assuming any one of them lets you prove the other two.

[CALLOUT]
id: equiv-three
label: Theorem
title: Equivalence of ordinary induction, strong induction, and WOP
tone: key

On the natural numbers \(\mathbb{N}\), the following three statements are logically equivalent:

(1) Ordinary (weak) mathematical induction.
(2) Strong (complete) mathematical induction.
(3) The Well-Ordering Principle.
[/CALLOUT]

We prove the cycle \((1) \implies (2) \implies (3) \implies (1)\), assuming \(n_0 = 0\) without loss of generality (shifting by \(n_0\) merely replaces \(P(n)\) by \(\widetilde{P}(m) = P(n_0 + m)\) on \(\mathbb{N}\)).

[CALLOUT]
id: equiv-proof
label: Proof
title: Cycle of implications \((1) \implies (2) \implies (3) \implies (1)\)
tone: note

Proof of \((1) \implies (2)\): Assume ordinary induction holds, and suppose a predicate \(P(n)\) on \(\mathbb{N}\) satisfies the hypotheses of strong induction: \(P(0)\) is true, and for every \(k \ge 0\), if \(P(0), P(1), \dots, P(k)\) are all true then \(P(k + 1)\) is true. Define a new, strengthened predicate
\[
Q(k) : \text{for every integer } j \text{ with } 0 \le j \le k,\, P(j) \text{ is true.}
\]
We prove \(\forall k \in \mathbb{N},\, Q(k)\) by ordinary induction. For the base case \(k = 0\), \(Q(0)\) says simply \(P(0)\), which holds by hypothesis. For the ordinary inductive step, fix \(k \ge 0\) and assume \(Q(k)\). By definition of \(Q(k)\), every \(P(j)\) for \(0 \le j \le k\) holds. By the strong induction hypothesis on \(P\), this implies \(P(k + 1)\). Combining \(P(0), \dots, P(k)\) with \(P(k + 1)\) gives \(Q(k + 1)\). By ordinary induction, \(Q(n)\) holds for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\); in particular, \(P(n)\) holds for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\).

Proof of \((2) \implies (3)\): Assume strong induction holds. We prove WOP contrapositively: let \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) be a subset with no least element, and prove \(S = \emptyset\). Define \(P(n)\) to be the statement "\(n \notin S\)." Base case (\(n = 0\)): if \(0 \in S\), then since \(0 \le s\) for all \(s \in \mathbb{N}\), \(0\) would be the least element of \(S\), impossible; hence \(P(0)\) holds. Strong inductive step: fix \(k \ge 0\) and assume \(P(0), P(1), \dots, P(k)\) all hold, meaning none of \(0, 1, \dots, k\) belong to \(S\). Then \(k + 1\) cannot belong to \(S\) either—otherwise every element of \(S\) would be at least \(k + 1\), making \(k + 1\) the least element of \(S\). Thus \(P(k + 1)\) holds. By strong induction, \(n \notin S\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), so \(S = \emptyset\).

Proof of \((3) \implies (1)\): Assume WOP holds, and suppose \(P(0)\) is true and \(\forall k \ge 0,\, P(k) \implies P(k + 1)\). Let \(C = \{n \in \mathbb{N} : P(n) \text{ is false}\}\) be the set of counterexamples. Suppose for contradiction that \(C \ne \emptyset\). By WOP, \(C\) has a least element \(m \in C\). Since \(P(0)\) is true, \(0 \notin C\), so \(m \ge 1\). Therefore \(m - 1 \in \mathbb{N}\), and because \(m - 1 < m\) and \(m\) is the least element of \(C\), we have \(m - 1 \notin C\), meaning \(P(m - 1)\) is true. Applying the inductive step at \(k = m - 1\) yields \(P(m)\), contradicting \(m \in C\). Hence \(C = \emptyset\), and \(P(n)\) holds for all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Look closely at the third leg of [REF: equiv-proof | the equivalence proof]: it gives a universal translation recipe between strong induction and minimal-counterexample WOP proofs. Saying "assume \(P(j)\) holds for all \(n_0 \le j < m\) and prove \(P(m)\)" in strong induction is structurally identical to saying "let \(m\) be the smallest counterexample, so \(P(j)\) holds for all \(n_0 \le j < m\), and derive \(P(m)\) to reach a contradiction" in WOP. Because the two principles are equivalent, the choice between them is a matter of clarity and naturalness rather than logical power.

[TABLE]
Method | Starting setup | Core deduction | Best suited for
Ordinary induction | Base case \(P(n_0)\) | \(P(k) \implies P(k + 1)\) | Sums, powers, single-step divisibility or inequalities
Strong induction | Base cases \(P(n_0), \dots, P(n_0 + b - 1)\) | \(\bigl(\forall j \in [n_0, k],\, P(j)\bigr) \implies P(k + 1)\) | Fixed-lag stamps/recurrences; arbitrary smaller splits like factorization
Well-Ordering Principle | Nonempty \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) (or counterexample set \(C\)) | Pick least \(m \in S\); build a contradiction or a smaller element | Division algorithm, descent/irrationality, minimal-counterexample proofs
[/TABLE]
