=== SECTION 1: A complete decision framework for oblique triangles ===

You now hold the full oblique-triangle toolkit: the Law of Sines (including its ambiguous SSA resolution), the Law of Cosines, and the SAS area formula. In real geometry, surveying, and navigation problems, nobody labels the diagram "use Law of Sines" or "use Law of Cosines." Your first job is to classify the given measurements and select the right starting law immediately.

Every solvable oblique triangle falls into one of five three-piece configurations (note that AAA—three angles alone—determines only similarity, never side lengths):

[TABLE]
Given data | Opposite pair known? | Starting law | Possible triangles | Watch out for
AAS or ASA | Yes (after \(180^\circ\) sum for ASA) | Law of Sines | \(1\) (if angle sum \(< 180^\circ\)) | Keep exact original pair; avoid rounding reuse
SSA | Yes (one pair + second side) | Law of Sines | \(0, 1,\) or \(2\) | Always test supplement \(B_2 = 180^\circ - B_1\)
SAS | No | Law of Cosines (for opposite side) | \(1\) | Watch sign of \(-2ab\cos C\) if \(C > 90^\circ\); avoid Law of Sines on longest side next
SSS | No | Law of Cosines (for largest angle) | \(0\) or \(1\) | Check triangle inequality first
[/TABLE]

Notice the single binary decision rule that governs the whole table:
- Do you know a complete opposite pair (an angle AND its opposite side, or two angles that immediately yield the third angle opposite the given side)? Start with the Law of Sines.
- Do you have NO opposite pair (SAS or SSS)? Start with the Law of Cosines.

=== SECTION 2: Avoiding the hybrid SAS trap ===

One of the subtlest mistakes in oblique-triangle solving happens halfway through an SAS problem. Suppose you are given \(a = 5\), \(b = 12\), and \(C = 40^\circ\). You correctly start with the Law of Cosines to find the third side \(c\):
\[
c^2 = 5^2 + 12^2 - 2(5)(12)\cos 40^\circ \approx 169 - 120(0.7660) \approx 77.08 \implies c \approx 8.78.
\]
Now you have all three sides (\(a = 5, c \approx 8.78, b = 12\)) and angle \(C = 40^\circ\). It is tempting to switch to the faster Law of Sines to find one of the remaining angles \(A\) or \(B\). Which one is SAFE to find with the Law of Sines?

Look at what happens if you blindly solve for angle \(B\) (opposite the LONGEST side \(b = 12\)):
\[
\sin B = \frac{12\sin 40^\circ}{8.78} \approx 0.8785 \implies \sin^{-1}(0.8785) \approx 61.5^\circ.
\]
Then subtracting from \(180^\circ\) gives \(A \approx 180^\circ - 40^\circ - 61.5^\circ = 78.5^\circ\). Wait a second! Look at that result: \(A \approx 78.5^\circ > B \approx 61.5^\circ\), even though side \(a = 5\) is LESS than HALF as long as side \(b = 12\)! That is impossible!

Why did it fail? Because \(b = 12\) is the longest side and \(a^2 + c^2 \approx 25 + 77.08 = 102.08 < 144 = b^2\), angle \(B\) is actually OBTUSE: \(B \approx 180^\circ - 61.5^\circ = 118.5^\circ\), giving \(A \approx 180^\circ - 40^\circ - 118.5^\circ = 21.5^\circ\). The calculator key \(\sin^{-1}\) can NEVER output an obtuse angle!

Now see what happens if you solve for the SMALLER remaining angle \(A\) (opposite \(a = 5\)) instead:
\[
\sin A = \frac{5\sin 40^\circ}{8.78} \approx 0.3661 \implies A = \sin^{-1}(0.3661) \approx 21.5^\circ.
\]
Because \(a = 5\) is NOT the longest side of the triangle, angle \(A\) is GUARANTEED to be acute—a triangle can have at most one obtuse angle, and it MUST sit opposite the longest side! Subtracting then gives the true obtuse angle \(B = 180^\circ - 40^\circ - 21.5^\circ = 118.5^\circ\) automatically!

[CALLOUT]
id: hybrid-sas-rule
label: Critical rule
title: Safe second step after the Law of Cosines in an SAS triangle
tone: caution

After using the Law of Cosines to find the third side of an SAS triangle, you have two completely ambiguity-free options for the next angle:
(1) Apply the Law of Sines ONLY to the SHORTER of the two originally given sides (whose opposite angle is guaranteed to be acute).
(2) Or stay with the Law of Cosines to find either remaining angle, since \(\cos^{-1}\) distinguishes acute from obtuse angles automatically.

Never apply the Law of Sines blindly to the angle opposite the longest side of the triangle, and ALWAYS finish with the side-angle order check (largest angle opposite longest side, smallest angle opposite shortest side).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Navigation bearings, surveying, and triangulation ===

Real-world geometry problems rarely hand you a pre-labeled triangle; instead, they give bearings, baselines, and sight lines that you translate into an SAS, ASA, or AAS model.

Consider a two-leg navigation course. A vessel leaves port \(P\) and sails \(10\text{ km}\) on a compass bearing of \(\mathrm{N}\,40^\circ\,\mathrm{E}\) to waypoint \(Q\), then turns onto a bearing of \(\mathrm{S}\,70^\circ\,\mathrm{E}\) and sails \(14\text{ km}\) to island \(R\). How far is the island directly from port?

Draw vertical North-South reference lines at both \(P\) and \(Q\). By alternate interior angles, looking back from \(Q\) toward \(P\) runs \(40^\circ\) west of due South (\(\mathrm{S}\,40^\circ\,\mathrm{W}\)). Leg 2 runs \(70^\circ\) east of due South (\(\mathrm{S}\,70^\circ\,\mathrm{E}\)). Because one ray lies \(40^\circ\) west of South and the other lies \(70^\circ\) east of South, the interior angle at waypoint \(Q\) is
\[
\angle PQR = 40^\circ + 70^\circ = 110^\circ.
\]

Now \(\triangle PQR\) is an SAS triangle with sides \(10\text{ km}\) and \(14\text{ km}\) enclosing \(110^\circ\). The Law of Cosines gives the straight-line distance \(d = PR\):
\[
d = \sqrt{10^2 + 14^2 - 2(10)(14)\cos 110^\circ} \approx \sqrt{296 - 280(-0.3420)} \approx \sqrt{391.77} \approx 19.8\text{ km}.
\]
To find the direct return or outbound bearing from \(P\) to \(R\), apply [REF: hybrid-sas-rule | our safe second-step rule]: find the acute angle \(\angle QPR\) (opposite \(14 < 19.8\), so guaranteed acute) via the Law of Sines (\(\sin\angle QPR = \frac{14\sin 110^\circ}{19.8} \approx 0.664 \implies \angle QPR \approx 41.6^\circ\)), then add it to the first leg's \(40^\circ\) east of North to get \(\mathrm{N}\,81.6^\circ\,\mathrm{E}\).

By contrast, triangulation across an obstacle (such as a canyon or bay) measures a known baseline \(AB\) and the two sighting angles \(\angle CAB\) and \(\angle CBA\) to a distant target \(C\). That creates an ASA triangle: subtract from \(180^\circ\) to get \(\angle ACB\), then apply the Law of Sines to find the distance from either station to \(C\).

Always pick the diagonal that cuts off a triangle where THREE measurements are already known! Solve that first triangle to find the exact length of the shared diagonal \(BD\) (usually via SAS Law of Cosines or ASA Law of Sines), and then pass \(BD\) into the second triangle as a known side to unlock its remaining angles, sides, and area. Add the areas of both triangles to get the total area of the quadrilateral.
