=== SECTION 1: Directed angles in standard position and coterminal families ===

In right-triangle geometry, angles are static openings measured between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) (or at most \(180^\circ\) in a general triangle). Yet a rotating wheel, a swinging pendulum, or a planet orbiting a star can turn past \(180^\circ\), complete multiple full revolutions, or rotate in the opposite direction. To describe rotation of any size and either direction in the Cartesian plane, we place every angle into a single shared reference frame called standard position.

[CALLOUT]
id: standard-position
label: Definition
title: Directed angle in standard position
tone: key

An angle in the Cartesian plane is in standard position when:

(1) Its vertex sits at the origin \((0, 0)\).
(2) Its initial ray lies along the positive \(x\)-axis.
(3) Its terminal ray is obtained by rotating the initial ray about the origin: counterclockwise rotation is positive, and clockwise rotation is negative.
[/CALLOUT]

The four coordinate axes divide the plane into four quadrants, numbered counterclockwise from the upper right: Quadrant I (\(0^\circ\) to \(90^\circ\)), Quadrant II (\(90^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\)), Quadrant III (\(180^\circ\) to \(270^\circ\)), and Quadrant IV (\(270^\circ\) to \(360^\circ\)). An angle whose terminal ray lands directly on the \(x\)-axis or \(y\)-axis—such as \(0^\circ, \pm 90^\circ, \pm 180^\circ, \pm 270^\circ, \pm 360^\circ\)—is called a quadrantal angle; it lies on a boundary rather than inside any quadrant.

Because rotating a full circle (\(360^\circ\) counterclockwise or \(-360^\circ\) clockwise) brings a ray straight back to where it started, infinitely many distinct rotations share the exact same terminal ray. For example, \(50^\circ\), \(410^\circ = 50^\circ + 360^\circ\), and \(-310^\circ = 50^\circ - 360^\circ\) all end on the same ray in Quadrant I, even though they represent different amounts and directions of turning. Two angles in standard position with the same terminal ray are called coterminal angles.

[CALLOUT]
id: coterminal-deg
label: Rule
title: Coterminal angles in degrees
tone: key

Two angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) in standard position are coterminal if and only if they differ by an integer multiple of \(360^\circ\):
\[
\beta = \alpha + 360^\circ k \quad \text{for some integer } k \in \mathbb{Z}.
\]
Every angle has a unique least nonnegative coterminal angle in the interval \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\), obtained by adding or subtracting multiples of \(360^\circ\) until the result lands in \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\).
[/CALLOUT]

To locate the quadrant of a large positive or negative angle such as \(-500^\circ\), add multiples of \(360^\circ\) until the angle enters \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\). Adding one turn gives \(-500^\circ + 360^\circ = -140^\circ\) (still negative); adding a second turn gives \(-500^\circ + 720^\circ = 220^\circ\). Since \(180^\circ < 220^\circ < 270^\circ\), the angle \(-500^\circ\) has least nonnegative coterminal angle \(220^\circ\) and terminates in Quadrant III. Notice why it is dangerous to drop the minus sign early: \(+140^\circ\) lies in Quadrant II, not Quadrant III.

=== SECTION 2: Radian measure as an arc-to-radius ratio ===

Dividing a full circle into \(360\) degrees is a historical convention, not a geometric necessity; we could just as easily have chosen \(100\) or \(400\) parts. Mathematics and physics use a natural, scale-free angle unit built directly out of the circle itself: the radian.

Place a central angle \(\theta\) at the center of a circle of radius \(r > 0\), and let \(s\) be the length of the circular arc intercepted by \(\theta\). If you double the radius to \(2r\) while keeping the central angle fixed, the circle scales uniformly by a factor of \(2\), so the intercepted arc length also doubles to \(2s\). More generally, any two circles with radii \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are similar figures with scale factor \(r_2 / r_1\). Therefore the intercepted arc lengths \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) of the same central angle satisfy \(s_2 = (r_2/r_1)s_1\), which rearranges to
\[
\frac{s_2}{r_2} = \frac{s_1}{r_1}.
\]
Because the ratio \(s/r\) does not depend on the radius \(r\) of the circle, it measures the angle \(\theta\) itself.

[CALLOUT]
id: radian-def
label: Definition
title: Radian measure
tone: key

The radian measure \(\theta\) of a central angle that intercepts an arc of length \(s\) on a circle of radius \(r\) is
\[
\theta = \frac{s}{r}
\]
(with a negative sign if the angle is rotated clockwise). In particular, an angle of \(1\text{ radian}\) intercepts an arc whose length equals the circle's radius (\(s = r\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Because \(s\) and \(r\) are both lengths measured in the same unit (such as centimeters or meters), their units cancel in \(s/r\). A radian is therefore a pure, dimensionless real number. That is why we write \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}\) or \(\theta = 2\) without a symbol like \(^\circ\); when no degree symbol is written on an angle, the angle is in radians.

To connect radians to degrees, apply [REF: radian-def | the definition] to one complete counterclockwise revolution. The full circumference of a circle of radius \(r\) is \(C = 2\pi r\), so the radian measure of a full rotation is
\[
\theta_{\text{full}} = \frac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi\text{ radians}.
\]
Thus \(360^\circ = 2\pi\text{ rad}\), and dividing both sides by \(2\) gives the fundamental bridge between the two systems:
\[
180^\circ = \pi\text{ radians}.
\]
Dividing by \(\pi\) shows how large a single radian is in degrees:
\[
1\text{ rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.3^\circ.
\]
Visualizing \(1\text{ rad} \approx 57.3^\circ\)—just under \(60^\circ\), because an equilateral chord of length \(r\) bends slightly outward when curved into an arc of length \(r\)—makes radian sizes immediately intuitive.

[CALLOUT]
id: pi-caution
label: Common pitfall
title: \(\pi\) is a number near \(3.1416\), not \(180\)
tone: caution

(1) Writing "\(\pi = 180^\circ\)" drops the unit "radians" and invites confusion. The number \(\pi \approx 3.1416\) is not \(180\); rather, an angle of \(\pi\) radians equals an angle of \(180^\circ\).
(2) Never confuse a bare radian number with degrees. An angle of \(2\) (with no degree symbol) means \(2\text{ radians} \approx 114.6^\circ\) in Quadrant II, nowhere near \(2^\circ\) in Quadrant I.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Exact degree-radian conversion and fractions of \(\pi\) ===

From the equivalence \(180^\circ = \pi\text{ rad}\), both conversion factors equal \(1\):
\[
\frac{\pi\text{ rad}}{180^\circ} = 1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{180^\circ}{\pi\text{ rad}} = 1.
\]
Multiplying by the appropriate factor converts cleanly between units:

- Degrees to radians: multiply by \(\dfrac{\pi}{180^\circ}\) and reduce the fraction.
- Radians to degrees: multiply by \(\dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi}\) (when the angle is a rational multiple of \(\pi\), the \(\pi\) factors cancel cleanly).

For example:
\[
210^\circ = 210^\circ \cdot \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} = \frac{7\pi}{6}, \qquad
-\frac{5\pi}{4} = -\frac{5\pi}{4} \cdot \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} = -225^\circ.
\]

Even faster than mechanical multiplication is thinking structurally in fractions of a half-turn (\(\pi = 180^\circ\)). Dividing a half-circle into \(2, 3, 4\), or \(6\) equal wedges produces the four benchmark building blocks of trigonometry:
\[
\frac{\pi}{2} = 90^\circ, \qquad \frac{\pi}{3} = 60^\circ, \qquad \frac{\pi}{4} = 45^\circ, \qquad \frac{\pi}{6} = 30^\circ.
\]
Every common angle is simply an integer count of one of these wedges. For instance, \(135^\circ = 3 \cdot 45^\circ = \frac{3\pi}{4}\), and \(\frac{5\pi}{3} = 5 \cdot 60^\circ = 300^\circ\).

[TABLE]
Degrees | \(0^\circ\) | \(30^\circ\) | \(45^\circ\) | \(60^\circ\) | \(90^\circ\) | \(120^\circ\) | \(135^\circ\) | \(150^\circ\) | \(180^\circ\) | \(270^\circ\) | \(360^\circ\)
Radians | \(0\) | \(\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) | \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) | \(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) | \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) | \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) | \(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\) | \(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\) | \(\pi\) | \(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\) | \(2\pi\)
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 4: Coterminal angles and quadrants in radians ===

In radians, one full revolution is \(2\pi\), so [REF: coterminal-deg | the coterminal rule] becomes: two angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) in radians are coterminal if and only if
\[
\beta = \alpha + 2\pi k \quad \text{for some integer } k \in \mathbb{Z}.
\]
The unique least nonnegative coterminal angle lies in \([0, 2\pi)\), and the four quadrant boundaries in radians are:

- Quadrant I: \(0 < \theta < \dfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.571\)
- Quadrant II: \(\dfrac{\pi}{2} < \theta < \pi \approx 3.142\)
- Quadrant III: \(\pi < \theta < \dfrac{3\pi}{2} \approx 4.712\)
- Quadrant IV: \(\dfrac{3\pi}{2} < \theta < 2\pi \approx 6.283\)

When an angle is given as a fraction of \(\pi\), write \(2\pi\) with the same denominator so you can add or subtract full turns mentally. For example, to find the least nonnegative coterminal angle and quadrant of \(\frac{19\pi}{4}\), write one full turn as \(2\pi = \frac{8\pi}{4}\). Subtracting two full turns (\(4\pi = \frac{16\pi}{4}\)) leaves
\[
\frac{19\pi}{4} - \frac{16\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}.
\]
Because \(\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{2\pi}{4} < \frac{3\pi}{4} < \frac{4\pi}{4} = \pi\), the angle \(\frac{19\pi}{4}\) terminates in Quadrant II.

For a negative angle such as \(-\frac{17\pi}{3}\), write \(2\pi = \frac{6\pi}{3}\). Adding three full turns (\(\frac{18\pi}{3}\)) yields
\[
-\frac{17\pi}{3} + \frac{18\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{3} \in [0, 2\pi),
\]
so \(-\frac{17\pi}{3}\) terminates in Quadrant I.

Finally, when an angle is given as a plain real number without \(\pi\)—such as \(3.5\) radians—compare it directly against the decimal quadrant boundaries \(0, 1.571, 3.142, 4.712, 6.283\). Since \(3.142 < 3.5 < 4.712\), an angle of \(3.5\) radians lies just past \(\pi\) in Quadrant III.
