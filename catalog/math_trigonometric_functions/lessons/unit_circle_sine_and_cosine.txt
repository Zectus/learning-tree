=== SECTION 1: Wrapping the real line onto the unit circle ===

In a right triangle, the two acute angles are trapped strictly between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) (or \(0\) and \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) radians). Yet waves, circular orbits, and oscillating springs require sine and cosine to accept any real number \(t\)—positive, negative, zero, or larger than \(2\pi\). How can we define \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\) for every real number \(t\) so that they agree seamlessly with right-triangle ratios when \(0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2}\)?

Place the unit circle—the circle of radius \(1\) centered at the origin, with equation \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\)—in the Cartesian plane. Starting at the point \((1, 0)\), move along the circle through a directed arc length \(t\) (counterclockwise if \(t \ge 0\), clockwise if \(t < 0\)). Because the circle has radius \(r = 1\), the radian angle swept out is \(\theta = s/r = t/1 = t\): the radian measure of the standard-position angle and the directed arc length along the unit circle are the exact same real number \(t\). Let \(P(x, y)\) be the terminal point reached on the unit circle.

[CALLOUT]
id: unit-circle-def
label: Definition
title: Unit-circle definition of cosine and sine
tone: key

For any real number \(t\), let \(P(x, y)\) be the point on the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) reached by traveling directed arc length \(t\) from \((1, 0)\) (counterclockwise for \(t \ge 0\), clockwise for \(t < 0\)). Then:

(1) \(\cos t = x\) (the horizontal coordinate of \(P\)).
(2) \(\sin t = y\) (the vertical coordinate of \(P\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Why does [REF: unit-circle-def | this definition] agree with right-triangle trigonometry whenever \(0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2}\)? When \(0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2}\), the terminal point \(P(x, y)\) lies in Quadrant I with \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\). Dropping a perpendicular from \(P(x, y)\) to \((x, 0)\) on the positive \(x\)-axis forms a right triangle with hypotenuse \(1\), adjacent leg \(x\), and opposite leg \(y\). The right-triangle ratios therefore give
\[
\cos t = \frac{\mathrm{adj}}{\mathrm{hyp}} = \frac{x}{1} = x, \qquad \sin t = \frac{\mathrm{opp}}{\mathrm{hyp}} = \frac{y}{1} = y,
\]
matching [REF: unit-circle-def | the unit-circle coordinates] identically. The unit circle does not replace right-triangle ratios—it extends them effortlessly to all real numbers \(t\).

=== SECTION 2: Quadrantal points and symmetry around the circle ===

Four landmark points sit directly on the coordinate axes, giving exact values for every quadrantal multiple of \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) immediately by reading coordinates:

- \(t = 0\): \(P(1, 0) \implies \cos 0 = 1\), \(\sin 0 = 0\).
- \(t = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\): \(P(0, 1) \implies \cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0\), \(\sin\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 1\).
- \(t = \pi\): \(P(-1, 0) \implies \cos\pi = -1\), \(\sin\pi = 0\).
- \(t = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}\): \(P(0, -1) \implies \cos\!\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right) = 0\), \(\sin\!\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right) = -1\).

In Quadrant I, the three special angles from right-triangle geometry give three exact unit-circle points:
\[
t = \frac{\pi}{6}:\ \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right), \qquad
t = \frac{\pi}{4}:\ \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right), \qquad
t = \frac{\pi}{3}:\ \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right).
\]
You do NOT need to memorize sixteen separate coordinate pairs around the circle. Every other multiple of \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) or \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) is a mirror reflection of one of those three Quadrant I points across the \(y\)-axis, the \(x\)-axis, or the origin! Reflecting across an axis keeps the coordinate magnitudes identical and only adjusts the signs according to the quadrant.

To evaluate \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\) at any multiple of \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) or \(\frac{\pi}{4}\):
1. Identify the acute gap (reference angle) between \(t\) and the nearest horizontal axis (\(0\), \(\pi\), or \(2\pi\)). This tells you which of \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{\pi}{4}\), or \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) supplies the magnitudes.
2. Identify the quadrant of \(t\) to attach the signs of \(x = \cos t\) and \(y = \sin t\).

[TABLE]
Quadrant | Angle range in \([0, 2\pi)\) | Sign of \(\cos t = x\) | Sign of \(\sin t = y\)
Quadrant I | \((0, \pi/2)\) | \(+\) | \(+\)
Quadrant II | \((\pi/2, \pi)\) | \(-\) | \(+\)
Quadrant III | \((\pi, 3\pi/2)\) | \(-\) | \(-\)
Quadrant IV | \((3\pi/2, 2\pi)\) | \(+\) | \(-\)
[/TABLE]

For example, take \(t = \frac{7\pi}{4}\). It lies \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) shy of \(2\pi = \frac{8\pi}{4}\), placing it in Quadrant IV with reference angle \(\frac{\pi}{4}\). From the Quadrant I point \(\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)\), reflecting across the \(x\)-axis into Quadrant IV keeps \(x\) positive and makes \(y\) negative:
\[
\cos\!\left(\frac{7\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \qquad \sin\!\left(\frac{7\pi}{4}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.
\]

=== SECTION 3: Periodicity, boundedness, and the Pythagorean relation ===

Because every point \(P(\cos t, \sin t)\) sits on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) of circumference \(2\pi\), four major structural properties drop straight out of the circle's geometry without any heavy algebra.

First, in trigonometry we use the standard exponent shorthand \(\cos^2 t\) to mean \((\cos t)^2\) and \(\sin^2 t\) to mean \((\sin t)^2\) (never \(\cos(t^2)\)). Substituting \(x = \cos t\) and \(y = \sin t\) into the unit-circle equation \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) immediately gives the Pythagorean relation:
\[
\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1 \qquad \text{for every real number } t.
\]

Second, because \(\cos^2 t = 1 - \sin^2 t \le 1\) and \(\sin^2 t = 1 - \cos^2 t \le 1\) (or simply because no point on a circle of radius \(1\) centered at the origin can ever stray past \(\pm 1\) along either axis), both functions are strictly bounded:
\[
-1 \le \cos t \le 1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad -1 \le \sin t \le 1 \qquad \text{for every real number } t.
\]

Third, adding or subtracting any full turn \(2\pi\) returns the moving point to the exact same location on the unit circle, so both functions are \(2\pi\)-periodic:
\[
\cos(t + 2\pi k) = \cos t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sin(t + 2\pi k) = \sin t \qquad \text{for all } t \in \mathbb{R} \text{ and all } k \in \mathbb{Z}.
\]

[CALLOUT]
id: unit-circle-properties
label: Properties
title: Fundamental geometric properties of sine and cosine
tone: key

For every real number \(t\) and every integer \(k\):

(1) Pythagorean relation: \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\).
(2) Boundedness: \(-1 \le \cos t \le 1\) and \(-1 \le \sin t \le 1\).
(3) \(2\pi\)-periodicity: \(\cos(t + 2\pi k) = \cos t\) and \(\sin(t + 2\pi k) = \sin t\).
(4) Even and odd symmetry: \(\cos(-t) = \cos t\) (even) and \(\sin(-t) = -\sin t\) (odd).
(5) Supplementary angles: \(\sin(\pi - t) = \sin t\) and \(\cos(\pi - t) = -\cos t\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us verify [REF: unit-circle-properties.4 | items (4)] and [REF: unit-circle-properties.5 | (5)] geometrically right on the circle:

- Negating the arc length from \(t\) to \(-t\) travels the same distance clockwise instead of counterclockwise from \((1, 0)\), which reflects \(P(x, y)\) across the \(x\)-axis to \((x, -y)\). The \(x\)-coordinate stays unchanged (\(\cos(-t) = \cos t\)), while the \(y\)-coordinate flips sign (\(\sin(-t) = -\sin t\)).
- Replacing \(t\) with \(\pi - t\) starts at \((-1, 0)\) (angle \(\pi\)) and backs up by \(t\), which reflects \(P(x, y)\) across the \(y\)-axis to \((-x, y)\). Thus the \(y\)-coordinate stays unchanged (\(\sin(\pi - t) = \sin t\)), while the \(x\)-coordinate flips sign (\(\cos(\pi - t) = -\cos t\)). Notice this works for ALL real \(t\), not only acute angles!

Let us put [REF: unit-circle-properties | these properties] to work together. First, to evaluate \(\cos\!\left(\frac{13\pi}{6}\right) - \sin\!\left(-\frac{5\pi}{4}\right)\):
- By \(2\pi\)-periodicity, \(\frac{13\pi}{6} - 2\pi = \frac{\pi}{6}\), so \(\cos\!\left(\frac{13\pi}{6}\right) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\).
- By odd symmetry, \(\sin\!\left(-\frac{5\pi}{4}\right) = -\sin\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{4}\right) = -\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\).
- Subtracting gives \(\frac{\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{2}}{2}\).

Second, suppose a terminal point \(P(\cos t, \sin t)\) lies in Quadrant IV with \(\sin t = -\frac{3}{5}\). By [REF: unit-circle-properties.1 | the Pythagorean relation],
\[
\cos^2 t = 1 - \left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 = 1 - \frac{9}{25} = \frac{16}{25} \implies \cos t = \pm\frac{4}{5}.
\]
Because \(P\) lies in Quadrant IV, where \(x > 0\), we select the positive root: \(\cos t = \frac{4}{5}\). Always check the quadrant sign when taking a square root—the equation \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\) alone determines only the magnitude, never the sign!
