=== SECTION 1: What it means to solve a right triangle ===

Knowing the trigonometric ratios of an acute angle turns a right triangle from a static diagram into a measuring instrument. Whenever you know two independent pieces of information about a right triangle besides the \(90^\circ\) angle—as long as at least one of them is a side length—the entire triangle is uniquely locked in place. Finding all remaining unknown side lengths and acute angle measures is called solving the right triangle.

Two solvable cases cover every right triangle (labeled \(\triangle ABC\) with right angle at \(C\), opposite side lengths \(a, b, c\), and hypotenuse \(c\)):

1. One side and one acute angle are known.
2. Two sides are known.

Why isn't knowing both acute angles enough? By AA similarity, two angles determine only the shape of the triangle, never its size; infinitely many scaled triangles share the same angles.

In Case 1 (one side and one acute angle), begin by finding the second acute angle immediately via \(B = 90^\circ - A\) (no rounding needed). Next, to find each missing side, pick a trigonometric ratio that links the unknown side directly to the original given side and the given angle.

For example, suppose \(\triangle ABC\) has right angle at \(C\), acute angle \(A = 38^\circ\), and adjacent leg \(b = 14\text{ m}\). Then \(B = 90^\circ - 38^\circ = 52^\circ\). To find opposite leg \(a\), use \(\tan 38^\circ = \frac{a}{14}\), giving
\[
a = 14\tan 38^\circ \approx 14(0.7813) \approx 10.94\text{ m}.
\]
Now look at how to find the hypotenuse \(c\). You could compute \(c = \sqrt{14^2 + (10.94)^2}\), or you could go straight back to the original given data using \(\cos 38^\circ = \frac{14}{c}\):
\[
c = \frac{14}{\cos 38^\circ} \approx \frac{14}{0.7880} \approx 17.77\text{ m}.
\]
Always prefer the direct formula using the exact given data! Reusing a rounded intermediate number like \(a \approx 10.94\) carries its rounding error forward into the next calculation, whereas \(c = 14/\cos 38^\circ\) avoids error propagation completely.

[CALLOUT]
id: direct-ratio-rule
label: Strategy
title: Solving right triangles cleanly
tone: key

(1) Use original given measurements whenever possible so intermediate rounding errors do not compound.
(2) Solve symbolically for the unknown side before reaching for a calculator.
(3) Run a three-point reasonableness check on every finished triangle: the hypotenuse must be strictly longer than either leg, the two acute angles must sum to \(90^\circ\), and the larger acute angle must sit opposite the longer leg.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 2: Finding unknown acute angles from two sides ===

Now consider Case 2: you know two side lengths of a right triangle and need to recover the third side and both acute angles. The missing side comes directly from the Pythagorean theorem \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\), kept exact as a radical until the very end.

How do we recover an acute angle \(\theta\) when we know one of its ratios, such as \(\tan\theta = \frac{5}{8}\)? Because each of \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\), and \(\tan\theta\) moves strictly in one direction across \((0^\circ, 90^\circ)\) (sine and tangent strictly increase; cosine strictly decreases), every valid positive ratio corresponds to one and only one acute angle in \((0^\circ, 90^\circ)\). On a scientific calculator, the inverse-ratio lookup keys \(\sin^{-1}\), \(\cos^{-1}\), and \(\tan^{-1}\) return that unique acute angle:

- If \(\sin\theta = p\) with \(0 < p < 1\), then \(\theta = \sin^{-1}(p) \in (0^\circ, 90^\circ)\).
- If \(\cos\theta = p\) with \(0 < p < 1\), then \(\theta = \cos^{-1}(p) \in (0^\circ, 90^\circ)\).
- If \(\tan\theta = p\) with \(p > 0\), then \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(p) \in (0^\circ, 90^\circ)\).

In this lesson, treat \(\sin^{-1}\), \(\cos^{-1}\), and \(\tan^{-1}\) strictly as numerical angle-lookup tools for acute angles in \((0^\circ, 90^\circ)\); their formal behavior as functions over wider domains is developed later in Trigonometric Identities and Equations. Always check that your calculator is set to degree mode when working in degrees, and remember that the superscript \(-1\) denotes angle lookup ("the acute angle whose sine is \(p\)"), never the reciprocal \(1/\sin p\).

Let us solve right triangle \(ABC\) with right angle at \(C\), leg \(a = 5\), and hypotenuse \(c = 13\).
1. Missing leg \(b\): \(b = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{169 - 25} = 12\).
2. Acute angle \(A\) directly from the two given sides \(a = 5\) (opposite \(A\)) and \(c = 13\) (hypotenuse):
\[
\sin A = \frac{5}{13} \implies A = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{5}{13}\right) \approx 22.62^\circ.
\]
3. Acute angle \(B\): either \(B = 90^\circ - A \approx 67.38^\circ\), or directly from the given sides via \(\cos B = \frac{5}{13} \implies B = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{5}{13}\right) \approx 67.38^\circ\).

Running [REF: direct-ratio-rule.3 | our reasonableness check]: the hypotenuse \(13\) is strictly the longest side, \(22.62^\circ + 67.38^\circ = 90^\circ\), and the larger acute angle \(B \approx 67.38^\circ\) lies opposite the longer leg \(b = 12\).

=== SECTION 3: Angles of elevation, angles of depression, and compass bearings ===

Most real-world right-triangle problems arrive as verbal descriptions rather than pre-drawn triangles. Turning words into an accurate diagram requires two standard geometric conventions: sight-line angles and compass bearings.

When an observer looks upward from a horizontal line of sight to an elevated object, the acute angle between the horizontal ray and the line of sight is the angle of elevation. When an observer looks downward from a horizontal line of sight to a lower object, the acute angle between the horizontal ray and the line of sight is the angle of depression.

Because the upper horizontal ray at \(T\) and the ground line at \(O\) are parallel, the line of sight acts as a transversal. By alternate interior angles, the angle of depression \(\beta\) from \(T\) down to \(O\) equals the angle of elevation \(\alpha\) from \(O\) up to \(T\).

[CALLOUT]
id: depression-caution
label: Common pitfall
title: Angles of depression are measured from the horizontal, never the vertical
tone: caution

When sighting downward from a cliff, tower, or aircraft at an angle of depression \(\beta\), the angle \(\beta\) sits OUTSIDE the right triangle between the horizontal ray and the line of sight—NOT between the vertical tower wall and the line of sight. Inside the right triangle, the angle at the top vertex is \(90^\circ - \beta\), and the angle at the bottom vertex is \(\beta\).
[/CALLOUT]

In navigation and surveying, directions in a horizontal plane are commonly expressed as quadrant compass bearings, written in the form \(\mathrm{N}\,\theta\,\mathrm{E}\), \(\mathrm{N}\,\theta\,\mathrm{W}\), \(\mathrm{S}\,\theta\,\mathrm{E}\), or \(\mathrm{S}\,\theta\,\mathrm{W}\), where \(0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ\). Start facing either due North or due South (the first letter), then turn through acute angle \(\theta\) toward East or West (the second letter).

=== SECTION 4: Multi-triangle indirect measurement ===

Often you cannot walk all the way to the base of a tall mountain, canyon wall, or tower across a river, so no single right triangle has a known horizontal leg. Instead, surveyors measure the angle of elevation twice: once at a farther point \(A\) (\(\text{angle } \alpha\)), and again at a closer point \(B\) (\(\text{angle } \beta > \alpha\)) located a known baseline distance \(d\) directly toward the base \(C\).

Both observations share the same unknown vertical height \(h = CT\). Let \(x = BC\) be the unknown horizontal distance from the closer sighting point \(B\) to the base \(C\); then the horizontal distance from \(A\) to \(C\) is \(x + d\).

To eliminate the unwanted distance \(x\), express each horizontal base in terms of \(h\) using the two right triangles \(\triangle BTC\) and \(\triangle ATC\):
\[
\tan\beta = \frac{h}{x} \implies x = \frac{h}{\tan\beta} = h\cot\beta,
\]
\[
\tan\alpha = \frac{h}{x + d} \implies x + d = \frac{h}{\tan\alpha} = h\cot\alpha.
\]
Subtracting the first equation from the second cancels \(x\) in one step:
\[
d = (x + d) - x = h\cot\alpha - h\cot\beta = h(\cot\alpha - \cot\beta).
\]
Solving for \(h\) yields the exact two-sighting height formula:
\[
h = \frac{d}{\cot\alpha - \cot\beta}.
\]
Notice why we solved for \(x\) rather than \(h\) first: both equations contain \(x\) linearly with coefficient \(1\), so subtracting them immediately isolates \(d\) on one side and \(h\) on the other.

Let us test this on numbers. Suppose a baseline of \(d = 80\text{ m}\) yields angles of elevation \(\alpha = 25^\circ\) and \(\beta = 40^\circ\). Then
\[
h = \frac{80}{\cot 25^\circ - \cot 40^\circ} = \frac{80}{\frac{1}{\tan 25^\circ} - \frac{1}{\tan 40^\circ}} \approx \frac{80}{2.1445 - 1.1918} \approx 84.0\text{ m}.
\]
Keeping the exact formula until the final calculation prevents intermediate rounding from distorting the difference in the denominator.
