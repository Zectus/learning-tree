=== SECTION 1: Arc length in radian measure ===

One of the main reasons radians replace degrees throughout higher mathematics and physics is that circular measurement formulas lose all awkward conversion constants and become as clean as linear proportions.

Recall that for a circle of radius \(r > 0\), a nonnegative central angle \(\theta\) measured in radians is defined as the ratio of intercepted arc length \(s\) to radius \(r\):
\[
\theta = \frac{s}{r}.
\]
Multiplying both sides by \(r\) solves immediately for the arc length \(s\).

[CALLOUT]
id: arc-length-formula
label: Formula
title: Circular arc length
tone: key

On a circle of radius \(r > 0\), an arc subtended by a central angle of \(\theta \ge 0\) radians has length
\[
s = r\theta.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

The condition that \(\theta\) is in radians is not a minor technicality—it is what makes the formula true. Suppose a circle has radius \(r = 10\text{ cm}\) and a central angle of \(60^\circ\). Because \(60^\circ = \frac{\pi}{3}\text{ rad}\), [REF: arc-length-formula | the arc-length formula] gives
\[
s = 10 \cdot \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{10\pi}{3} \approx 10.47\text{ cm}.
\]
Look at what happens if you mistakenly substitute \(60\) directly without converting to radians: you get \(10 \cdot 60 = 600\text{ cm}\), which is almost ten times the entire circumference \(2\pi(10) \approx 62.83\text{ cm}\) of the circle! Whenever an angle is given in degrees, convert it to radians first by multiplying by \(\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: radian-unit-caution
label: Common pitfall
title: Always convert degrees to radians before substituting
tone: caution

Both \(s = r\theta\) and the sector-area formula \(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta\) below hold exclusively when \(\theta\) is measured in radians. Substituting a degree number directly inflates the answer by a factor of \(180/\pi \approx 57.3\).
[/CALLOUT]

Because [REF: arc-length-formula | \(s = r\theta\)] connects three quantities, knowing any two determines the third. For instance, if an arc of length \(s = 18\text{ m}\) subtends a central angle of \(150^\circ\), first convert \(150^\circ = \frac{5\pi}{6}\text{ rad}\), then solve for \(r\):
\[
r = \frac{s}{\theta} = \frac{18}{5\pi/6} = \frac{108}{5\pi}\text{ m}.
\]

=== SECTION 2: Circular sector area ===

A circular sector is the pie-slice region bounded by two radii of length \(r\) and the circular arc of length \(s\) between them. How large is its area \(A\)?

Because a circle is rotationally uniform, the fraction of the full circle's area \(\pi r^2\) occupied by the sector equals the fraction of a full revolution \(2\pi\) occupied by its central angle \(\theta\) (in radians):
\[
\frac{A}{\pi r^2} = \frac{\theta}{2\pi}.
\]
Multiplying both sides by \(\pi r^2\) cancels the factor of \(\pi\) cleanly:
\[
A = \pi r^2 \cdot \frac{\theta}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta.
\]
Moreover, since \(s = r\theta\), we can rewrite \(\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}r(r\theta) = \frac{1}{2}rs\). Notice the geometric intuition behind \(A = \frac{1}{2}rs\): if you slice a sector into many tiny narrow wedges, each wedge looks nearly like a thin triangle of height \(r\) and base \(\Delta s\), so summing their areas produces \(\frac{1}{2}r \sum \Delta s = \frac{1}{2}rs\).

[CALLOUT]
id: sector-area-formula
label: Formula
title: Area of a circular sector
tone: key

A sector of a circle of radius \(r > 0\) with central angle \(\theta\) radians (\(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\)) and arc length \(s = r\theta\) has area
\[
A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}rs.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Having both forms in [REF: sector-area-formula | the sector-area formula] lets you solve direct and inverse problems without detouring through unnecessary variables:

- Given \(r\) and \(\theta\), compute \(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta\) directly.
- Given \(A\) and \(r\), solve \(\theta = \frac{2A}{r^2}\) (and remember to keep the factor of \(2\), which comes from clearing the \(\frac{1}{2}\)).
- Given \(A\) and \(s\), use \(A = \frac{1}{2}rs\) to get \(r = \frac{2A}{s}\) directly, and then \(\theta = \frac{s}{r}\) if needed.

For example, suppose a circular irrigation sprinkler waters a sector of radius \(r = 12\text{ m}\) with area \(A = 54\pi\text{ m}^2\). Substituting into \(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta\) gives
\[
54\pi = \frac{1}{2}(144)\theta = 72\theta \implies \theta = \frac{54\pi}{72} = \frac{3\pi}{4}\text{ rad} = 135^\circ,
\]
and the outer watered arc has length \(s = 12 \cdot \frac{3\pi}{4} = 9\pi\text{ m}\).

=== SECTION 3: Compound regions and applied circular geometry ===

Many physical regions are differences of concentric sectors rather than full pie slices—for instance, the glass swept by a windshield wiper blade whose rubber edge does not reach the pivot, or a curved racing lane between an inner radius \(r_1\) and an outer radius \(r_2\).

Such a region is called an annular sector. It is formed by taking the outer sector of radius \(r_2\) and removing the inner sector of radius \(r_1\) with the same central angle \(\theta\) (in radians). Subtracting their areas gives
\[
A_{\text{ring}} = \frac{1}{2}r_2^2\theta - \frac{1}{2}r_1^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(r_2^2 - r_1^2\bigr)\theta.
\]
Notice a vital algebraic warning: \(r_2^2 - r_1^2\) is NOT equal to \((r_2 - r_1)^2\). Using only the blade length \(r_2 - r_1\) as a radius pretends the wiper sweeps a small pie slice anchored at the pivot, severely undercounting the much wider outer band!

To test this on numbers, suppose a curved garden path runs between inner radius \(r_1 = 4\text{ m}\) and outer radius \(r_2 = 6\text{ m}\) through a \(90^\circ = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad}\) turn. Its area is
\[
A = \frac{1}{2}(6^2 - 4^2)\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{1}{2}(36 - 16)\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = 5\pi\text{ m}^2.
\]
By contrast, plugging \((6 - 4)^2 = 4\) would have given only \(\pi\text{ m}^2\)—off by a factor of \(5\).

Arc length also models surface distances along a meridian of longitude on Earth. Because a meridian is a great circle of radius \(R\) centered at Earth's center, two cities on the same meridian have a central angle equal to the angular separation of their latitudes (subtract latitudes in the same hemisphere; add them when one is north and one is south of the equator). Converting that angle to radians and multiplying by \(R\) yields the surface arc distance \(s = R\theta\).
