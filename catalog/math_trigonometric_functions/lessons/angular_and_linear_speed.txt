=== SECTION 1: Angular speed, linear speed, and the bridge \(v = r\omega\) ===

When a ceiling fan spins steadily, every point along a blade completes a full circle in the exact same time, yet the outer tip slices through the air much faster than a point near the hub. Describing circular motion cleanly requires two distinct speed measures: one for how fast the angle turns, and one for how fast a point travels along its circular path.

Suppose an object moves at a constant rate around a circle of radius \(r > 0\), sweeping through a central angle \(\theta\) (in radians) and covering an arc length \(s\) in elapsed time \(t > 0\).

[CALLOUT]
id: angular-linear-def
label: Definition
title: Constant angular speed and linear speed
tone: key

For uniform circular motion of radius \(r > 0\) sweeping angle \(\theta\) (in radians) and arc length \(s\) during time \(t > 0\):

(1) Angular speed: \(\omega = \dfrac{\theta}{t}\) (measured in radians per unit time, such as \(\mathrm{rad/s}\)).
(2) Linear (tangential) speed: \(v = \dfrac{s}{t}\) (measured in length per unit time, such as \(\mathrm{m/s}\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Here \(\omega\) is the lowercase Greek letter omega. Now substitute the radian arc-length relation \(s = r\theta\) directly into [REF: angular-linear-def.2 | the definition of linear speed]:
\[
v = \frac{s}{t} = \frac{r\theta}{t} = r\left(\frac{\theta}{t}\right) = r\omega.
\]
That single line yields the kinematic bridge of uniform circular motion.

[CALLOUT]
id: v-equals-rw
label: Formula
title: Linear-angular speed relation
tone: key

For a point moving along a circle of radius \(r\) at constant angular speed \(\omega\) measured in radians per unit time,
\[
v = r\omega.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Just like \(s = r\theta\), [REF: v-equals-rw | the formula \(v = r\omega\)] requires \(\omega\) to be expressed in radians per unit time (never in revolutions per minute or degrees per second). Because radians are dimensionless, multiplying meters by \(\mathrm{rad/s}\) directly produces \(\mathrm{m/s}\).

In engineering and everyday life, rotation rates are usually reported in revolutions per minute (\(\mathrm{rpm}\)) or degrees per second rather than \(\mathrm{rad/s}\). Converting into radians per unit time uses two exact identities:
\[
1\text{ rev} = 2\pi\text{ rad} \qquad\text{and}\qquad 1\text{ min} = 60\text{ s}.
\]
For example, consider a \(0.4\text{ m}\)-radius grinding wheel spinning at \(900\text{ rpm}\). First convert \(\omega\) to \(\mathrm{rad/s}\):
\[
\omega = 900\,\frac{\text{rev}}{\text{min}} \cdot \frac{2\pi\text{ rad}}{1\text{ rev}} \cdot \frac{1\text{ min}}{60\text{ s}} = 30\pi\text{ rad/s}.
\]
Then apply [REF: v-equals-rw | \(v = r\omega\)] to obtain the rim speed:
\[
v = (0.4\text{ m})(30\pi\text{ rad/s}) = 12\pi\text{ m/s} \approx 37.7\text{ m/s}.
\]

=== SECTION 2: Rolling wheels without slipping ===

A second major application of [REF: v-equals-rw | \(v = r\omega\)] is a wheel rolling along a flat road without skidding or spinning in place. In one full revolution of a wheel of radius \(r\), the tire lays down its entire circumference \(2\pi r\) along the pavement, so the axle advances forward by exactly \(2\pi r\). More generally, as the wheel turns through \(\theta\) radians, the axle moves forward by \(d = s = r\theta\).

Dividing both sides by elapsed time \(t\) shows that the forward speed of the vehicle equals the rim's tangential speed relative to the axle:
\[
v_{\text{vehicle}} = \frac{d}{t} = \frac{r\theta}{t} = r\omega.
\]
Two unit traps appear repeatedly in rolling-wheel problems:
1. Specifications often give the tire's diameter \(D\) instead of its radius \(r = D/2\). Always halve the diameter before applying \(v = r\omega\).
2. Make sure the radius and the linear speed share the same length unit (both in meters, or both in feet) before dividing \(\omega = v/r\).

For example, if a car with tires of diameter \(60\text{ cm}\) (\(r = 30\text{ cm} = 0.30\text{ m}\)) travels at \(18\text{ m/s}\), its wheels spin at
\[
\omega = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{18\text{ m/s}}{0.30\text{ m}} = 60\text{ rad/s}.
\]
To express this in \(\mathrm{rpm}\), convert back:
\[
60\,\frac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \cdot \frac{1\text{ rev}}{2\pi\text{ rad}} \cdot \frac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}} = \frac{1800}{\pi}\text{ rpm} \approx 573\text{ rpm}.
\]

=== SECTION 3: Coupled systems—same axle versus belt or meshed rims ===

Machines such as bicycles, conveyor drives, and clockwork gear trains link several rotating wheels together. Every mechanical coupling falls into one of two simple cases, and keeping them straight solves virtually any rotational transmission problem.

[CALLOUT]
id: coupling-rules
label: Principle
title: Two mechanical coupling rules
tone: key

(1) Rigidly mounted on the same axle: two wheels (or two points on one rigid disk) rotate through the exact same angle in any given time, so they share the same angular speed:
\[
\omega_1 = \omega_2 \qquad \left(\text{and therefore } \frac{v_1}{r_1} = \frac{v_2}{r_2}\right).
\]
(2) Connected by a non-slipping belt, chain, or meshed gear teeth: every centimeter of belt or gear rim that leaves wheel \(1\) must enter wheel \(2\) in the same time, so their rims share the same linear speed:
\[
v_1 = v_2 \qquad \left(\text{and therefore } r_1\omega_1 = r_2\omega_2\right).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

The graph below shows a smaller drive pulley of radius \(r_1 = 1\) coupled by a belt to a larger driven pulley of radius \(r_2 = 2\). Because \(r_1\omega_1 = r_2\omega_2\), the larger pulley turns at half the angular speed of the smaller pulley: whenever the small pulley completes \(1\) full turn (length \(2\pi\) of belt), the large pulley turns only half a revolution (also length \(2\pi\) of belt).

Drag the slider \(a\) (the rotation angle of pulley \(1\) in radians) and watch spoke \(P_2\) sweep through exactly half the angle of spoke \(P_1\) while the two rim points travel identical arc lengths.

Notice a handy algebraic shortcut in [REF: coupling-rules.2 | \(r_1\omega_1 = r_2\omega_2\)]: because multiplying both sides by the same conversion factor (such as \(\frac{2\pi}{60}\) from \(\mathrm{rpm}\) to \(\mathrm{rad/s}\)) cancels out, the product \(r\omega\) is also equal when both angular speeds are kept in \(\mathrm{rpm}\)—as long as you do not plug \(\mathrm{rpm}\) into \(v = r\omega\) to compute an actual linear speed!

Let us trace a multi-stage system that combines both rules. Suppose a motor shaft drives a \(5\text{ cm}\) pulley at \(240\text{ rpm}\), which is belt-linked to a \(20\text{ cm}\) pulley mounted on the same axle as a \(12\text{ cm}\) drum winding a cable. How fast does the cable move in \(\mathrm{m/s}\)?

1. Motor pulley angular speed: \(\omega_1 = 240\,\frac{\text{rev}}{\text{min}} \cdot \frac{2\pi}{60} = 8\pi\text{ rad/s}\).
2. Belt coupling from \(r_1 = 5\text{ cm}\) to \(r_2 = 20\text{ cm}\) gives \(v_1 = v_2\), so
\[
\omega_2 = \frac{r_1}{r_2}\,\omega_1 = \frac{5}{20}(8\pi) = 2\pi\text{ rad/s}.
\]
3. Same axle connects the \(20\text{ cm}\) pulley to the \(12\text{ cm}\) drum, so \(\omega_{\text{drum}} = \omega_2 = 2\pi\text{ rad/s}\).
4. Cable speed off the drum (\(r_{\text{drum}} = 0.12\text{ m}\)):
\[
v_{\text{cable}} = r_{\text{drum}}\,\omega_{\text{drum}} = (0.12)(2\pi) = 0.24\pi\text{ m/s}.
\]
