=== SECTION 1: Why right-triangle ratios depend only on the angle ===

Suppose two architects draw right triangles with the same acute angle of \(35^\circ\), one on a small notepad and the other on a large blueprint. The blueprint triangle has sides ten times longer than the notepad triangle, yet if both architects divide the side across from the \(35^\circ\) angle by the longest side, they get the exact same number. Why does a side-length ratio depend only on the angle and never on how large the triangle is?

The answer comes from AA similarity. Consider two right triangles \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle A'B'C'\), with right angles at \(C\) and \(C'\), that share the same acute angle \(\theta\) at vertices \(A\) and \(A'\). Because both triangles have a \(90^\circ\) angle and an angle of measure \(\theta\), their third angles must both equal \(90^\circ - \theta\). By AA similarity, \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle A'B'C'\), so there is a single positive scale factor \(k\) such that
\[
a' = ka, \qquad b' = kb, \qquad c' = kc,
\]
where \(a, b, c\) are the side lengths of \(\triangle ABC\) and \(a', b', c'\) are the corresponding side lengths of \(\triangle A'B'C'\). When you form the ratio of any two sides in the larger triangle, the scale factor \(k\) cancels immediately:
\[
\frac{a'}{c'} = \frac{ka}{kc} = \frac{a}{c}, \qquad \frac{b'}{c'} = \frac{kb}{kc} = \frac{b}{c}, \qquad \frac{a'}{b'} = \frac{ka}{kb} = \frac{a}{b}.
\]
Because every right triangle with acute angle \(\theta\) is similar to every other such triangle, each ratio of two side lengths is uniquely determined by \(\theta\) alone.

To name the six possible side ratios unambiguously, we label the three sides of a right triangle relative to the chosen acute angle \(\theta\):

- The hypotenuse (\(\mathrm{hyp}\)) is the longest side, always directly across from the \(90^\circ\) angle.
- The opposite side (\(\mathrm{opp}\)) is the leg across the triangle from angle \(\theta\).
- The adjacent side (\(\mathrm{adj}\)) is the leg that meets the hypotenuse at vertex \(\theta\).

With these three labels fixed for a given acute angle \(\theta\), there are exactly six ordered ratios of two sides, defining the six trigonometric ratios of \(\theta\).

[CALLOUT]
id: six-ratios
label: Definition
title: The six right-triangle trigonometric ratios
tone: key

For an acute angle \(\theta\) (\(0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ\)) in a right triangle with opposite leg \(\mathrm{opp}\), adjacent leg \(\mathrm{adj}\), and hypotenuse \(\mathrm{hyp}\):

(1) Sine: \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{\mathrm{opp}}{\mathrm{hyp}}\)
(2) Cosine: \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathrm{adj}}{\mathrm{hyp}}\)
(3) Tangent: \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\mathrm{opp}}{\mathrm{adj}}\)
(4) Cosecant: \(\csc\theta = \dfrac{\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{opp}}\)
(5) Secant: \(\sec\theta = \dfrac{\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{adj}}\)
(6) Cotangent: \(\cot\theta = \dfrac{\mathrm{adj}}{\mathrm{opp}}\)
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: six-ratios | these definitions] to a concrete \(3\text{--}4\text{--}5\) right triangle \(\triangle ABC\) with right angle at \(C\), where \(AC = 4\), \(BC = 3\), and \(AB = 5\). Standing at angle \(A\), the opposite leg is \(BC = 3\), the adjacent leg is \(AC = 4\), and the hypotenuse is \(AB = 5\), so:
\[
\sin A = \frac{3}{5}, \qquad \cos A = \frac{4}{5}, \qquad \tan A = \frac{3}{4},
\]
\[
\csc A = \frac{5}{3}, \qquad \sec A = \frac{5}{4}, \qquad \cot A = \frac{4}{3}.
\]
Now move to the other acute vertex \(B\) of the exact same triangle. The hypotenuse is still \(AB = 5\), but the legs swap roles: relative to \(B\), the opposite leg is \(AC = 4\) and the adjacent leg is \(BC = 3\). Consequently, \(\sin B = \frac{4}{5}\) and \(\cos B = \frac{3}{5}\), reversing the values we found for \(A\).

[CALLOUT]
id: orientation-caution
label: Common pitfall
title: Opposite and adjacent depend on the reference vertex
tone: caution

(1) Never assume the horizontal leg is adjacent or the vertical leg is opposite. Changing from one acute angle of a right triangle to the other swaps \(\mathrm{opp}\) and \(\mathrm{adj}\) while leaving \(\mathrm{hyp}\) unchanged.
(2) Do not pair names by matching starting letters: cosecant is the reciprocal of sine (\(\csc\theta = 1/\sin\theta\)), and secant is the reciprocal of cosine (\(\sec\theta = 1/\cos\theta\)). Each reciprocal pair has one "co-" prefix and one without.
[/CALLOUT]

Notice also a basic size check for acute angles: because the hypotenuse of a right triangle is strictly longer than either leg, we always have \(0 < \sin\theta < 1\) and \(0 < \cos\theta < 1\), while their reciprocals satisfy \(\csc\theta > 1\) and \(\sec\theta > 1\). Meanwhile, \(\tan\theta\) and \(\cot\theta\) compare the two legs, so either one can take any positive value.

=== SECTION 2: Reciprocal, quotient, and cofunction relationships ===

The six numbers in [REF: six-ratios | the definition] are not six independent quantities. They are all built from the same three side lengths \(\mathrm{opp}\), \(\mathrm{adj}\), and \(\mathrm{hyp}\), which forces three simple families of relationships among them for every acute angle \(\theta\).

First, compare each of the last three ratios with the first three:
\[
\csc\theta = \frac{\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{opp}} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{opp}/\mathrm{hyp}} = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}, \qquad
\sec\theta = \frac{\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{adj}} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{adj}/\mathrm{hyp}} = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}, \qquad
\cot\theta = \frac{\mathrm{adj}}{\mathrm{opp}} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{opp}/\mathrm{adj}} = \frac{1}{\tan\theta}.
\]
Because \(\mathrm{opp}\), \(\mathrm{adj}\), and \(\mathrm{hyp}\) are all positive for an acute angle, none of these denominators is zero.

Second, divide the numerator and denominator of \(\tan\theta = \mathrm{opp}/\mathrm{adj}\) by \(\mathrm{hyp}\):
\[
\tan\theta = \frac{\mathrm{opp}/\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{adj}/\mathrm{hyp}} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta},
\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad
\cot\theta = \frac{\mathrm{adj}/\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{opp}/\mathrm{hyp}} = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}.
\]
These quotient relations show that once \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) are known, all four remaining ratios follow immediately by division and reciprocals.

Third, return to the observation from Section 1 about the two acute angles of a single right triangle. Since the angles of a triangle sum to \(180^\circ\) and one angle is \(90^\circ\), the two acute angles are complementary: if one measures \(\theta\), the other measures \(90^\circ - \theta\). Relative to \(90^\circ - \theta\), the opposite side is the adjacent side of \(\theta\), and the adjacent side is the opposite side of \(\theta\). Reading all six ratios from vertex \(90^\circ - \theta\) therefore gives:
\[
\sin(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{\mathrm{adj\ to\ }\theta}{\mathrm{hyp}} = \cos\theta,
\qquad
\cos(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{\mathrm{opp\ to\ }\theta}{\mathrm{hyp}} = \sin\theta,
\]
\[
\tan(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{\mathrm{adj\ to\ }\theta}{\mathrm{opp\ to\ }\theta} = \cot\theta,
\qquad
\cot(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{\mathrm{opp\ to\ }\theta}{\mathrm{adj\ to\ }\theta} = \tan\theta,
\]
\[
\sec(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{opp\ to\ }\theta} = \csc\theta,
\qquad
\csc(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{adj\ to\ }\theta} = \sec\theta.
\]
This explains the origin of the prefix "co-" in cosine, cotangent, and cosecant: it stands for "complementary." The cosine of an acute angle is literally the sine of its complement, and the same holds for tangent/cotangent and secant/cosecant. Moreover, if you fix the hypotenuse at length \(1\) and increase an acute angle \(\alpha\) from \(0^\circ\) toward \(90^\circ\), the opposite leg grows strictly longer; hence two acute angles have the same sine if and only if they are equal. Combining this with \(\cos\beta = \sin(90^\circ - \beta)\) shows that for acute angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), \(\sin\alpha = \cos\beta\) holds precisely when \(\alpha + \beta = 90^\circ\).

[CALLOUT]
id: basic-relations
label: Summary
title: Reciprocal, quotient, and cofunction relations for acute \(\theta\)
tone: key

(1) Reciprocal relations: \(\csc\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\), \(\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\), \(\cot\theta = \dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}\).
(2) Quotient relations: \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\), \(\cot\theta = \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\).
(3) Cofunction pairs: \(\sin(90^\circ - \theta) = \cos\theta\), \(\tan(90^\circ - \theta) = \cot\theta\), \(\sec(90^\circ - \theta) = \csc\theta\) (and each relation works in reverse, such as \(\cos(90^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta\)).
[/CALLOUT]

For example, suppose an acute angle \(\theta\) has \(\sin\theta = \frac{8}{17}\) and \(\cos\theta = \frac{15}{17}\). Without even drawing a triangle, [REF: basic-relations.2 | the quotient relation] gives \(\tan\theta = \frac{8/17}{15/17} = \frac{8}{15}\), [REF: basic-relations.1 | the reciprocal relation] gives \(\sec\theta = \frac{17}{15}\), and [REF: basic-relations.3 | the cofunction relations] give \(\cos(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{8}{17}\) and \(\cot(90^\circ - \theta) = \tan\theta = \frac{8}{15}\).

=== SECTION 3: Exact trigonometric ratios for \(30^\circ\), \(45^\circ\), and \(60^\circ\) ===

Most acute angles have trigonometric ratios that can only be approximated by decimals, but three angles—\(30^\circ\), \(45^\circ\), and \(60^\circ\)—come from elementary symmetric polygons whose side lengths are exact radicals. Rather than memorizing a wall of fractions, you can reconstruct every exact value in seconds from two standard reference triangles.

To get \(45^\circ\), cut a square of side length \(1\) along its diagonal. The diagonal bisects the \(90^\circ\) corners into two \(45^\circ\) angles, forming an isosceles right triangle with legs \(1\) and \(1\). By the Pythagorean theorem, the hypotenuse has length \(\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}\). Reading directly off this \(1\text{--}1\text{--}\sqrt{2}\) triangle and rationalizing denominators gives:
\[
\sin 45^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \qquad
\cos 45^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \qquad
\tan 45^\circ = \frac{1}{1} = 1,
\]
\[
\csc 45^\circ = \sqrt{2}, \qquad
\sec 45^\circ = \sqrt{2}, \qquad
\cot 45^\circ = 1.
\]

To get \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\), start with an equilateral triangle of side length \(2\) (all angles \(60^\circ\)) and drop an altitude from the top vertex to the midpoint of the base. That altitude splits the equilateral triangle into two congruent \(30^\circ\text{--}60^\circ\text{--}90^\circ\) right triangles, each with hypotenuse \(2\) and shorter leg \(1\) (half of the base). By the Pythagorean theorem, the altitude has length \(\sqrt{2^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt{3}\).

In the \(1\text{--}\sqrt{3}\text{--}2\) triangle, the side opposite \(30^\circ\) is \(1\) and the side adjacent to \(30^\circ\) is \(\sqrt{3}\), while for \(60^\circ\) those two legs trade places:
\[
\sin 30^\circ = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \cos 30^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \qquad \tan 30^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3},
\]
\[
\sin 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \qquad \cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \tan 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{1} = \sqrt{3}.
\]
Taking reciprocals immediately produces \(\csc\), \(\sec\), and \(\cot\) for both angles. Notice how [REF: basic-relations.3 | the cofunction relations] appear right across the rows of \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\): \(\sin 30^\circ = \cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2}\), \(\tan 30^\circ = \cot 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\), and \(\sec 30^\circ = \csc 60^\circ = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}\).

When combining special values in arithmetic expressions, keep exact radicals throughout and simplify at the end. For instance, to evaluate \(\sec 60^\circ \cdot \cot 30^\circ - (\sin 60^\circ)^2\), substitute \(\sec 60^\circ = 2\), \(\cot 30^\circ = \sqrt{3}\), and \(\sin 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\):
\[
\sec 60^\circ \cdot \cot 30^\circ - (\sin 60^\circ)^2 = 2\sqrt{3} - \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = 2\sqrt{3} - \frac{3}{4}.
\]
Notice also a quick ordering sanity check on sine: \(0 < \frac{1}{2} < \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} < \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} < 1\), which can be remembered as \(\frac{\sqrt{1}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) as \(\theta\) rises from \(30^\circ\) to \(45^\circ\) to \(60^\circ\), while cosine runs in the exact reverse order.

=== SECTION 4: Recovering all six ratios from a single given ratio ===

Because all right triangles with the same acute angle \(\theta\) are similar, knowing just one of the six trigonometric ratios of \(\theta\) fixes the shape of the triangle completely—and therefore determines all five remaining ratios exactly, without ever needing to know the degree measure of \(\theta\).

The strategy is a three-step geometric reconstruction:
1. Write the given ratio as a fraction matching its definition in [REF: six-ratios | the ratio table] (using denominator \(1\) if the given value is an integer or standalone radical).
2. Draw a reference right triangle with acute angle \(\theta\), label the two known sides with the numerator and denominator of that fraction, and compute the missing third side with the Pythagorean theorem \(\mathrm{opp}^2 + \mathrm{adj}^2 = \mathrm{hyp}^2\).
3. Read off the remaining five ratios directly from the labeled triangle and rationalize any radicals in denominators.

Let us work through a case where the missing side is not a whole number. Suppose \(\theta\) is an acute angle with \(\sec\theta = \frac{7}{3}\). Since \(\sec\theta = \frac{\mathrm{hyp}}{\mathrm{adj}}\), we choose a representative right triangle with \(\mathrm{hyp} = 7\) and \(\mathrm{adj} = 3\). Because we are finding a leg rather than the hypotenuse, the Pythagorean theorem gives
\[
\mathrm{opp}^2 + 3^2 = 7^2 \implies \mathrm{opp}^2 = 49 - 9 = 40 \implies \mathrm{opp} = \sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}.
\]
Be careful at this step: when both legs are given (from \(\tan\theta\) or \(\cot\theta\)), you add squares to find the hypotenuse; when the hypotenuse and one leg are given (from \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\), \(\csc\theta\), or \(\sec\theta\)), you subtract the leg's square from the hypotenuse's square.

With \(\mathrm{opp} = 2\sqrt{10}\), \(\mathrm{adj} = 3\), and \(\mathrm{hyp} = 7\), every remaining ratio follows directly:
\[
\sin\theta = \frac{2\sqrt{10}}{7}, \qquad
\cos\theta = \frac{3}{7}, \qquad
\tan\theta = \frac{2\sqrt{10}}{3},
\]
\[
\csc\theta = \frac{7}{2\sqrt{10}} = \frac{7\sqrt{10}}{20}, \qquad
\cot\theta = \frac{3}{2\sqrt{10}} = \frac{3\sqrt{10}}{20}.
\]
As a quick check, \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{2\sqrt{10}/7}{3/7} = \frac{2\sqrt{10}}{3} = \tan\theta\), and both \(\sin\theta \approx 0.904\) and \(\cos\theta \approx 0.429\) lie strictly between \(0\) and \(1\).

Why was it legal to set \(\mathrm{hyp} = 7\) and \(\mathrm{adj} = 3\) instead of \(7k\) and \(3k\) for an unknown scale factor \(k > 0\)? If you carry \(k\) along, then \(\mathrm{opp} = \sqrt{(7k)^2 - (3k)^2} = 2\sqrt{10}\,k\), and \(k\) cancels out of every ratio anyway. Setting \(k = 1\) simply chooses the most convenient similar triangle.
