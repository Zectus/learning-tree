=== SECTION 1: Unwrapping the unit circle into waves ===

So far we have viewed \(\sin t\) and \(\cos t\) as coordinates of a point circling around \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\). When studying tides, sound waves, or alternating electrical current, however, we want to track how the output changes as the radian input moves steadily along a horizontal number line. That means graphing \(y = \sin x\) and \(y = \cos x\) in the standard Cartesian plane.

Pause right here to notice a shift in the meaning of the letter \(x\). On the unit circle, \(x\) stood for the horizontal coordinate of the point \(P(x, y)\). On the graph of \(y = \sin x\) or \(y = \cos x\), \(x\) now stands for the real-number radian input (the angle / arc length), and \(y\) stands for the function's output value!

To build the graph of \(y = \sin x\) on \([0, 2\pi]\), track the vertical height of the moving unit-circle point at the five quarter-turn landmarks \(0, \frac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi\):
- At \(x = 0\), height is \(0\) (midline).
- At \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\), height peaks at \(+1\) (maximum).
- At \(x = \pi\), height returns to \(0\) (midline).
- At \(x = \frac{3\pi}{2}\), height drops to \(-1\) (minimum).
- At \(x = 2\pi\), height returns to \(0\) (completing one full cycle).

Now do the same for \(y = \cos x\) by tracking the unit-circle point's horizontal coordinate: it starts at its peak \(+1\) at \(x = 0\), crosses \(0\) at \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\), hits its trough \(-1\) at \(x = \pi\), crosses \(0\) again at \(x = \frac{3\pi}{2}\), and returns to \(+1\) at \(x = 2\pi\).

[TABLE]
Quarter-period input \(x\) | \(0\) | \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) | \(\pi\) | \(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\) | \(2\pi\)
\(y = \sin x\) | \(0\) | \(1\) (max) | \(0\) | \(-1\) (min) | \(0\)
\(y = \cos x\) | \(1\) (max) | \(0\) | \(-1\) (min) | \(0\) | \(1\) (max)
[/TABLE]

Because both functions are \(2\pi\)-periodic (\(\sin(x + 2\pi) = \sin x\) and \(\cos(x + 2\pi) = \cos x\)), the shape on \([0, 2\pi]\) repeats endlessly to the left and right across the entire real line \(\mathbb{R}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: smooth-wave-caution
label: Common pitfall
title: Never connect the five key points with straight V-shaped segments
tone: caution

The five quarter-period points are a skeleton, not corners of a zigzag. As a point moves along the unit circle near the top \((0, 1)\), its vertical coordinate changes very slowly while its horizontal motion is nearly flat; near \((1, 0)\), its vertical coordinate rises most rapidly. Therefore \(y = \sin x\) and \(y = \cos x\) are smooth, rounded waves that flatten out horizontally at every peak and trough and bow away from the \(x\)-axis between zeros and extrema.
[/CALLOUT]

You can verify that bowing quantitatively on \((0, \frac{\pi}{2})\): halfway from \(0\) to \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) is \(x = \frac{\pi}{4}\), where a straight line from \((0, 0)\) to \((\frac{\pi}{2}, 1)\) would have height \(0.5\), yet \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.707 > 0.5\).

=== SECTION 2: Core wave features—period, amplitude, zeros, extrema, and symmetry ===

With both waves visible across multiple cycles, we can read off their complete function anatomy directly from the graphs and the unit-circle properties.

[CALLOUT]
id: basic-wave-features
label: Summary
title: Key features of \(y = \sin x\) and \(y = \cos x\)
tone: key

(1) Domain and range: both have domain \(\mathbb{R} = (-\infty, \infty)\) and range \([-1, 1]\).
(2) Fundamental period: \(2\pi\) (the smallest positive length \(T\) for which \(f(x + T) = f(x)\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\)).
(3) Midline and amplitude: both oscillate about the horizontal midline \(y = 0\) with amplitude \(1\) (half the total height from minimum \(-1\) to maximum \(+1\)).
(4) Graph symmetry: \(y = \sin x\) is odd (\(\sin(-x) = -\sin x\), symmetric by a \(180^\circ\) rotation about the origin), whereas \(y = \cos x\) is even (\(\cos(-x) = \cos x\), symmetric across the \(y\)-axis).
[/CALLOUT]

Tracking where each function hits \(0\), \(+1\), and \(-1\) across all of \(\mathbb{R}\) (for any integer \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)) gives a clean pattern:

[TABLE]
Feature | \(y = \sin x\) | \(y = \cos x\)
\(x\)-intercepts (zeros) | \(x = k\pi\) | \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\)
Maxima (\(y = 1\)) | \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k\) | \(x = 2\pi k\)
Minima (\(y = -1\)) | \(x = \dfrac{3\pi}{2} + 2\pi k\) | \(x = \pi + 2\pi k\)
Symmetry | Odd (origin) | Even (\(y\)-axis)
[/TABLE]

Notice how zeros occur every half-period (\(\pi\) units apart), whereas consecutive maxima (or consecutive minima) occur every full period (\(2\pi\) units apart). Between any adjacent maximum and minimum lies exactly half a period (\(\pi\) units), and between an extremum and an adjacent zero lies exactly one quarter-period (\(\frac{\pi}{2}\) units).

=== SECTION 3: The quarter-period shift connecting sine and cosine ===

Look closely at the two curves in Section 1: \(y = \sin x\) and \(y = \cos x\) have the exact same wave shape! Every feature of \(y = \cos x\) simply occurs \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) units earlier (to the left) than the matching feature of \(y = \sin x\). For instance, \(y = \sin x\) reaches its peak \(1\) at \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\), whereas \(y = \cos x\) reaches its peak \(1\) at \(x = 0\).

Recall from function transformations that replacing \(x\) by \(x + c\) (with \(c > 0\)) shifts a graph LEFT by \(c\) units, while replacing \(x\) by \(x - c\) shifts it RIGHT by \(c\) units. Shifting \(y = \sin x\) left by \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) therefore gives:
\[
\cos x = \sin\!\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \qquad \text{for every real number } x.
\]
You can also see why this holds directly on the unit circle: rotating any terminal point \(P(\cos x, \sin x)\) counterclockwise by a quarter-turn (\(+\frac{\pi}{2}\)) sends \((a, b)\) to \((-b, a)\). Hence the new vertical coordinate \(\sin\!\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\) equals the old horizontal coordinate \(a = \cos x\)!

Conversely, shifting \(y = \cos x\) horizontally—or shifting \(y = \sin x\) by a half-period \(\pi\)—produces other basic waves. Notice what happens when you shift either wave by half a period (\(\pi\)): because adding \(\pi\) sends \((x, y)\) to \((-x, -y)\) on the unit circle, a horizontal shift by \(\pi\) acts identically to a vertical reflection across the \(x\)-axis:
\[
\sin(x + \pi) = -\sin x, \qquad \cos(x + \pi) = -\cos x.
\]
