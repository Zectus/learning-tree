=== SECTION 1: Turning circular and repeating motion into a sinusoidal model ===

Many real-world quantities rise and fall smoothly on a regular schedule: the height of a passenger on a Ferris wheel, the depth of water in a tidal harbor, the hours of daylight across a year, or the displacement of an idealized mass bouncing on a spring. Whenever a quantity repeats smoothly between a fixed maximum \(M\) and a fixed minimum \(m\) over a constant cycle time \(T\), we can model it with a sinusoidal function of time \(t\):
\[
y(t) = A\cos\!\bigl(B(t - C)\bigr) + D \qquad \text{or} \qquad y(t) = A\sin\!\bigl(B(t - C)\bigr) + D.
\]

A Ferris wheel makes the physical meaning of every parameter concrete. Suppose a wheel has radius \(r\text{ m}\), its lowest point sits \(2\text{ m}\) above the ground, and it rotates counterclockwise at a constant rate completing one full turn every \(T = 30\text{ s}\). A passenger boards at the very bottom at time \(t = 0\).

Track the height \(h(t)\) (in meters) above the ground:
- Minimum height: \(m = 2\text{ m}\) (at the bottom).
- Center height (midline): \(D = r + 2\text{ m}\) (height of the wheel's axle).
- Maximum height: \(M = 2r + 2\text{ m}\) (at the top).
- Amplitude: \(\lvert A \rvert = r\text{ m}\) (the wheel's radius, measuring the maximum vertical excursion above or below the axle).
- Angular frequency \(B\): one full turn takes \(T = 30\text{ s}\), so \(B = \frac{2\pi}{30} = \frac{\pi}{15}\text{ rad/s}\).

Because the passenger starts at the MINIMUM height at \(t = 0\), choosing a reflected cosine (\(-\cos\)) makes the phase shift \(C = 0\) automatically:
\[
h(t) = -r\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{15} t\right) + (r + 2).
\]
Check \(t = 0\): \(h(0) = -r(1) + r + 2 = 2\text{ m}\). Check half a rotation later at \(t = 15\text{ s}\): \(h(15) = -r(-1) + r + 2 = 2r + 2\text{ m}\).

Drag the wheel-radius slider \(r\) below and watch how changing the radius changes both the amplitude and the axle height \(D = r + 2\), while keeping the boarding height at \(2\text{ m}\) and the period at \(30\text{ s}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: modeling-workflow
label: Procedure
title: Four-step workflow for building a sinusoidal model
tone: key

(1) Vertical geometry: compute the midline \(D = \dfrac{M + m}{2}\) and amplitude \(\lvert A \rvert = \dfrac{M - m}{2}\) directly from the maximum \(M\) and minimum \(m\) (both carry the same physical units as the output).
(2) Period and \(B\): identify the full cycle length \(T\) (if given adjacent peak and trough times, double that half-cycle spacing), then compute \(B = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}\) (units: radians per unit of input).
(3) Smart template choice at \(t = 0\): pick the curve that eliminates phase shift whenever possible:
- Starts at maximum: \(+A\cos(Bt) + D\)
- Starts at minimum: \(-A\cos(Bt) + D\)
- Starts at midline rising: \(+A\sin(Bt) + D\)
- Starts at midline falling: \(-A\sin(Bt) + D\)
(4) Phase shift when needed: if no landmark sits at \(t = 0\), pick the clearest known maximum, minimum, or midline crossing at \(t = C\) and replace \(Bt\) with \(B(t - C)\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 2: Extracting models from extrema tables and phase-shifted data ===

In field measurements—such as tide charts or seasonal temperature records—a peak or trough rarely occurs right at \(t = 0\). Instead, you are typically given the times and values of an adjacent high point and low point.

Suppose a harbor tide chart reports high tide of \(4.6\text{ m}\) at \(3\text{:}00\text{ a.m.}\) (\(t = 3\text{ h}\)) and the immediately following low tide of \(1.0\text{ m}\) at \(9\text{:}00\text{ a.m.}\) (\(t = 9\text{ h}\)), with water depth \(h(t)\) modeled sinusoidally. Apply [REF: modeling-workflow | our four-step workflow]:
1. Midline and amplitude:
\[
D = \frac{4.6 + 1.0}{2} = 2.8\text{ m}, \qquad \lvert A \rvert = \frac{4.6 - 1.0}{2} = 1.8\text{ m}.
\]
2. Period: the interval from high tide at \(t = 3\) to the next low tide at \(t = 9\) is \(9 - 3 = 6\text{ h}\), which is HALF a cycle. Therefore the full period is \(T = 2(6) = 12\text{ h}\), giving
\[
B = \frac{2\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{6}\text{ rad/h}.
\]
3. Template and phase shift: since a maximum occurs at \(t = 3\), positive cosine shifted right by \(3\) hours matches immediately:
\[
h(t) = 1.8\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(t - 3)\right) + 2.8.
\]
We could equally well anchor a negative cosine at the low tide \(t = 9\), writing \(h(t) = -1.8\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(t - 9)\right) + 2.8\), or anchor a positive sine at \(t = 0\) (one quarter-period, \(3\text{ h}\), before the peak!), obtaining the even cleaner formula \(h(t) = 1.8\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6} t\right) + 2.8\). Checking \(t = 3\) gives \(1.8\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}) + 2.8 = 4.6\text{ m}\), and checking \(t = 9\) gives \(1.8\sin(\frac{3\pi}{2}) + 2.8 = 1.0\text{ m}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: half-cycle-pitfall
label: Common pitfall
title: Peak-to-trough time is HALF a period, not a full period
tone: caution

The time from a maximum to the immediately following minimum is \(\frac{T}{2}\). Setting \(T\) equal to that gap doubles \(B\) and makes the model oscillate twice as fast as the data. Always double the peak-to-trough spacing before computing \(B = \frac{2\pi}{T}\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Evaluating models, quarter-period landmarks, and interpreting units ===

Once a sinusoidal model is built, we interrogate it in three ways at this level:

1. Direct evaluation at a specified time \(t\): substitute \(t\) into the formula and evaluate the sine or cosine in radians! Never switch to degrees when \(B = \frac{2\pi}{T}\) was built in radians. For our harbor model \(h(t) = 1.8\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(t - 3)\right) + 2.8\), what is the depth at \(5\text{:}00\text{ a.m.}\) (\(t = 5\))?
\[
h(5) = 1.8\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(5 - 3)\right) + 2.8 = 1.8\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) + 2.8 = 1.8(0.5) + 2.8 = 3.7\text{ m}.
\]
2. Reading times of maxima, minima, and midline crossings via quarter-period symmetry: every adjacent pair of landmarks (max to midline, midline to min, min to midline, midline to max) is separated by exactly \(\frac{T}{4}\). In our \(12\)-hour tide model, \(\frac{T}{4} = 3\text{ h}\), so starting from the high tide at \(t = 3\text{ h}\):

[TABLE]
Time \(t\) (hours) | \(0\) | \(3\) | \(6\) | \(9\) | \(12\) | \(15\)
State | Midline rising | High tide | Midline falling | Low tide | Midline rising | High tide
Depth \(h(t)\) | \(2.8\text{ m}\) | \(4.6\text{ m}\) | \(2.8\text{ m}\) | \(1.0\text{ m}\) | \(2.8\text{ m}\) | \(4.6\text{ m}\)
[/TABLE]

3. Interpreting parameters physically with units:
- \(D\) is the average (equilibrium) value over one full cycle, in output units.
- \(\lvert A \rvert\) is the maximum deviation from the average, in output units; the total peak-to-trough swing is \(2\lvert A \rvert\).
- \(T = \frac{2\pi}{\lvert B \rvert}\) is the duration of one complete repetition, in input units; its reciprocal \(f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\lvert B \rvert}{2\pi}\) is the frequency (cycles per unit time).
- \(C\) is the time of the chosen reference landmark (such as the date of longest daylight), in input units.

What if you need to find the exact times when a sinusoid equals an arbitrary intermediate target height \(k\)—say when our harbor tide equals \(3.25\text{ m}\)—that is neither a maximum, a minimum, nor the midline? Solving \(A\cos\!\bigl(B(t - C)\bigr) + D = k\) for general intermediate values \(k\) requires inverse trigonometric equations and general solution families, which are developed systematically in Trigonometric Identities and Equations. Here we focus on direct evaluation and landmark timing via quarter-period symmetry.
