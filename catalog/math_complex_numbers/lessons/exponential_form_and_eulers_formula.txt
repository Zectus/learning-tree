=== SECTION 1: From polar multiplication to Euler's formula ===

In the polar form of complex numbers, the unit-circle factor \(\operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) behaves in a strikingly familiar way. For all real angles \(\theta, \theta_1, \theta_2\) measured in radians, we already established three algebraic identities:
\[
\operatorname{cis}(\theta_1)\,\operatorname{cis}(\theta_2) = \operatorname{cis}(\theta_1 + \theta_2), \qquad \operatorname{cis}(0) = 1, \qquad \operatorname{cis}(-\theta) = \frac{1}{\operatorname{cis}(\theta)}.
\]
Compare those three rules to the laws of an ordinary exponential function \(b^t\): \(b^{t_1} b^{t_2} = b^{t_1 + t_2}\), \(b^0 = 1\), and \(b^{-t} = 1/b^t\). The match is exact—multiplication turns into addition of inputs, \(0\) maps to \(1\), and negating the input produces the reciprocal.

In calculus, comparing power series or solving the differential equation \(f'(\theta) = i f(\theta)\) with \(f(0) = 1\) confirms that this exponential behavior is not merely an analogy: \(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) is literally the base-\(e\) exponential evaluated at the pure imaginary exponent \(i\theta\). Because those proofs rely on calculus tools outside our scope here, we accept Euler's formula as a supplied foundational identity and build its full algebraic and trigonometric power.

[CALLOUT]
id: eulers-formula
label: Formula (supplied)
title: Euler's formula and Euler's identity
tone: key

For every real angle \(\theta\) measured in radians:

(1) Euler's formula:
\[
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.
\]
(2) Unit modulus: \(|e^{i\theta}| = \sqrt{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta} = 1\), so \(e^{i\theta}\) traces the unit circle centered at \(0\) in the Argand plane.
(3) Periodicity: \(e^{i(\theta + 2\pi k)} = e^{i\theta}\) for every integer \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\); more generally, \(e^{i\alpha} = e^{i\beta}\) if and only if \(\alpha - \beta = 2\pi k\) for some \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\).
(4) Euler's identity: setting \(\theta = \pi\) gives \(e^{i\pi} = \cos\pi + i\sin\pi = -1\), or equivalently \(e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

Always verify that \(\theta\) is in RADIANS when using [REF: eulers-formula.1 | Euler's formula]: writing \(e^{i\pi/2} = i\) is exact, whereas substituting degrees into \(e^{i\theta}\) breaks the base-\(e\) relationship.

Because every nonzero complex number has polar form \(z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\) with \(r = |z| > 0\) and \(\theta \in \arg(z)\), [REF: eulers-formula.1 | Euler's formula] compresses it into exponential form:
\[
z = r e^{i\theta}.
\]
Every polar operation now obeys ordinary exponent laws automatically:
- Product: \((r_1 e^{i\theta_1})(r_2 e^{i\theta_2}) = r_1 r_2 e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}\).
- Quotient: \(\frac{r_1 e^{i\theta_1}}{r_2 e^{i\theta_2}} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} e^{i(\theta_1 - \theta_2)}\).
- Reciprocal: \(z^{-1} = r^{-1} e^{-i\theta}\).
- Conjugate: because \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\) and \(\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta\), we have \(\overline{e^{i\theta}} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta = e^{-i\theta}\), and hence \(\overline{r e^{i\theta}} = r e^{-i\theta}\).

[TABLE]
Standard form | Polar form | Exponential form | Key note
\(1\) | \(1\operatorname{cis}(0)\) | \(e^{0}\) | Positive real axis
\(i\) | \(1\operatorname{cis}(\pi/2)\) | \(e^{i\pi/2}\) | Quarter-turn rotation
\(-1\) | \(1\operatorname{cis}(\pi)\) | \(e^{i\pi}\) | Euler's identity \(e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0\)
\(-i\) | \(1\operatorname{cis}(-\pi/2)\) | \(e^{-i\pi/2}\) | Also equals \(e^{i3\pi/2}\)
\(-1 + i\) | \(\sqrt{2}\operatorname{cis}(3\pi/4)\) | \(\sqrt{2}\,e^{i3\pi/4}\) | Quadrant II principal argument
[/TABLE]

Let us see how cleanly exponential form handles a multi-step calculation. To write \(w = \frac{(1 + i\sqrt{3})(2i)}{-1 + i}\) in exponential and rectangular forms, convert each piece first: \(1 + i\sqrt{3} = 2e^{i\pi/3}\), \(2i = 2e^{i\pi/2}\), and \(-1 + i = \sqrt{2}\,e^{i3\pi/4}\). Then
\[
w = \frac{(2e^{i\pi/3})(2e^{i\pi/2})}{\sqrt{2}\,e^{i3\pi/4}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\,e^{i(\frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{3\pi}{4})} = 2\sqrt{2}\,e^{i\pi/12}.
\]
Note also how [REF: eulers-formula.3 | \(2\pi\)-periodicity] solves exponential angle equations: to find all \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\) satisfying \(e^{2i\theta} = -1 = e^{i\pi}\), set \(2\theta = \pi + 2\pi k\), giving \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + \pi k\), which produces \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\) (\(k = 0\)) and \(\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}\) (\(k = 1\)). Forgetting the \(+2\pi k\) term would miss the second solution completely.

In this lesson, the exponent \(i\theta\) is always pure imaginary (real \(\theta\)). Defining general complex exponentials \(e^{x + iy}\) as functions and defining complex logarithms belong to Complex Analysis.

=== SECTION 2: Euler's formulas for cosine and sine ===

Euler's formula turns complex exponentials into trigonometric functions, and we can just as easily run the bridge in reverse to express \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) purely in terms of \(e^{i\theta}\) and \(e^{-i\theta}\). Write [REF: eulers-formula.1 | Euler's formula] for \(\theta\) and for \(-\theta\):
\[
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta, \qquad e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta.
\]
Adding the two equations cancels \(i\sin\theta\) and gives \(e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta} = 2\cos\theta\). Subtracting the second from the first cancels \(\cos\theta\) and gives \(e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta\). Dividing by \(2\) and by \(2i\) yields Euler's trigonometric formulas.

[CALLOUT]
id: euler-trig-formulas
label: Identities
title: Euler's formulas for \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\)
tone: key

For every real \(\theta\):

(1) \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}\).
(2) \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Geometrically, these two formulas say something transparent on the unit circle: \(e^{i\theta}\) and \(e^{-i\theta} = \overline{e^{i\theta}}\) are reflections of each other across the real axis, so their midpoint \(\frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}\) lies on the real axis at \(\cos\theta\), while the vertical chord connecting them has signed height \(2\sin\theta\) (corresponding to \(e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta\)). Notice how effortlessly [REF: euler-trig-formulas | Euler's trigonometric formulas] prove trigonometric identities using nothing more than ordinary algebra! For example, to derive the double-angle identity for \(\cos^2\theta\), simply square \(\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}\):
\[
\cos^2\theta = \frac{(e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta})^2}{4} = \frac{e^{2i\theta} + 2 + e^{-2i\theta}}{4} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{e^{2i\theta} + e^{-2i\theta}}{2} = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}.
\]
Similarly, multiplying \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) gives
\[
2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2\left(\frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\right)\left(\frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}\right) = \frac{e^{2i\theta} - e^{-2i\theta}}{2i} = \sin(2\theta).
\]

=== SECTION 3: The half-angle factoring trick ===

Exponential form makes products and quotients trivial, but what about a SUM or DIFFERENCE of two unit-modulus complex numbers, such as \(1 + e^{i\theta}\), \(1 - e^{i\theta}\), or \(e^{i\alpha} + e^{i\beta}\)? Trying to convert back to rectangular form and take \(\sqrt{(1 + \cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta}\) works, but there is a far cleaner and more general technique that stays entirely inside exponential algebra: factor out the halfway exponent!

Look at \(1 + e^{i\theta} = e^{i\cdot 0} + e^{i\theta}\). The midpoint of the two exponents \(0\) and \(i\theta\) is \(i\theta/2\). Factoring \(e^{i\theta/2}\) out of BOTH terms makes the remaining exponents exact negatives of each other:
\[
1 + e^{i\theta} = e^{i\theta/2}\left(e^{-i\theta/2} + e^{i\theta/2}\right).
\]
By [REF: euler-trig-formulas.1 | Euler's cosine formula], the parenthesized sum is literally \(2\cos(\theta/2)\)! Thus
\[
1 + e^{i\theta} = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) e^{i\theta/2}.
\]
Doing the exact same factoring on \(1 - e^{i\theta}\) produces \(e^{-i\theta/2} - e^{i\theta/2} = -(e^{i\theta/2} - e^{-i\theta/2}) = -2i\sin(\theta/2)\) by [REF: euler-trig-formulas.2 | Euler's sine formula]:
\[
1 - e^{i\theta} = e^{i\theta/2}\left(e^{-i\theta/2} - e^{i\theta/2}\right) = -2i\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) e^{i\theta/2}.
\]
And for any two angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), factoring out the average exponent \(e^{i(\alpha + \beta)/2}\) gives
\[
e^{i\alpha} + e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)/2}\left(e^{i(\alpha - \beta)/2} + e^{-i(\alpha - \beta)/2}\right) = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}\right) e^{i(\alpha + \beta)/2}.
\]

[CALLOUT]
id: half-angle-factoring
label: Technique
title: Half-angle factoring for sums and differences of unit exponentials
tone: key

For every real \(\theta\):

(1) \(1 + e^{i\theta} = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) e^{i\theta/2}\).
(2) \(1 - e^{i\theta} = -2i\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) e^{i\theta/2} = 2\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) e^{i(\theta - \pi)/2}\) (using \(-i = e^{-i\pi/2}\)).
[/CALLOUT]

There is one essential sign check whenever you use [REF: half-angle-factoring | half-angle factoring] to read off the modulus \(r\) and argument \(\phi\) of \(z = r e^{i\phi}\): the modulus \(r\) MUST be nonnegative!
- For example, take \(z = 1 + e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}}\). Here \(\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{\pi}{3}\), so \(\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{2} > 0\), giving \(z = 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)e^{i\pi/3} = e^{i\pi/3}\) directly (modulus \(1\), argument \(\frac{\pi}{3}\)).
- Now contrast \(z = 1 + e^{i\frac{5\pi}{3}}\). Here \(\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{5\pi}{6}\), so \(\cos\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} < 0\)! Writing \(z = -\sqrt{3}\,e^{i5\pi/6}\) is algebraically true, but \(-\sqrt{3}\) is NOT the modulus because it is negative. Absorb the minus sign as \(-1 = e^{-i\pi}\):
\[
z = \sqrt{3}\,e^{-i\pi} e^{i5\pi/6} = \sqrt{3}\,e^{-i\pi/6}.
\]
Now the modulus is \(r = \sqrt{3} > 0\) and the principal argument is \(-\frac{\pi}{6} \in (-\pi, \pi]\). Checking in rectangular form: \(1 + e^{i5\pi/3} = 1 + \left(\frac{1}{2} - i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = \frac{3}{2} - i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \sqrt{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}i\right) = \sqrt{3}\,e^{-i\pi/6}\).
