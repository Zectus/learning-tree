=== SECTION 1: Solving \(z^n = 1\) in \(\mathbb{C}\) ===

Over the real numbers, the equation \(z^n = 1\) has very few solutions: only \(z = 1\) when \(n\) is odd, and \(z = \pm 1\) when \(n\) is even. Over \(\mathbb{C}\), however, the polynomial \(z^n - 1\) has degree \(n\), so we expect \(n\) distinct solutions. How do we find all of them systematically, and where do they sit in the Argand plane?

Write an unknown solution \(z \ne 0\) in exponential form \(z = r e^{i\theta}\) with \(r > 0\) and \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}\). By De Moivre's Theorem, \(z^n = r^n e^{in\theta}\). Meanwhile, \(1 = 1\cdot e^{i\cdot 0}\). Thus \(z^n = 1\) becomes
\[
r^n e^{in\theta} = 1\cdot e^{i\cdot 0}.
\]
Two nonzero complex numbers in exponential form are equal if and only if their positive moduli match and their arguments differ by an integer multiple of \(2\pi\):
- Matching moduli: \(r^n = 1\) with \(r > 0\). Because \(r \mapsto r^n\) is strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\), the unique positive real solution is \(r = 1\). Every \(n\)th root of unity lies on the unit circle \(|z| = 1\).
- Matching arguments modulo \(2\pi\): \(n\theta = 0 + 2\pi k\) for some integer \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\), which gives
\[
\theta_k = \frac{2\pi k}{n}, \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}.
\]
This produces complex numbers \(\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k / n} = \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right) + i\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right)\).

At first glance, letting \(k\) range over all integers seems to give infinitely many solutions. Why do we get EXACTLY \(n\) distinct roots? Check both directions:
1. Distinctness for \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1\): if \(0 \le j < k \le n - 1\), then the angle difference satisfies \(0 < \theta_k - \theta_j = \frac{2\pi(k - j)}{n} < 2\pi\), so it is NOT a multiple of \(2\pi\); hence \(\omega_j \ne \omega_k\).
2. Repetition for all other \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\): by integer division by \(n\), write any \(k = qn + r\) with \(q \in \mathbb{Z}\) and remainder \(r \in \{0, 1, \dots, n - 1\}\). Then
\[
\omega_k = e^{2\pi i(qn + r)/n} = e^{2\pi i q} e^{2\pi i r / n} = 1 \cdot \omega_r = \omega_r.
\]
Therefore every integer \(k\) repeats one of the \(n\) roots \(\omega_0, \omega_1, \dots, \omega_{n-1}\). Notice also that setting \(\omega = \omega_1 = e^{2\pi i/n}\) gives \(\omega_k = (e^{2\pi i/n})^k = \omega^k\) for every \(k\).

[CALLOUT]
id: roots-of-unity-def
label: Theorem
title: The \(n\)th roots of unity
tone: key

For every integer \(n \ge 1\), the equation \(z^n = 1\) has exactly \(n\) distinct complex solutions, called the \(n\)th roots of unity:
\[
\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k / n} = \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right) + i\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right) = \omega^k, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1,
\]
where \(\omega = e^{2\pi i / n}\).

(1) All \(n\) roots lie on the unit circle \(|z| = 1\) at angles spaced by \(\frac{2\pi}{n}\).
(2) For \(n \ge 3\), they form the vertices of a regular \(n\)-gon inscribed in the unit circle with one vertex at \(\omega_0 = 1\).
(3) Every root satisfies \(\overline{\omega_k} = \omega_k^{-1} = \omega_{n - k}\) (for \(1 \le k \le n - 1\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us list the first few small cases concretely:
- \(n = 2\): \(1\) and \(-1\).
- \(n = 3\) (cube roots of unity): \(1\), \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = -\frac{1}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), and \(\omega^2 = e^{4\pi i/3} = -\frac{1}{2} - i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) (an equilateral triangle).
- \(n = 4\): \(1, i, -1, -i\) (a square).
- \(n = 5\): \(1, e^{2\pi i/5}, e^{4\pi i/5}, e^{6\pi i/5}, e^{8\pi i/5}\) (a regular pentagon).

The plot below shows the \(5\)th roots of unity inscribed in the unit circle. Notice how \(\omega^1\) and \(\omega^4 = \overline{\omega^1}\) are symmetric across the real axis, as are \(\omega^2\) and \(\omega^3 = \overline{\omega^2}\), while every step from \(\omega^k\) to \(\omega^{k+1}\) rotates counterclockwise by \(\frac{2\pi}{5} = 72^\circ\).

=== SECTION 2: Vanishing sums and primitive roots of unity ===

Look at the regular pentagon above. If you place five equal masses at the five vertices on the unit circle, where is their center of mass? By rotational symmetry, it MUST be the origin \(0\)! That geometric intuition corresponds to one of the most useful algebraic identities in complex numbers.

[CALLOUT]
id: vanishing-sum-identity
label: Theorem
title: Vanishing sum of \(n\)th roots of unity
tone: key

For every integer \(n \ge 2\) and every \(n\)th root of unity \(\zeta\) with \(\zeta \ne 1\):
\[
1 + \zeta + \zeta^2 + \dots + \zeta^{n-1} = 0.
\]
In particular, the sum of all \(n\)th roots of unity is \(0\): \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \omega^k = 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

We can prove [REF: vanishing-sum-identity | the vanishing sum identity] in two complementary ways:
- Algebraic proof: expand \((\zeta - 1)(1 + \zeta + \zeta^2 + \dots + \zeta^{n-1})\) to see every middle term telescope, leaving \(\zeta^n - 1\). Because \(\zeta\) is an \(n\)th root of unity, \(\zeta^n - 1 = 0\). Because \(\zeta \ne 1\), we have \(\zeta - 1 \ne 0\), so dividing by \(\zeta - 1\) forces \(1 + \zeta + \zeta^2 + \dots + \zeta^{n-1} = 0\).
- Geometric symmetry proof (for the full sum \(S = 1 + \omega + \omega^2 + \dots + \omega^{n-1}\)): multiplying \(S\) by \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/n}\) rotates every vertex counterclockwise by \(\frac{2\pi}{n}\), sending \(\omega^k \mapsto \omega^{k+1}\) and wrapping \(\omega^{n-1} \mapsto \omega^n = 1\). Thus \(\omega S\) sums the exact same \(n\) vertices in shifted order, so \(\omega S = S\), i.e., \((\omega - 1)S = 0\). Since \(\omega \ne 1\), we conclude \(S = 0\).

Notice a subtle distinction inside [REF: roots-of-unity-def | the set of \(n\)th roots of unity]. Take \(n = 6\) with \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/6} = e^{i\pi/3}\). The six roots are \(1, \omega, \omega^2, \omega^3, \omega^4, \omega^5\). Look at the powers generated by each individual root:
- Powers of \(\omega_1 = \omega\): \(\omega^0, \omega^1, \omega^2, \omega^3, \omega^4, \omega^5\) — all \(6\) roots!
- Powers of \(\omega_2 = \omega^2 = e^{2\pi i/3}\): \(1, \omega^2, \omega^4\), and then \((\omega^2)^3 = 1\) — only \(3\) roots (it is really a cube root of unity!).
- Powers of \(\omega_3 = \omega^3 = -1\): \(1, -1\) — only \(2\) roots.
- Powers of \(\omega_5 = \omega^5 = e^{-i\pi/3}\): \(1, \omega^5, \omega^4, \omega^3, \omega^2, \omega^1\) — all \(6\) roots!

An \(n\)th root of unity \(\zeta\) is called a primitive \(n\)th root of unity if \(n\) is the SMALLEST positive integer \(m\) such that \(\zeta^m = 1\) (equivalently, its powers \(1, \zeta, \zeta^2, \dots, \zeta^{n-1}\) generate all \(n\) roots of unity without repeating early).

Which \(\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}\) (\(0 \le k \le n - 1\)) are primitive? Let \(d = \gcd(k, n)\). If \(d > 1\), write \(k = d k'\) and \(n = d n'\) with \(1 \le n' < n\); then
\[
\omega_k^{n'} = e^{2\pi i (d k') n' / (d n')} = e^{2\pi i k'} = 1,
\]
so \(\omega_k\) already hits \(1\) at the smaller exponent \(n' < n\) and is NOT primitive. Conversely, if \(\gcd(k, n) = 1\), then \(\omega_k^m = 1 \iff \frac{km}{n} \in \mathbb{Z} \iff n \mid km\); since \(n\) and \(k\) share no common factor greater than \(1\), \(n\) must divide \(m\), so the smallest positive exponent is \(m = n\).

[CALLOUT]
id: primitive-roots-criterion
label: Theorem
title: Primitive \(n\)th roots of unity
tone: key

An \(n\)th root of unity \(\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k / n}\) (\(0 \le k \le n - 1\)) is a primitive \(n\)th root of unity if and only if \(\gcd(k, n) = 1\).
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 3: Factoring \(z^n - 1\) over \(\mathbb{C}\) and over \(\mathbb{R}\) ===

Because \(\omega_0, \omega_1, \dots, \omega_{n-1}\) are the \(n\) distinct roots of the monic degree-\(n\) polynomial \(z^n - 1\), its complete factorization over \(\mathbb{C}\) is
\[
z^n - 1 = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(z - \omega_k) = (z - 1)(z - \omega_1)(z - \omega_2)\cdots(z - \omega_{n-1}).
\]
Now pair the non-real conjugate roots to obtain the complete factorization of \(z^n - 1\) into irreducible factors over \(\mathbb{R}\). By [REF: roots-of-unity-def.3 | conjugate pairing], for every index \(k\) strictly between \(0\) and \(n/2\), the conjugate of \(\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}\) is \(\omega_{n-k} = e^{-2\pi i k/n}\). Multiplying their linear factors gives
\[
(z - \omega_k)(z - \omega_{n-k}) = z^2 - (\omega_k + \overline{\omega_k})z + |\omega_k|^2 = z^2 - 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right)z + 1.
\]
What remains unpaired on the real axis?
- Always \(k = 0\), giving the real linear factor \((z - 1)\).
- When \(n\) is even, also \(k = n/2\) (\(\omega_{n/2} = e^{i\pi} = -1\)), giving the real linear factor \((z + 1)\).

[CALLOUT]
id: zn-minus-1-real-factor
label: Formula
title: Complete real factorization of \(z^n - 1\)
tone: key

(1) If \(n = 2m + 1\) is odd:
\[
z^n - 1 = (z - 1)\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(z^2 - 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right)z + 1\right).
\]
(2) If \(n = 2m\) is even:
\[
z^n - 1 = (z - 1)(z + 1)\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}\left(z^2 - 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right)z + 1\right).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: zn-minus-1-real-factor | these formulas] to \(n = 6\) and \(n = 5\):
- For \(n = 6\) (even, \(m = 3\)): the real roots give \((z - 1)(z + 1)\), and the conjugate pairs \(k = 1\) (\(\frac{2\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{3}\), \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}\)) and \(k = 2\) (\(\frac{4\pi}{6} = \frac{2\pi}{3}\), \(\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}\)) give
\[
z^6 - 1 = (z - 1)(z + 1)(z^2 - z + 1)(z^2 + z + 1),
\]
matching the classical algebraic factorization \((z^3 - 1)(z^3 + 1)\).
- For \(n = 5\) (odd, \(m = 2\)):
\[
z^5 - 1 = (z - 1)\left(z^2 - 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)z + 1\right)\left(z^2 - 2\cos\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)z + 1\right).
\]
This factorization would be nearly invisible from elementary algebra alone, yet roots of unity deliver it in one line!
