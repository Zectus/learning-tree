=== SECTION 1: Argument loci — open rays, sectors, and circular arcs ===

In our earlier lesson on the Argand plane, modulus equations like \(|z - z_0| = r\) and \(|z - a| = |z - b|\) described circles and perpendicular bisectors. Now that we have polar form and the principal argument \(\operatorname{Arg}(z) \in (-\pi, \pi]\), what geometric figures arise when we constrain ARGUMENTS instead of distances?

Start with the simplest argument constraint:
\[
\operatorname{Arg}(z - z_0) = \alpha, \qquad \alpha \in (-\pi, \pi].
\]
Geometrically, \(z - z_0\) is the vector pointing FROM the fixed point \(z_0\) TO the variable point \(z\). Requiring \(\operatorname{Arg}(z - z_0) = \alpha\) fixes the exact direction of that vector — angle \(\alpha\) counterclockwise from the positive real direction — while leaving its positive length \(r = |z - z_0| > 0\) completely free. Therefore the locus is the OPEN RAY starting at \(z_0\) and heading infinitely in direction \(\alpha\).
- Crucially, the vertex \(z_0\) itself is EXCLUDED from the locus, because at \(z = z_0\) we have \(z - z_0 = 0\), and \(\arg(0)\) is undefined!
- Similarly, an inequality such as \(\alpha \le \operatorname{Arg}(z - z_0) \le \beta\) (with \(-\pi < \alpha < \beta \le \pi\)) describes an angular sector (wedge) with vertex \(z_0\) excluded, bounded by the two rays at angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).

Now take the next step: what is the locus of a QUOTIENT argument constraint
\[
\operatorname{Arg}\!\left(\frac{z - a}{z - b}\right) = \alpha
\]
for two distinct fixed points \(a, b \in \mathbb{C}\)? Recall the polar quotient law:
\[
\arg\!\left(\frac{z - a}{z - b}\right) \equiv \arg(z - a) - \arg(z - b) \pmod{2\pi}.
\]
Interpret that difference geometrically! The vector \(z - b\) points from \(b\) to \(z\), and \(z - a\) points from \(a\) to \(z\). Turning from the direction of \(z - b\) to the direction of \(z - a\) is the exact same angle as standing at \(z\) and turning counterclockwise from the ray \(\overrightarrow{zb}\) to the ray \(\overrightarrow{za}\) — that is, the directed inscribed angle \(\angle bza\)!

From Euclidean geometry (the Inscribed Angle Theorem), the set of points \(z\) on one side of the chord \(ab\) that see the chord at a constant inscribed angle \(|\alpha|\) (for \(0 < |\alpha| < \pi\)) is an OPEN CIRCULAR ARC joining \(a\) and \(b\) (with both endpoints \(a\) and \(b\) EXCLUDED, since at \(z = a\) the numerator is \(0\) and at \(z = b\) we divide by \(0\)).

Let us check a concrete example carefully so we get the orientation and major/minor arc distinction 100% right! Take \(a = 1\), \(b = -1\), and consider points \(z = iy\) on the positive imaginary axis (\(y > 0\)):
- \(z - a = -1 + iy\) sits in Quadrant II with angle \(\pi - \arctan(y)\).
- \(z - b = 1 + iy\) sits in Quadrant I with angle \(\arctan(y)\).
- Hence \(\operatorname{Arg}\!\left(\frac{z - 1}{z + 1}\right) = \pi - 2\arctan(y)\).

Plug in \(\alpha = \frac{\pi}{3}\): solving \(\pi - 2\arctan(y) = \frac{\pi}{3}\) gives \(\arctan(y) = \frac{\pi}{3}\), so \(y = \sqrt{3} > 1\). This arc arches HIGH above the unit circle — it is a MAJOR arc (greater than a semicircle)! By contrast, what happens on the LOWER portion of the EXACT SAME circle (centered at \((0, \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}})\) with radius \(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\), passing through \(1, -1,\) and \(i\sqrt{3}\))? At its lowest point \(z = -\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}}\), we compute \(\arg(-1 - \frac{i}{\sqrt{3}}) = -\frac{5\pi}{6}\) and \(\arg(1 - \frac{i}{\sqrt{3}}) = -\frac{\pi}{6}\), so
\[
\operatorname{Arg}\!\left(\frac{z - 1}{z + 1}\right) = -\frac{5\pi}{6} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = -\frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{3} - \pi!
\]
That lower piece is the COMPLEMENTARY MINOR arc of the same circle! Why did the principal argument jump by \(-\pi\) when crossing the chord \(ab\)? Because an inscribed quadrilateral's opposite angles sum to \(\pi\), AND crossing the chord reverses whether the turn from \(\overrightarrow{zb}\) to \(\overrightarrow{za}\) is counterclockwise (positive) or clockwise (negative), flipping \(\alpha\) to \(\alpha - \pi\)!

The plot below shows both complementary arcs on the circle centered at \((0, \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}})\) of radius \(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\), along with the sample point \(z = i\sqrt{3}\) where the counterclockwise angle from \(\overrightarrow{zb}\) to \(\overrightarrow{za}\) is \(\frac{\pi}{3}\). (Note that the continuous curves visually meet at \(a = 1\) and \(b = -1\), even though both endpoints are excluded from the argument loci.)

[CALLOUT]
id: arg-loci-catalog
label: Summary
title: Geometric loci of \(\operatorname{Arg}\!\left(\frac{z - a}{z - b}\right) = \alpha\) (\(a \ne b\), endpoints \(a, b\) always excluded)
tone: key

(1) \(\alpha = 0\): the two open rays on the line through \(a\) and \(b\) OUTSIDE the segment \(ab\) (where \(z - a\) and \(z - b\) point in the same direction).
(2) \(\alpha = \pi\): the open line segment STRICTLY BETWEEN \(a\) and \(b\) (where \(z - a\) and \(z - b\) point in opposite directions).
(3) \(|\alpha| = \frac{\pi}{2}\): an open semicircle having \(ab\) as diameter.
(4) \(0 < |\alpha| < \frac{\pi}{2}\) (acute inscribed angle): an open MAJOR circular arc joining \(a\) and \(b\).
(5) \(\frac{\pi}{2} < |\alpha| < \pi\) (obtuse inscribed angle): an open MINOR circular arc joining \(a\) and \(b\).
(6) Replacing \(\alpha > 0\) by \(\alpha - \pi\) (or \(\alpha < 0\) by \(\alpha + \pi\)) gives the complementary open arc on the exact same circle.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 2: Planar transformations and spiral similarities in \(\mathbb{C}\) ===

Complex arithmetic encodes every rigid motion and similarity of the Euclidean plane into a single line of algebra! We already know that adding \(b\) translates every point by \(b\), and multiplying by \(r e^{i\theta}\) (\(r > 0\)) scales by \(r\) and rotates counterclockwise by \(\theta\) around the origin \(0\).

How do we rotate a point \(z\) counterclockwise by an angle \(\theta\) around an ARBITRARY pivot point \(z_0 \ne 0\)? Do it in three elementary steps:
1. Translate the plane by \(-z_0\) so the pivot moves to the origin: \(z \mapsto z - z_0\).
2. Rotate around the origin by \(\theta\): \((z - z_0) \mapsto e^{i\theta}(z - z_0)\).
3. Translate back by \(+z_0\) to restore the pivot:
\[
z' = z_0 + e^{i\theta}(z - z_0).
\]
Notice the exact same three-step conjugation works for scaling (dilation) by a positive factor \(k > 0\) from a pivot \(z_0\): \(z' = z_0 + k(z - z_0)\).

What about reflections? Conjugation \(z \mapsto \bar{z}\) reflects across the real axis, and \(z \mapsto -\bar{z}\) reflects across the imaginary axis. To reflect across a line through the origin making angle \(\theta\) with the positive real axis, rotate the line onto the real axis (\(e^{-i\theta} z\)), conjugate (\(\overline{e^{-i\theta} z} = e^{i\theta}\bar{z}\)), and rotate back by \(e^{i\theta}\):
\[
z' = e^{2i\theta}\bar{z}.
\]

Finally, combine scaling, rotation, and translation into the general linear map
\[
T(z) = a z + b, \qquad a, b \in \mathbb{C},\quad a \ne 0.
\]
Writing \(a = r e^{i\theta}\) (\(r = |a| > 0\), \(\theta = \arg(a)\)), \(T\) scales every distance by \(|a|\), rotates every direction counterclockwise by \(\arg(a)\), and shifts by \(b\) — it is an orientation-preserving (direct) similarity, also called a SPIRAL SIMILARITY. Whenever \(a \ne 1\), \(T\) has a UNIQUE fixed point \(z_0\) satisfying \(a z_0 + b = z_0\), namely \(z_0 = \frac{b}{1 - a}\). Subtracting \(z_0 = a z_0 + b\) from \(T(z) = a z + b\) gives
\[
T(z) - z_0 = a(z - z_0) \implies T(z) = z_0 + a(z - z_0),
\]
proving that EVERY direct similarity with \(a \ne 1\) is simply a scaling by \(|a|\) combined with a rotation by \(\arg(a)\) centered at its fixed point \(z_0\)! (When \(a = 1\), \(T(z) = z + b\) is a pure translation.)

[TABLE]
Planar transformation | Complex formula | Key invariants or parameters
Translation by \(b\) | \(z \mapsto z + b\) | Preserves lengths, angles, orientation; no fixed point if \(b \ne 0\)
Rotation by \(\theta\) about \(z_0\) | \(z \mapsto z_0 + e^{i\theta}(z - z_0)\) | Fixes \(z_0\); preserves distances and orientation
Dilation by \(k > 0\) from \(z_0\) | \(z \mapsto z_0 + k(z - z_0)\) | Fixes \(z_0\); scales all lengths by \(k\); preserves directions
Reflection across line through \(0\) at angle \(\theta\) | \(z \mapsto e^{2i\theta}\bar{z}\) | Preserves lengths; reverses orientation
Direct (spiral) similarity (\(a \ne 0, 1\)) | \(z \mapsto a z + b\) | Fixed center \(z_0 = \frac{b}{1 - a}\); scale \(\lvert a \rvert\), rotation \(\arg(a)\)
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 3: Solving Euclidean geometry problems with complex rotation ===

Because multiplying a vector by \(i = e^{i\pi/2}\) rotates it \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise and multiplying by \(e^{\pm i\pi/3} = \frac{1 \pm i\sqrt{3}}{2}\) rotates it \(\pm 60^\circ\), many classical Euclidean coordinate-geometry problems collapse into two lines of complex arithmetic!

Worked example (locating vertices of a square): Suppose \(z_1 = 1 + i\) and \(z_2 = 4 + 3i\) are two adjacent vertices of a square listed in counterclockwise order \(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4\). Find \(z_3\) and \(z_4\).
1. Compute the edge vector from \(z_1\) to \(z_2\): \(v = z_2 - z_1 = (4 + 3i) - (1 + i) = 3 + 2i\).
2. Rotate \(v\) counterclockwise by \(90^\circ\) to get the adjacent edge vector from \(z_1\) to \(z_4\) (and equally from \(z_2\) to \(z_3\)):
   \[
   i v = i(3 + 2i) = -2 + 3i.
   \]
3. Add \(i v\) to \(z_1\) and \(z_2\):
   \[
   z_4 = z_1 + i v = (1 + i) + (-2 + 3i) = -1 + 4i, \qquad z_3 = z_2 + i v = (4 + 3i) + (-2 + 3i) = 2 + 6i.
   \]
No slopes, no distance formulas, no quadratic equations — just one multiplication by \(i\)! (If the vertices were listed clockwise instead, we would multiply by \(-i\) rather than \(+i\).)

Now apply complex rotation to prove one of the most elegant algebraic characterizations in planar geometry: when do three complex numbers \(z_1, z_2, z_3\) form an equilateral triangle?

[CALLOUT]
id: equilateral-criterion
label: Theorem
title: Algebraic criterion for an equilateral triangle in \(\mathbb{C}\)
tone: key

Three distinct complex numbers \(z_1, z_2, z_3 \in \mathbb{C}\) are the vertices of an equilateral triangle if and only if
\[
z_1^2 + z_2^2 + z_3^2 = z_1 z_2 + z_2 z_3 + z_3 z_1.
\]
(If we allow repeated vertices, the equation also holds for the degenerate triple \(z_1 = z_2 = z_3\).)
[/CALLOUT]

Let us derive [REF: equilateral-criterion | this criterion] directly from \(60^\circ\) rotation!
- First observe that the two \(60^\circ\) rotation factors \(e^{i\pi/3} = \frac{1 + i\sqrt{3}}{2}\) and \(e^{-i\pi/3} = \frac{1 - i\sqrt{3}}{2}\) are the exact two complex roots of the quadratic equation \(\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1 = 0\) (since their sum is \(1\) and their product is \(1\)).
- Now take three distinct points \(z_1, z_2, z_3 \in \mathbb{C}\) (so \(z_2 - z_1 \ne 0\)). They form an equilateral triangle (either counterclockwise or clockwise) if and only if rotating the side vector \(z_2 - z_1\) by \(+\frac{\pi}{3}\) or \(-\frac{\pi}{3}\) produces the side vector \(z_3 - z_1\); that is,
  \[
  \frac{z_3 - z_1}{z_2 - z_1} = e^{\pm i\pi/3}.
  \]
- Setting \(\lambda = \frac{z_3 - z_1}{z_2 - z_1}\), this holds if and only if \(\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1 = 0\). Because \(z_2 - z_1 \ne 0\), multiplying through by \((z_2 - z_1)^2\) is completely reversible:
  \[
  (z_3 - z_1)^2 - (z_3 - z_1)(z_2 - z_1) + (z_2 - z_1)^2 = 0.
  \]
- Expand each term carefully:
  \[
  (z_3^2 - 2z_1 z_3 + z_1^2) - (z_2 z_3 - z_1 z_3 - z_1 z_2 + z_1^2) + (z_2^2 - 2z_1 z_2 + z_1^2) = 0.
  \]
- Collecting like terms cancels one \(z_1^2\), simplifies \(-2z_1 z_3 + z_1 z_3 = -z_1 z_3\), and simplifies \(z_1 z_2 - 2z_1 z_2 = -z_1 z_2\), leaving
  \[
  z_1^2 + z_2^2 + z_3^2 - z_1 z_2 - z_2 z_3 - z_3 z_1 = 0 \iff z_1^2 + z_2^2 + z_3^2 = z_1 z_2 + z_2 z_3 + z_3 z_1!
  \]
Every single step is reversible for distinct points, so both directions of the biconditional are established in one clean derivation!
