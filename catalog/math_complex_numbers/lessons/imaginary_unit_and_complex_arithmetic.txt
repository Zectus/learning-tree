=== SECTION 1: Why the real numbers are not enough ===

In real algebra, every real number \(x\) satisfies \(x^2 \ge 0\). That single fact makes the equation
\[
x^2 + 1 = 0
\]
impossible to solve inside \(\mathbb{R}\), because no real number squares to \(-1\). For centuries this looked like a dead end—just as negative numbers once looked impossible when people worked only with positive quantities. Yet enlarging the number system by adjoining a single new number whose square is \(-1\) does far more than solve \(x^2 + 1 = 0\): it builds a self-contained arithmetic in which every quadratic equation—and eventually every polynomial equation—has solutions, while preserving the familiar algebraic laws of addition and multiplication.

To make this extension precise, we introduce a new symbol \(i\), called the imaginary unit, defined by the single algebraic property
\[
i^2 = -1.
\]
We do not try to prove that \(i^2 = -1\) from real numbers; this equation is the defining rule of \(i\). Building all expressions of the form \(a + bi\) from real numbers \(a\) and \(b\) yields the set of complex numbers.

[CALLOUT]
id: complex-number-def
label: Definition
title: The complex numbers in standard form
tone: key

The set of complex numbers, denoted \(\mathbb{C}\), is
\[
\mathbb{C} = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{R}\},
\]
where \(i\) is the imaginary unit satisfying \(i^2 = -1\). Writing a complex number as \(z = a + bi\) with \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\) is called its standard (or rectangular) form.

(1) The real number \(a\) is the real part of \(z\), written \(\operatorname{Re}(z) = a\).
(2) The real number \(b\) is the imaginary part of \(z\), written \(\operatorname{Im}(z) = b\).
(3) Two complex numbers \(a + bi\) and \(c + di\) (with \(a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}\)) are equal if and only if \(a = c\) and \(b = d\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how every real number \(a\) sits naturally inside \(\mathbb{C}\) as \(a + 0i\): the real numbers are precisely the complex numbers with \(\operatorname{Im}(z) = 0\). At the opposite extreme, a complex number of the form \(bi\) with \(b \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(b \ne 0\) (meaning \(\operatorname{Re}(z) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z) \ne 0\)) is called a pure imaginary number. For example, \(5i\) and \(-\sqrt{2}\,i\) are pure imaginary, whereas \(0 = 0 + 0i\) is real.

Pay close attention to [REF: complex-number-def.2 | the definition of the imaginary part]: \(\operatorname{Im}(z)\) is the real coefficient \(b\), without the factor \(i\).

[CALLOUT]
id: imag-part-caution
label: Common pitfall
title: The imaginary part is a real number
tone: caution

For \(z = 4 - 7i\), we have \(\operatorname{Re}(z) = 4\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z) = -7\), not \(-7i\). Both \(\operatorname{Re}(z)\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z)\) are always real numbers.
[/CALLOUT]

Similarly, for \(z = 3i = 0 + 3i\), we have \(\operatorname{Re}(z) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z) = 3\); for \(z = -6 = -6 + 0i\), we have \(\operatorname{Re}(z) = -6\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z) = 0\); and for \(z = 1 - i\), we have \(\operatorname{Re}(z) = 1\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z) = -1\).

The equality rule in [REF: complex-number-def.3 | part (3)] is what turns a single complex equation into two ordinary real equations. Suppose real numbers \(u\) and \(v\) satisfy
\[
(3u - 1) + (u + 2v)i = 8 + 7i.
\]
Matching real parts gives \(3u - 1 = 8\), so \(u = 3\). Matching imaginary parts gives \(u + 2v = 7\); substituting \(u = 3\) gives \(2v = 4\), so \(v = 2\). Notice how matching the coefficients of \(1\) and \(i\) independently requires both \(u\) and \(v\) to be real—otherwise a real-looking term could hide a multiple of \(i\).

=== SECTION 2: Cyclic integer powers of the imaginary unit ===

What happens when we raise \(i\) to successive integer powers? Starting from \(i^0 = 1\) and \(i^1 = i\), multiplying repeatedly by \(i\) and replacing \(i^2\) with \(-1\) gives:
\[
i^0 = 1, \qquad i^1 = i, \qquad i^2 = -1, \qquad i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i, \qquad i^4 = (i^2)^2 = (-1)^2 = 1.
\]
Because \(i^4 = 1\), the sequence immediately begins repeating: \(i^5 = i^4 \cdot i = i\), \(i^6 = i^4 \cdot i^2 = -1\), \(i^7 = i^4 \cdot i^3 = -i\), and \(i^8 = (i^4)^2 = 1\).

What about negative integer powers? By the usual exponent rules, \(i^{-1}\) must be the multiplicative inverse of \(i\), meaning \(i \cdot i^{-1} = 1\). Test \(-i\):
\[
i \cdot (-i) = -i^2 = -(-1) = 1.
\]
Thus \(i^{-1} = -i\). Continuing downward by multiplying by \(i^{-1} = -i\) (or equivalently noticing that \(i^{-4} = (i^4)^{-1} = 1^{-1} = 1\)) shows that the exact same cycle of length \(4\) extends through all negative integers as well: \(i^{-1} = -i\), \(i^{-2} = -1\), \(i^{-3} = i\), and \(i^{-4} = 1\).

[CALLOUT]
id: powers-of-i
label: Rule
title: Integer powers of \(i\) modulo \(4\)
tone: key

For every integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\), divide \(n\) by \(4\) to write \(n = 4q + r\) with \(q \in \mathbb{Z}\) and remainder \(r \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}\). Because \(i^4 = 1\),
\[
i^n = (i^4)^q \, i^r = 1^q \, i^r = i^r.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

[TABLE]
Remainder \(r = n \bmod 4\) | Value of \(i^n\) | Sample exponents \(n\)
\(0\) | \(1\) | \(-8, -4, 0, 4, 8\)
\(1\) | \(i\) | \(-7, -3, 1, 5, 9\)
\(2\) | \(-1\) | \(-6, -2, 2, 6, 10\)
\(3\) | \(-i\) | \(-5, -1, 3, 7, 11\)
[/TABLE]

Let us apply [REF: powers-of-i | the modulo \(4\) rule] to both positive and negative exponents. To evaluate \(i^{75}\), divide \(75\) by \(4\): \(75 = 4 \cdot 18 + 3\), so \(i^{75} = i^3 = -i\). For a negative exponent such as \(i^{-19}\), either write \(-19 = 4(-5) + 1\) (careful: the remainder \(r\) must lie in \(\{0, 1, 2, 3\}\), not \(-3\)), or simply multiply by a convenient multiple of \(4\) in the exponent: since \(i^{20} = (i^4)^5 = 1\),
\[
i^{-19} = i^{-19} \cdot i^{20} = i^1 = i.
\]
Adding any multiple of \(4\) to the exponent never changes the value of \(i^n\), which is often the fastest way to evaluate negative powers without making sign errors in Euclidean division.

Notice also a useful consequence of the cycle: any four consecutive integer powers of \(i\) sum to \(0\), because for any integer \(m\),
\[
i^m + i^{m+1} + i^{m+2} + i^{m+3} = i^m(1 + i - 1 - i) = 0.
\]

=== SECTION 3: Addition, subtraction, and multiplication in standard form ===

We define addition, subtraction, and multiplication on \(\mathbb{C}\) so that complex numbers obey the same commutative, associative, and distributive laws as real polynomials in a variable \(i\), followed by the single reduction \(i^2 = -1\).

[CALLOUT]
id: complex-arithmetic-ops
label: Definition
title: Addition, subtraction, and multiplication in \(\mathbb{C}\)
tone: key

Let \(z = a + bi\) and \(w = c + di\) with \(a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}\).

(1) Addition: \((a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i\).
(2) Subtraction: \((a + bi) - (c + di) = (a - c) + (b - d)i\).
(3) Multiplication: \((a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i\).
[/CALLOUT]

There is no need to memorize the multiplication formula in [REF: complex-arithmetic-ops.3 | part (3)] as a bare template. Expanding with the distributive law produces it automatically:
\[
(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 = ac + (ad + bc)i - bd = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i.
\]
For example, to compute \((4 - 3i)(-2 + 5i)\), distribute and replace \(i^2\) with \(-1\):
\[
(4 - 3i)(-2 + 5i) = -8 + 20i + 6i - 15i^2 = -8 + 26i + 15 = 7 + 26i.
\]
Notice that the product of two non-real numbers can turn out to be completely real. For instance,
\[
(2 + 3i)(2 - 3i) = 4 - 6i + 6i - 9i^2 = 4 + 9 = 13.
\]
The cross terms cancel and \(i^2 = -1\) flips the sign of the last term from minus to plus.

Because these operations are built out of real arithmetic and \(i^2 = -1\), they inherit the core algebraic laws of \(\mathbb{R}\). Let us verify commutativity of multiplication and distributivity explicitly to see why nothing breaks:
- Commutativity of multiplication: \((c + di)(a + bi) = (ca - db) + (cb + da)i = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i = (a + bi)(c + di)\), using commutativity of real addition and multiplication.
- Distributivity: for \(z = a + bi\), \(w = c + di\), and \(u = e + fi\),
\[
z(w + u) = (a + bi)\bigl((c + e) + (d + f)i\bigr) = \bigl(a(c + e) - b(d + f)\bigr) + \bigl(a(d + f) + b(c + e)\bigr)i,
\]
which rearranges by real distributivity into \(\bigl((ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i\bigr) + \bigl((ae - bf) + (af + be)i\bigr) = zw + zu\).
Associativity of addition and multiplication, commutativity of addition, additive identity \(0 = 0 + 0i\), multiplicative identity \(1 = 1 + 0i\), and additive inverses \(-(a + bi) = -a - bi\) follow in the same direct way. Because all of these laws hold, familiar algebraic expansions such as \((z + w)^2 = z^2 + 2zw + w^2\) and \((z - w)(z + w) = z^2 - w^2\) remain valid for all \(z, w \in \mathbb{C}\).

We can now combine complex arithmetic with [REF: complex-number-def.3 | equality of complex numbers] to solve linear equations in real unknowns. Consider finding real numbers \(x\) and \(y\) such that
\[
(1 + 2i)x + (3 - i)y = 11 + 8i.
\]
First collect real and imaginary parts on the left-hand side:
\[
(x + 3y) + (2x - y)i = 11 + 8i.
\]
Equating real and imaginary parts yields the \(2 \times 2\) real system \(x + 3y = 11\) and \(2x - y = 8\). From the second equation, \(y = 2x - 8\); substituting into the first gives \(x + 3(2x - 8) = 11\), so \(7x = 35\) and \(x = 5\), whence \(y = 2(5) - 8 = 2\). Checking back, \((1 + 2i)(5) + (3 - i)(2) = (5 + 10i) + (6 - 2i) = 11 + 8i\).

=== SECTION 4: Square roots of negative numbers without sign fallacies ===

With \(i^2 = -1\) in hand, every negative real number \(-a\) (where \(a > 0\)) has two complex square roots, namely \(i\sqrt{a}\) and \(-i\sqrt{a}\), since \((i\sqrt{a})^2 = i^2 a = -a\) and \((-i\sqrt{a})^2 = i^2 a = -a\). By convention, the principal square root symbol \(\sqrt{-a}\) for \(a > 0\) denotes the one with positive imaginary part:
\[
\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a} \qquad (a > 0).
\]
For example, \(\sqrt{-9} = 3i\), \(\sqrt{-2} = i\sqrt{2}\), and \(\sqrt{-18} = i\sqrt{18} = 3i\sqrt{2}\).

In real algebra, one relies heavily on the product rule \(\sqrt{u}\sqrt{v} = \sqrt{uv}\) for nonnegative real numbers \(u, v \ge 0\). Extending that rule blindly to negative radicands produces a famous contradiction:
\[
\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} \stackrel{?}{=} \sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6.
\]
Yet using the definition \(\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a}\) first gives
\[
\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6.
\]
The second calculation is the correct one. The rule \(\sqrt{u}\sqrt{v} = \sqrt{uv}\) fails whenever both \(u\) and \(v\) are negative, because multiplying \(-4\) and \(-9\) inside a single radical erases the two factors of \(i\) whose product should be \(i^2 = -1\) rather than \(+1\).

[CALLOUT]
id: negative-radical-rule
label: Rule
title: Safe manipulation of negative radicals
tone: caution

Whenever a radical contains a negative real number \(-a\) (with \(a > 0\)), convert \(\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a}\) before multiplying, dividing, or combining radicals. Do not apply \(\sqrt{u}\sqrt{v} = \sqrt{uv}\) when both \(u < 0\) and \(v < 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: negative-radical-rule | the negative-radical rule] to simplify \((2 + \sqrt{-12})(1 - \sqrt{-3})\). Converting each negative radical first gives \(\sqrt{-12} = 2i\sqrt{3}\) and \(\sqrt{-3} = i\sqrt{3}\). Now expand in standard form:
\[
(2 + 2i\sqrt{3})(1 - i\sqrt{3}) = 2 - 2i\sqrt{3} + 2i\sqrt{3} - 2i^2(\sqrt{3})^2 = 2 - 2(-1)(3) = 8.
\]
As a second check, notice that \(\sqrt{-5}\cdot\sqrt{20} = (i\sqrt{5})(2\sqrt{5}) = 10i\), which does agree with \(\sqrt{(-5)(20)} = \sqrt{-100} = 10i\) because only one radicand was negative. Still, converting every \(\sqrt{-a}\) to \(i\sqrt{a}\) first is completely uniform and prevents errors in every case.

=== SECTION 5: Why the complex numbers cannot be ordered ===

Complex numbers keep all the familiar rules of real addition and multiplication, so it is natural to ask whether they also keep an order relation \(<\) like the one on the real number line. Is \(i > 0\), or is \(i < 0\)? Could we say \(2 + i < 3 + 2i\)?

The answer is no: there is no way to define an order relation \(<\) on \(\mathbb{C}\) that is compatible with complex addition and multiplication. Let us see why directly from the three basic properties of a compatible order:
- Trichotomy: for any \(u, v \in \mathbb{C}\), exactly one of \(u < v\), \(u = v\), or \(u > v\) holds.
- Compatibility with addition: if \(u < v\), then \(u + w < v + w\) for every \(w \in \mathbb{C}\).
- Compatibility with multiplication by positives: if \(u > 0\) and \(v > 0\), then \(uv > 0\).

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that such an order existed on \(\mathbb{C}\). A key consequence of these three properties is that every nonzero square must be positive: if \(u > 0\), then \(u \cdot u = u^2 > 0\); if \(u < 0\), then adding \(-u\) to both sides gives \(0 < -u\), so \((-u)(-u) = u^2 > 0\).

Apply this fact to \(u = 1\) (since \(1 \ne 0\)): we must have \(1 = 1^2 > 0\). Now apply the same fact to \(u = i\) (since \(i \ne 0\)): we must have \(i^2 > 0\), which means \(-1 > 0\). Adding \(1\) to both sides of \(-1 > 0\) yields \(0 > 1\), i.e., \(1 < 0\). We have derived both \(1 > 0\) and \(1 < 0\), directly violating trichotomy.

[CALLOUT]
id: no-order-in-c
label: Principle
title: No compatible order on \(\mathbb{C}\)
tone: caution

The complex numbers \(\mathbb{C}\) cannot be given an order relation \(<\) compatible with addition and multiplication. Expressions such as \(i > 0\), \(i < 0\), or \(1 + i < 2 + 3i\) are mathematically meaningless. Inequalities may be written only between real quantities extracted from complex numbers, such as \(\operatorname{Re}(z) < \operatorname{Re}(w)\) or \(\operatorname{Im}(z) \ge 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice that the proof did not depend on any particular choice of formula for \(<\): no clever trick can repair it, because \(i^2 = -1\) forces a square to be negative. Whenever you need to compare sizes of complex numbers in later lessons, you will compare real numbers derived from them—such as their real parts, imaginary parts, or distances from \(0\)—never the complex numbers themselves.
