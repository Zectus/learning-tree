=== SECTION 1: Function composition, associativity, and non-commutativity ===

Functions rarely act in isolation: we routinely feed the output of one function directly into the input of a second function, building pipelines from simpler maps. Set-theoretically, this chaining operation is function composition.

[CALLOUT]
id: composition-def
label: Definition
title: Composition of functions
tone: key

Let \(f : A \to B\) and \(g : B \to C\) be functions such that the codomain of \(f\) equals the domain of \(g\). The composition of \(g\) with \(f\) is the function \(g \circ f : A \to C\) defined by
\[
(g \circ f)(a) = g\bigl(f(a)\bigr) \qquad \text{for all } a \in A.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Read the order carefully: in \(g \circ f\), the RIGHT-HAND function \(f\) acts FIRST, taking \(a \in A\) to \(f(a) \in B\), and the LEFT-HAND function \(g\) acts SECOND, taking \(f(a) \in B\) to \(g(f(a)) \in C\). (This matches the relation composition convention \(S \circ R\) from our earlier study of binary relations.)

Notice the domain-codomain compatibility requirement: for \(g \circ f\) to be defined as a function \(A \to C\), every output of \(f\) must be a valid input to \(g\), which is guaranteed by matching \(\operatorname{codomain}(f) = \operatorname{domain}(g) = B\). (More generally, if \(f : A \to B_1\) and \(g : B_2 \to C\) with \(\operatorname{im}(f) \subseteq B_2\), the formula \(g(f(a))\) still makes sense, though in strict typed notation we match \(B_1 = B_2\) first—for instance by composing with the inclusion map \(B_1 \hookrightarrow B_2\).)

Two basic algebraic laws govern composition—and one familiar arithmetic law FAILS!

[CALLOUT]
id: composition-laws
label: Theorem
title: Associativity and identity laws for composition
tone: key

(1) Associativity: for any functions \(f : A \to B\), \(g : B \to C\), and \(h : C \to D\),
\[
h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f : A \to D.
\]
(2) Identity laws: for any function \(f : A \to B\),
\[
f \circ \operatorname{id}_A = f \qquad \text{and} \qquad \operatorname{id}_B \circ f = f.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Proof of (1): Both \(h \circ (g \circ f)\) and \((h \circ g) \circ f\) have the SAME domain \(A\) and the SAME codomain \(D\). Now let \(a \in A\) be arbitrary:
\[
\bigl(h \circ (g \circ f)\bigr)(a) = h\bigl((g \circ f)(a)\bigr) = h\bigl(g(f(a))\bigr) = (h \circ g)\bigl(f(a)\bigr) = \bigl((h \circ g) \circ f\bigr)(a).
\]
By equality of functions, \(h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f\). Because parentheses do not affect the result, we may write \(h \circ g \circ f\) unambiguously.

Proof of (2): Both \(f \circ \operatorname{id}_A\) and \(f\) map \(A \to B\), and for every \(a \in A\), \((f \circ \operatorname{id}_A)(a) = f(\operatorname{id}_A(a)) = f(a)\). Similarly, \(\operatorname{id}_B \circ f : A \to B\) satisfies \((\operatorname{id}_B \circ f)(a) = \operatorname{id}_B(f(a)) = f(a)\).

By contrast, function composition is NOT commutative in general! Even when both \(g \circ f\) and \(f \circ g\) are defined (for example, on \(A = B = C = \mathbb{R}\)), they typically differ. Take \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g(x) = x + 1\) on \(\mathbb{R}\):
\[
(g \circ f)(x) = g(x^2) = x^2 + 1, \qquad\text{whereas}\qquad (f \circ g)(x) = f(x + 1) = (x + 1)^2 = x^2 + 2x + 1.
\]
At \(x = 1\), \((g \circ f)(1) = 2\) while \((f \circ g)(1) = 4\), so \(g \circ f \ne f \circ g\).

=== SECTION 2: How injectivity and surjectivity interact with composition ===

Suppose we chain two functions \(f : A \to B\) and \(g : B \to C\) into \(g \circ f : A \to C\). Two natural questions arise:
- Forward direction: if \(f\) and \(g\) are injective (or surjective, or bijective), does \(g \circ f\) inherit that property?
- Backward direction: if the composite \(g \circ f\) is injective (or surjective), what MUST be true about \(f\) and \(g\) individually?

The backward direction carries a notorious asymmetry: injectivity of \(g \circ f\) constrains the INNER map \(f\), whereas surjectivity of \(g \circ f\) constrains the OUTER map \(g\)!

[CALLOUT]
id: comp-inj-surj
label: Theorem
title: Injectivity, surjectivity, and bijectivity under composition
tone: key

Let \(f : A \to B\) and \(g : B \to C\) be functions.

(1) If \(f\) and \(g\) are both injective, then \(g \circ f\) is injective.
(2) If \(f\) and \(g\) are both surjective, then \(g \circ f\) is surjective.
(3) If \(f\) and \(g\) are both bijective, then \(g \circ f\) is bijective.
(4) If \(g \circ f\) is injective, then \(f\) MUST be injective (but \(g\) need not be injective).
(5) If \(g \circ f\) is surjective, then \(g\) MUST be surjective (but \(f\) need not be surjective).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us prove every positive claim and construct sharp counterexamples for the two negative remarks!

Proof of (1): Assume \(f\) and \(g\) are injective. Let \(a_1, a_2 \in A\) satisfy \((g \circ f)(a_1) = (g \circ f)(a_2)\), i.e., \(g(f(a_1)) = g(f(a_2))\). Since \(g\) is injective, \(f(a_1) = f(a_2)\). Since \(f\) is injective, \(a_1 = a_2\). Hence \(g \circ f\) is injective.

Proof of (2): Assume \(f\) and \(g\) are surjective. Let \(c \in C\) be arbitrary. Since \(g : B \to C\) is surjective, there exists \(b \in B\) such that \(g(b) = c\). Since \(f : A \to B\) is surjective, there exists \(a \in A\) such that \(f(a) = b\). Then \((g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a)) = g(b) = c\). Hence \(g \circ f\) is surjective.

Part (3) follows immediately from combining (1) and (2).

Proof of (4): Assume \(g \circ f\) is injective. Let \(a_1, a_2 \in A\) satisfy \(f(a_1) = f(a_2)\). Applying \(g\) to both sides gives \(g(f(a_1)) = g(f(a_2))\), i.e., \((g \circ f)(a_1) = (g \circ f)(a_2)\). Because \(g \circ f\) is injective, \(a_1 = a_2\). Thus \(f\) is injective! (Intuitively, if \(f\) already collided two inputs \(a_1 \ne a_2\), then \(g\) could never un-collide them afterwards.)

Counterexample showing \(g\) need NOT be injective in (4) AND \(f\) need NOT be surjective in (5) at the exact same time: take \(A = \{1\}\), \(B = \{1, 2\}\), \(C = \{1\}\). Define \(f : A \to B\) by \(f(1) = 1\) (injective, NOT surjective), and define \(g : B \to C\) by \(g(1) = 1\) and \(g(2) = 1\) (surjective, NOT injective). Then \(g \circ f : \{1\} \to \{1\}\) sends \(1 \mapsto 1\), which is BIJECTIVE (both injective and surjective)! Yet \(g\) is NOT injective (its collision happens at \(2 \notin \operatorname{im}(f)\), which \(f\) never visits) and \(f\) is NOT surjective (missing \(2\) doesn't stop \(g\) from hitting \(1\)).

Proof of (5): Assume \(g \circ f\) is surjective. Let \(c \in C\) be arbitrary. Because \(g \circ f : A \to C\) is surjective, there exists \(a \in A\) such that \((g \circ f)(a) = c\), i.e., \(g(f(a)) = c\). Setting \(b = f(a) \in B\) gives an element of \(B\) with \(g(b) = c\). Hence \(g\) is surjective!

[TABLE]
Given property of \(g \circ f : A \to C\) | Forced on inner map \(f : A \to B\) | Forced on outer map \(g : B \to C\)
\(g \circ f\) is injective | \(f\) MUST be injective | Nothing forced on \(g\) (need not be injective or surjective)
\(g \circ f\) is surjective | Nothing forced on \(f\) (need not be injective or surjective) | \(g\) MUST be surjective
\(g \circ f\) is bijective | \(f\) MUST be injective | \(g\) MUST be surjective
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 3: Two-sided inverses and the bijection characterization ===

Can we "undo" a function \(f : A \to B\) by composing it with a partner function \(g : B \to A\) that restores every input in \(A\) AND every output in \(B\)?

[CALLOUT]
id: two-sided-inverse-def
label: Definition
title: Two-sided inverse (invertible function)
tone: key

Let \(f : A \to B\) be a function. A function \(g : B \to A\) is a two-sided inverse (or simply an inverse) of \(f\) if BOTH equations hold:
\[
g \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A \qquad \text{and} \qquad f \circ g = \operatorname{id}_B.
\]
Explicitly, \(g(f(a)) = a\) for all \(a \in A\), AND \(f(g(b)) = b\) for all \(b \in B\). If such a \(g\) exists, we say \(f\) is invertible.
[/CALLOUT]

Before asking WHEN an inverse exists, let us prove that IF a two-sided inverse exists, it is UNIQUE—and notice how effortlessly [REF: composition-laws | associativity and identity laws] do the work! Suppose \(g_1 : B \to A\) and \(g_2 : B \to A\) are both two-sided inverses of \(f\). Then
\[
g_1 = g_1 \circ \operatorname{id}_B = g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = \operatorname{id}_A \circ g_2 = g_2.
\]
Because the two-sided inverse is unique whenever it exists, we may denote it unambiguously by \(f^{-1} : B \to A\). (Be careful not to confuse this inverse function \(f^{-1} : B \to A\) with the set-valued preimage operation \(f^{-1}(T)\) from earlier lessons; when \(f\) IS invertible, the two agree cleanly via \(f^{-1}(\{b\}) = \{f^{-1}(b)\}\).)

Now we prove the central theorem linking invertibility and bijections.

[CALLOUT]
id: inverse-bijection-thm
label: Theorem
title: Characterization and algebra of two-sided inverses
tone: key

Let \(f : A \to B\) and \(g : B \to C\) be functions.

(1) \(f\) has a two-sided inverse if and only if \(f\) is bijective.
(2) If \(f\) is bijective, then \(f^{-1} : B \to A\) is also bijective, and \((f^{-1})^{-1} = f\).
(3) Socks-and-shoes rule: if \(f : A \to B\) and \(g : B \to C\) are both bijective, then \(g \circ f : A \to C\) is bijective and
\[
(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Proof of (1):
- (\(\implies\)): Suppose \(f\) has a two-sided inverse \(h : B \to A\). Since \(h \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A\) is bijective (in particular injective), [REF: comp-inj-surj.4 | our composition theorem] immediately forces \(f\) to be injective! Since \(f \circ h = \operatorname{id}_B\) is bijective (in particular surjective), [REF: comp-inj-surj.5 | our composition theorem] immediately forces \(f\) to be surjective! Hence \(f\) is bijective.
- (\(\Longleftarrow\)): Suppose \(f : A \to B\) is bijective. By the definition of bijection, for every \(b \in B\) there exists a UNIQUE \(a \in A\) such that \(f(a) = b\). Define \(h : B \to A\) by letting \(h(b)\) be this unique element \(a\). By construction, \(h\) satisfies existence and uniqueness, so \(h : B \to A\) is a well-defined function. Check both compositions:
  - For any \(b \in B\), \(f(h(b)) = b\) directly by definition of \(h(b)\), so \(f \circ h = \operatorname{id}_B\).
  - For any \(a \in A\), set \(b = f(a)\). Since \(a\) satisfies \(f(a) = b\) and such an element is unique, \(h(f(a)) = h(b) = a\), so \(h \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A\).
Thus \(h\) is a two-sided inverse of \(f\).

Proof of (2): Since \(f^{-1} \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A\) and \(f \circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_B\), the roles of \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) are completely symmetric: \(f\) is the unique two-sided inverse of \(f^{-1}\). By (1), \(f^{-1}\) is bijective and \((f^{-1})^{-1} = f\).

Proof of (3): We know \(g \circ f\) is bijective by [REF: comp-inj-surj.3 | composition of bijections]. To prove \((g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}\), by uniqueness of two-sided inverses it suffices to verify BOTH compositions using [REF: composition-laws.1 | associativity]:
\[
(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ \operatorname{id}_B \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A,
\]
\[
(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ \operatorname{id}_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_C.
\]
Why does the order reverse? To undo "put on socks (\(f\)), then put on shoes (\(g\))," you must first take off shoes (\(g^{-1}\)), then take off socks (\(f^{-1}\))!

Geometrically, for a real bijection \(f : A \to B\) with \(A, B \subseteq \mathbb{R}\), the graph of \(f^{-1}\) is obtained by swapping coordinates \((a, b) \leftrightarrow (b, a)\)—reflecting the graph of \(f\) across the diagonal line \(y = x\). === SECTION 4: One-sided inverses — left inverses and right inverses ===

What if a function \(f : A \to B\) is injective but NOT surjective, or surjective but NOT injective? Then it cannot have a two-sided inverse, yet we can still undo it from ONE side!

[CALLOUT]
id: one-sided-inverse-def
label: Definition
title: Left inverse (retraction) and right inverse (section)
tone: key

Let \(f : A \to B\) be a function.

(1) A function \(r : B \to A\) is a left inverse (or retraction) of \(f\) if \(r \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A\).
(2) A function \(s : B \to A\) is a right inverse (or section) of \(f\) if \(f \circ s = \operatorname{id}_B\).
[/CALLOUT]

Look how cleanly one-sided inverses characterize injectivity and surjectivity:

[CALLOUT]
id: one-sided-inverse-thm
label: Theorem
title: Existence of left and right inverses
tone: key

Let \(f : A \to B\) be a function.

(1) Left inverses and injectivity (for nonempty \(A\)): if \(A \ne \emptyset\), then \(f\) has a left inverse \(r : B \to A\) if and only if \(f\) is injective.
(2) Right inverses and surjectivity: \(f\) has a right inverse \(s : B \to A\) if and only if \(f\) is surjective.
(3) Compatibility: if \(f\) has BOTH a left inverse \(r : B \to A\) and a right inverse \(s : B \to A\), then \(r = s\), and this common function is the unique two-sided inverse \(f^{-1}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us examine all three parts carefully, including the edge case \(A = \emptyset\) and the role of choice!

Proof of (1):
- (\(\implies\)): If \(r \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A\), then \(r \circ f\) is injective, so by [REF: comp-inj-surj.4 | our composition theorem], \(f\) is injective. (This direction does not even need \(A \ne \emptyset\).)
- (\(\Longleftarrow\)): Assume \(A \ne \emptyset\) and \(f : A \to B\) is injective. Fix one default element \(a_0 \in A\) (this is where \(A \ne \emptyset\) is used!). Define \(r : B \to A\) by:
  - if \(b \in \operatorname{im}(f)\), set \(r(b)\) to be the UNIQUE \(a \in A\) with \(f(a) = b\) (unique because \(f\) is injective!);
  - if \(b \notin \operatorname{im}(f)\), set \(r(b) = a_0\).
  Then for every \(a \in A\), \(f(a) \in \operatorname{im}(f)\) and \(a\) is the unique input mapping to \(f(a)\), so \(r(f(a)) = a\). Thus \(r \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A\).

Why did we require \(A \ne \emptyset\) in (1)? If \(A = \emptyset\) and \(B \ne \emptyset\), the empty function \(f : \emptyset \to B\) is vacuously injective, yet NO function \(r : B \to \emptyset\) exists at all!

Notice also that when \(f\) is injective but NOT surjective (and \(A\) has at least two elements), the left inverse \(r\) is NOT unique: you are free to assign ANY element of \(A\) to each missed element \(b \in B \setminus \operatorname{im}(f)\). For example, take the doubling map \(f : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}\) defined by \(f(n) = 2n\). It is injective (not surjective). Both
\[
r_1(m) = \begin{cases} m/2 & \text{if } m \text{ is even}, \\ 0 & \text{if } m \text{ is odd} \end{cases}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
r_2(m) = \lfloor m/2 \rfloor
\]
satisfy \(r_1(2n) = n\) and \(r_2(2n) = n\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\), so BOTH \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are valid left inverses of \(f\), even though \(r_1(1) = 0\) while \(r_2(3) = 1 \ne 0 = r_1(3)\)!

Proof of (2):
- (\(\implies\)): If \(f \circ s = \operatorname{id}_B\), then \(f \circ s\) is surjective, so by [REF: comp-inj-surj.5 | our composition theorem], \(f\) is surjective.
- (\(\Longleftarrow\)): Assume \(f : A \to B\) is surjective. Then for EVERY \(b \in B\), the preimage fiber \(f^{-1}(\{b\})\) is nonempty. To build a right inverse \(s : B \to A\), we must select ONE element \(s(b) \in f^{-1}(\{b\})\) for each \(b \in B\). Once chosen, \(f(s(b)) = b\) holds automatically for all \(b \in B\), giving \(f \circ s = \operatorname{id}_B\).

Pause on that selection step! For concrete domains like \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{N}\), we can usually write down an explicit rule that picks one preimage per fiber without ambiguity:
- For the surjective map \(f : \mathbb{R} \to [0, \infty)\) given by \(f(x) = x^2\), every fiber \(f^{-1}(\{y\}) = \{-\sqrt{y}, \sqrt{y}\}\) is nonempty, and we have explicit right inverses \(s_1(y) = \sqrt{y}\), \(s_2(y) = -\sqrt{y}\), and even piecewise choices such as \(s_3(y) = \sqrt{y}\) for \(y \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, \infty)\) and \(s_3(y) = -\sqrt{y}\) for irrational \(y > 0\)!
- For an ARBITRARY surjection \(f : A \to B\) between completely unstructured infinite sets, however, there may be no explicit formula to pick one element simultaneously from infinitely many nonempty fibers \(f^{-1}(\{b\})\). Guaranteeing that such a selection function \(s\) exists in full generality relies on a set-theoretic choice principle (the Axiom of Choice).

Finally, proof of (3): If \(r \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A\) and \(f \circ s = \operatorname{id}_B\), then by [REF: composition-laws.1 | associativity],
\[
r = r \circ \operatorname{id}_B = r \circ (f \circ s) = (r \circ f) \circ s = \operatorname{id}_A \circ s = s.
\]
So the moment BOTH a left inverse and a right inverse exist, they collapse into the exact same unique two-sided inverse!
