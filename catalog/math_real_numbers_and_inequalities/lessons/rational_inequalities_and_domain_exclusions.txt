=== SECTION 1: Moving to one side and why cross-multiplying fails ===

Suppose we want to solve the rational inequality
\[
\frac{x + 3}{x - 1} \ge 2.
\]
If this were an equation \(\frac{x + 3}{x - 1} = 2\), multiplying both sides by \(x - 1\) (while noting \(x \ne 1\)) would give \(x + 3 = 2(x - 1)\), leading immediately to \(x = 5\). For an inequality, however, multiplying by \(x - 1\) is ILLEGAL without splitting into sign cases: when \(x > 1\), \(x - 1 > 0\) preserves \(\ge\), whereas when \(x < 1\), \(x - 1 < 0\) REVERSES \(\ge\) to \(\le\). Worse, a naive cross-multiplication produces \(x + 3 \ge 2x - 2 \implies x \le 5\), which falsely claims that \(x = 0\) is a solution even though \(\frac{0 + 3}{0 - 1} = -3 \not\ge 2\)!

Instead of cross-multiplying, we reuse the exact same zero-comparison strategy from polynomial inequalities: move every term to one side and combine over a single common denominator to obtain
\[
\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \lesseqqgtr 0.
\]
Carry this out on \(\frac{x + 3}{x - 1} \ge 2\):
\[
\frac{x + 3}{x - 1} - 2 \ge 0 \implies \frac{(x + 3) - 2(x - 1)}{x - 1} \ge 0 \implies \frac{5 - x}{x - 1} \ge 0.
\]
Why is comparing a single quotient against \(0\) so powerful? By the quotient sign rule from real-number order properties, for every \(x\) where \(Q(x) \ne 0\), the quotient \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) has the EXACT SAME sign as the product \(P(x)\,Q(x)\)! Thus a rational function \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) can change sign ONLY at:
- Numerator zeros (real roots of \(P(x) = 0\)), where the quotient equals \(0\) (provided \(Q(x) \ne 0\)), and
- Denominator zeros (real roots of \(Q(x) = 0\)), where the quotient is undefined and typically has a vertical asymptote.

Both types of zeros belong on the sign chart as critical points—yet they obey an absolute, non-negotiable asymmetry when forming the final solution set.

[CALLOUT]
id: rational-endpoint-asymmetry
label: Rule
title: Numerator zeros versus denominator zeros
tone: key

When solving a reduced rational inequality \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \lesseqqgtr 0\) (where \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\) are polynomials):

(1) Denominator zeros (\(Q(r) = 0\)): ALWAYS EXCLUDED from the solution set (open parenthesis), even for \(\le\) and \(\ge\), because division by \(0\) is undefined.
(2) Numerator zeros (\(P(r) = 0\) with \(Q(r) \ne 0\)): INCLUDED (square bracket or isolated singleton) for \(\le\) and \(\ge\), and EXCLUDED for \(<\) and \(>\).
[/CALLOUT]

Finish \(\frac{5 - x}{x - 1} \ge 0\) using [REF: rational-endpoint-asymmetry | this rule]. The critical points are \(x = 1\) (denominator zero, excluded) and \(x = 5\) (numerator zero, included). Rewrite \(\frac{5 - x}{x - 1} = -\frac{x - 5}{x - 1}\):
- For \(x > 5\): numerator \(5 - x < 0\), denominator \(x - 1 > 0\), quotient \(-\).
- For \(1 < x < 5\): numerator \(5 - x > 0\), denominator \(x - 1 > 0\), quotient \(+\).
- For \(x < 1\): numerator \(5 - x > 0\), denominator \(x - 1 < 0\), quotient \(-\).

Hence the solution set is \((1, 5]\)—open at \(1\), closed at \(5\). === SECTION 2: Multiplicities in rational sign charts ===

Because \(\frac{1}{(x - r)^k}\) has the exact same sign as \((x - r)^k\) for every \(x \ne r\), the odd/even multiplicity sign-change rules from polynomial inequalities carry over word for word to BOTH numerator and denominator factors:
- Across a critical point \(r\) whose factor \((x - r)^k\) (in the numerator or in the denominator) has ODD multiplicity \(k\), the quotient \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) CHANGES SIGN.
- Across a critical point \(r\) whose factor \((x - r)^k\) has EVEN multiplicity \(k\), the quotient KEEPS THE SAME SIGN on both sides of \(r\).
- An irreducible quadratic factor (\(\Delta < 0\)) in either the numerator or the denominator never vanishes and simply contributes the constant sign of its leading coefficient.

Let us build a complete sign chart for
\[
\frac{(x + 1)(x - 4)^2}{(x - 2)^3 (x + 3)^2} \le 0.
\]
List all four critical points in order, tagging each by role (N = numerator, D = denominator) and multiplicity:
- \(x = -3\): D, even (multiplicity \(2\)) — always excluded.
- \(x = -1\): N, odd (multiplicity \(1\)) — included because of \(\le\).
- \(x = 2\): D, odd (multiplicity \(3\)) — always excluded.
- \(x = 4\): N, even (multiplicity \(2\)) — included because of \(\le\).

Every factor has leading coefficient \(+1\), so for \(x > 4\) the quotient is \(+\). Propagate signs from right to left across each critical point:

[TABLE]
Region or point | \((-\infty, -3)\) | \(-3\) | \((-3, -1)\) | \(-1\) | \((-1, 2)\) | \(2\) | \((2, 4)\) | \(4\) | \((4, \infty)\)
Role / multiplicity | — | D (even) | — | N (odd) | — | D (odd) | — | N (even) | —
Sign / status | \(+\) | undef | \(+\) | \(0\) | \(-\) | undef | \(+\) | \(0\) | \(+\)
[/TABLE]

Read off the solution set for \(\le 0\) directly from the bottom row: the negative interval is \((-1, 2)\); the numerator zero \(-1\) closes its left endpoint to \([-1, 2)\); the denominator zero \(2\) stays open; and the numerator zero \(4\) is an isolated point where the quotient equals \(0 \le 0\), so \(\{4\}\) must be included! Thus the solution set is
\[
[-1, 2) \cup \{4\}.
\]
Notice what would have happened had we asked for \(\ge 0\) instead: the positive intervals are \((-\infty, -3)\), \((-3, -1)\), \((2, 4)\), and \((4, \infty)\). At the numerator zero \(4\), the two adjacent intervals \((2, 4)\) and \((4, \infty)\) MERGE across \(4\) into \((2, \infty)\). At the denominator zero \(-3\), however, the two adjacent intervals \((-\infty, -3)\) and \((-3, -1]\) CANNOT merge because \(-3\) is undefined—leaving \((-\infty, -3) \cup (-3, -1] \cup (2, \infty)\).

=== SECTION 3: Common factors and multi-fraction inequalities ===

Two structural situations require extra discipline: canceling common factors between numerator and denominator, and combining two or more rational terms.

First, consider a rational inequality where numerator and denominator share a common factor:
\[
\frac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 - 4x + 3} \le 0.
\]
Factor both polynomials completely:
\[
\frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{(x - 1)(x - 3)} \le 0.
\]
BEFORE canceling \((x - 1)\), record the full domain restriction of the ORIGINAL expression: the denominator vanishes at BOTH \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\), so \(x \ne 1\) and \(x \ne 3\). Only for \(x \ne 1\) is the original quotient equal to \(\frac{x + 1}{x - 3}\). Solving \(\frac{x + 1}{x - 3} \le 0\) yields \([-1, 3)\). Now intersect with the recorded domain exclusion \(x \ne 1\): because \(1\) lies inside \([-1, 3)\), we must remove it! The true solution set is
\[
[-1, 1) \cup (1, 3).
\]

[CALLOUT]
id: cancel-domain-trap
label: Caution
title: Never cancel a common factor without preserving its domain exclusion
tone: caution

If \((x - a)\) divides both \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\), the original rational expression is UNDEFINED at \(x = a\). Whenever you cancel \((x - a)\), write the restriction \(x \ne a\) explicitly beside the simplified inequality and exclude \(a\) from the final solution set.
[/CALLOUT]

What if the common factor has different multiplicities in numerator and denominator, such as \(\frac{(x - 1)^2(x + 2)}{(x - 1)(x - 3)} \le 0\)? Again, record \(x \ne 1\) and \(x \ne 3\) first! After canceling one copy of \((x - 1)\), the reduced inequality is \(\frac{(x - 1)(x + 2)}{x - 3} \le 0\) with \(x \ne 1\). The reduced inequality alone would include \(x = 1\) as a numerator zero, but the original domain restriction \(x \ne 1\) overrides it and forces an open parenthesis at \(1\).

Second, consider an inequality with rational expressions on both sides, such as
\[
\frac{2}{x - 1} \le \frac{1}{x + 2}.
\]
Resist every temptation to cross-multiply! Subtract the right side from the left and combine over the least common denominator \((x - 1)(x + 2)\):
\[
\frac{2}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{x + 2} \le 0 \implies \frac{2(x + 2) - (x - 1)}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} \le 0 \implies \frac{x + 5}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} \le 0.
\]
The critical points are \(-5\) (numerator, odd, included), \(-2\) (denominator, odd, excluded), and \(1\) (denominator, odd, excluded). All factors have positive leading coefficient, so the sign alternates from right to left as \(+\) on \((1, \infty)\), \(-\) on \((-2, 1)\), \(+\) on \((-5, -2)\), and \(-\) on \((-\infty, -5)\). Thus the solution set is
\[
(-\infty, -5] \cup (-2, 1).
\]
Notice how cleanly the sign chart captures BOTH pieces—including the interval \((-2, 1)\) where the two denominators \((x - 1)\) and \((x + 2)\) have opposite signs, which naive cross-multiplication completely scrambles.
