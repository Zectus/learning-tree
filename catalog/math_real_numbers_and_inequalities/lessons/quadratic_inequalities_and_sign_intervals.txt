=== SECTION 1: Standard form and the two-root case ===

Linear inequalities can be solved by isolating \(x\) on one side. As soon as \(x^2\) enters—as in \(x^2 - 5x + 6 < 0\)—isolating \(x\) directly fails. Writing \(x(x - 5) < -6\) does not tell you which numbers work, and taking square roots before checking signs invites errors. The right strategy is to move every term to one side so the inequality compares a single quadratic expression against \(0\):
\[
ax^2 + bx + c < 0 \qquad (\text{or } \le,\; >,\; \ge), \qquad a \ne 0.
\]
Why compare against \(0\)? Because the sign rules for products and squares from our order properties let us read the sign of \(ax^2 + bx + c\) cleanly from its roots and leading coefficient \(a\), and the discriminant \(\Delta = b^2 - 4ac\) divides every quadratic into exactly three regimes: \(\Delta > 0\), \(\Delta = 0\), and \(\Delta < 0\).

Begin with the most common case: \(\Delta > 0\). Then \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) has two distinct real roots \(r_1 < r_2\), and the quadratic factors as
\[
ax^2 + bx + c = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2).
\]
The two roots \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) partition the real line into three open intervals: \((-\infty, r_1)\), \((r_1, r_2)\), and \((r_2, \infty)\). On each interval, neither linear factor changes sign:
- For \(x < r_1\): both \(x - r_1 < 0\) and \(x - r_2 < 0\), so their product \((x - r_1)(x - r_2) > 0\).
- For \(r_1 < x < r_2\): \(x - r_1 > 0\) while \(x - r_2 < 0\), so their product \((x - r_1)(x - r_2) < 0\).
- For \(x > r_2\): both \(x - r_1 > 0\) and \(x - r_2 > 0\), so their product \((x - r_1)(x - r_2) > 0\).

Multiplying by \(a\) either keeps those signs (if \(a > 0\)) or flips all three signs (if \(a < 0\)). Thus, whenever \(\Delta > 0\), \(ax^2 + bx + c\) has the SAME sign as \(a\) outside the roots and the OPPOSITE sign from \(a\) strictly between the roots!

Let us work through a concrete example: solve \(2x^2 - 3x - 2 \ge 0\). Factoring gives
\[
(2x + 1)(x - 2) = 2\left(x + \frac{1}{2}\right)(x - 2) \ge 0.
\]
The roots are \(r_1 = -\frac{1}{2}\) and \(r_2 = 2\), and the leading coefficient is \(a = 2 > 0\). Organize the signs of the factors in a sign table:

[TABLE]
Interval or point | Sign of \(2x + 1\) | Sign of \(x - 2\) | Sign of \((2x + 1)(x - 2)\)
\(\left(-\infty, -\frac{1}{2}\right)\) | \(-\) | \(-\) | \(+\)
\(x = -\frac{1}{2}\) | \(0\) | \(-\) | \(0\)
\(\left(-\frac{1}{2}, 2\right)\) | \(+\) | \(-\) | \(-\)
\(x = 2\) | \(+\) | \(0\) | \(0\)
\((2, \infty)\) | \(+\) | \(+\) | \(+\)
[/TABLE]

Because we want \(\ge 0\), we collect the two positive intervals together with both zeros \(x = -\frac{1}{2}\) and \(x = 2\):
\[
\left(-\infty, -\frac{1}{2}\right] \cup [2, \infty).
\]
Geometrically, \(y = 2x^2 - 3x - 2\) is an upward-opening parabola (\(a = 2 > 0\)) crossing the horizontal axis at \(x = -\frac{1}{2}\) and \(x = 2\). It lies below the axis strictly between the roots and on or above the axis outside them.

What if \(a < 0\), as in \(-x^2 + 4x + 5 > 0\)? You have two equivalent choices: either note that the downward-opening parabola is positive BETWEEN its roots, or multiply both sides by \(-1\) first (reversing the inequality!) to work with a positive leading coefficient:
\[
x^2 - 4x - 5 < 0 \implies (x + 1)(x - 5) < 0 \implies (-1, 5).
\]
Multiplying by \(-1\) to make \(a > 0\) is a clean habit that prevents sign-direction slips.

=== SECTION 2: Repeated roots and sign-definite quadratics ===

When \(\Delta \le 0\), the quadratic does NOT cross the horizontal axis twice, and mechanical "inside/outside two roots" templates fail completely. Instead, completing the square explains both remaining regimes in one stroke.

For any \(ax^2 + bx + c\) with \(a \ne 0\), completing the square yields the exact identity
\[
ax^2 + bx + c = a\left[\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{\Delta}{4a^2}\right], \qquad \Delta = b^2 - 4ac.
\]

Regime 2: \(\Delta = 0\) (one repeated root \(r = -\frac{b}{2a}\)). The identity collapses to a single scaled square:
\[
ax^2 + bx + c = a(x - r)^2.
\]
Because \((x - r)^2 \ge 0\) for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), with equality ONLY at \(x = r\), the quadratic never changes sign—it merely touches \(0\) at \(x = r\) and bounces back! Consequently, all four inequality types produce distinct, often degenerate sets (shown below for \(a > 0\); for \(a < 0\), simply multiply by \(-1\) first to flip the inequality):

[TABLE]
Inequality (with \(a > 0\), \(\Delta = 0\)) | Equivalent condition | Solution set
\(a(x - r)^2 > 0\) | \(x \ne r\) | \(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{r\} = (-\infty, r) \cup (r, \infty)\)
\(a(x - r)^2 \ge 0\) | Always true | \(\mathbb{R} = (-\infty, \infty)\)
\(a(x - r)^2 < 0\) | Never true | \(\emptyset\)
\(a(x - r)^2 \le 0\) | \((x - r)^2 = 0\), i.e., \(x = r\) | \(\{r\}\)
[/TABLE]

Notice that fourth row in particular: a non-strict inequality \(a(x - r)^2 \le 0\) with \(a > 0\) has the SINGLETON solution set \(\{r\}\)! For example, \(-3x^2 + 12x - 12 \ge 0\) becomes \(3(x - 2)^2 \le 0\) after multiplying by \(-1\), which holds only at \(x = 2\), giving \(\{2\}\).

Regime 3: \(\Delta < 0\) (no real roots). Here \(-\Delta > 0\) and \(4a^2 > 0\), so the positive constant \(\frac{-\Delta}{4a^2} > 0\) is added to the non-negative square \(\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2\). Thus the bracketed quantity is STRICTLY POSITIVE for every real number \(x\):
\[
\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + \frac{-\Delta}{4a^2} \ge \frac{-\Delta}{4a^2} > 0.
\]
Multiplying by \(a\) shows that \(ax^2 + bx + c\) is strictly positive on all of \(\mathbb{R}\) when \(a > 0\), and strictly negative on all of \(\mathbb{R}\) when \(a < 0\). Such a quadratic is called sign-definite: its graph never meets the horizontal axis, so any inequality \(ax^2 + bx + c \lesseqqgtr 0\) has solution set either ALL of \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\emptyset\).

For example, test \(x^2 - 4x + 7 \le 0\). Computing \(\Delta = (-4)^2 - 4(1)(7) = -12 < 0\) and \(a = 1 > 0\) (or completing the square to \((x - 2)^2 + 3 \le 0\)) immediately tells us that \(x^2 - 4x + 7 \ge 3 > 0\) everywhere, so the solution set is \(\emptyset\). Meanwhile, \(-x^2 + 2x - 5 < 0\) has \(a = -1 < 0\) and \(\Delta = 4 - 20 = -16 < 0\), so it is strictly negative everywhere and its solution set is \(\mathbb{R}\).

Drag the slider \(c\) in the plot of \(y = x^2 - 2x + c\) below to watch all three discriminant regimes smoothly unfold: \(\Delta = 4 - 4c\) is positive for \(c < 1\) (two roots), zero at \(c = 1\) (tangent at \((1, 0)\)), and negative for \(c > 1\) (strictly above the horizontal axis).

[CALLOUT]
id: quad-three-regimes
label: Summary
title: Complete sign classification of \(ax^2 + bx + c\) (\(a \ne 0\))
tone: key

Let \(\Delta = b^2 - 4ac\).

(1) \(\Delta > 0\): two real roots \(r_1 < r_2\); same sign as \(a\) on \((-\infty, r_1) \cup (r_2, \infty)\), opposite sign on \((r_1, r_2)\).
(2) \(\Delta = 0\): one repeated root \(r = -\frac{b}{2a}\); same sign as \(a\) on \(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{r\}\), zero at \(r\), never opposite sign.
(3) \(\Delta < 0\): no real roots; strictly the same sign as \(a\) for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\).
[/CALLOUT]

What when \(\Delta > 0\) is not a perfect square? The exact same sign rule applies using the quadratic formula to locate \(r_1, r_2\). For instance, \(x^2 - 2x - 1 < 0\) has \(\Delta = 4 + 4 = 8 > 0\) and roots \(1 \pm \sqrt{2}\), so with \(a = 1 > 0\) its solution set is \((1 - \sqrt{2}, 1 + \sqrt{2})\).

=== SECTION 3: Sign-definiteness criteria and parametric quadratics ===

A classic application of [REF: quad-three-regimes | the three-regime classification] is finding all values of a real parameter \(k\) that force an expression \(a(k)x^2 + b(k)x + c(k)\) to keep one constant sign for EVERY \(x \in \mathbb{R}\).

Reading directly off [REF: quad-three-regimes | the classification], for a genuine quadratic (\(a \ne 0\)):

[CALLOUT]
id: sign-definiteness
label: Criterion
title: Always-positive and always-negative quadratics (\(a \ne 0\))
tone: key

For \(a \ne 0\):

(1) \(ax^2 + bx + c > 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) \(\iff\) \(a > 0\) and \(\Delta < 0\).
(2) \(ax^2 + bx + c \ge 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) \(\iff\) \(a > 0\) and \(\Delta \le 0\).
(3) \(ax^2 + bx + c < 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) \(\iff\) \(a < 0\) and \(\Delta < 0\).
(4) \(ax^2 + bx + c \le 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) \(\iff\) \(a < 0\) and \(\Delta \le 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

[CALLOUT]
id: leading-zero-check
label: Caution
title: Always check the degenerate case \(a(k) = 0\) separately
tone: caution

The criterion [REF: sign-definiteness | above] assumes \(a \ne 0\). Whenever the leading coefficient depends on a parameter \(k\), you MUST test \(a(k) = 0\) directly in the original inequality before applying \(\Delta < 0\) or \(\Delta \le 0\). Sometimes \(a(k) = 0\) produces a valid constant inequality (so that \(k\) belongs to the answer), and sometimes it produces a non-constant linear function or false constant (so that \(k\) must be excluded).
[/CALLOUT]

Compare two closely related examples to see why [REF: leading-zero-check | checking \(a(k) = 0\)] makes or breaks the final answer.

Example 1: Find all \(k \in \mathbb{R}\) such that \(kx^2 + 2x + 1 > 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\).
- Degenerate check (\(k = 0\)): the inequality becomes \(2x + 1 > 0\), which fails for \(x \le -\frac{1}{2}\). Thus \(k = 0\) does NOT work.
- Quadratic case (\(k \ne 0\)): by [REF: sign-definiteness.1 | the always-positive criterion], we require \(k > 0\) and
\[
\Delta = 2^2 - 4(k)(1) = 4 - 4k < 0 \implies k > 1.
\]
Intersecting \(k > 0\) with \(k > 1\) gives \(k \in (1, \infty)\).

Example 2: Find all \(k \in \mathbb{R}\) such that \(kx^2 - 2kx + 3 \ge 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\).
- Degenerate check (\(k = 0\)): the inequality becomes \(3 \ge 0\), which IS true for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\)! Thus \(k = 0\) MUST be included.
- Quadratic case (\(k \ne 0\)): by [REF: sign-definiteness.2 | the non-negative criterion], we require \(k > 0\) and
\[
\Delta = (-2k)^2 - 4(k)(3) = 4k^2 - 12k = 4k(k - 3) \le 0.
\]
Notice that solving \(\Delta \le 0\) is itself a quadratic inequality in \(k\): its roots are \(0\) and \(3\) with positive leading coefficient \(4\), so \(4k(k - 3) \le 0 \iff k \in [0, 3]\). Intersecting with \(k > 0\) gives \((0, 3]\).
- Combining with the degenerate case \(k = 0\) yields the full answer \(k \in [0, 3]\).

Notice also how "no real solutions" translates directly into sign-definiteness: saying "\(ax^2 + bx + c \le 0\) has solution set \(\emptyset\)" is logically identical to saying "\(ax^2 + bx + c > 0\) for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\)."
