=== SECTION 1: Critical points and the sign-chart method ===

In the previous lesson we solved quadratic inequalities by locating the real roots of \(ax^2 + bx + c\) and reading its sign on the intervals between them. Now suppose we face a higher-degree polynomial inequality such as
\[
x^3 - 3x^2 - 4x + 12 > 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad (x + 2)(x - 1)^2(x - 4) \le 0.
\]
We cannot read the answer from a single parabola opening direction anymore. What lets us generalize quadratic sign intervals to polynomials of ANY degree?

The essential bridge is a basic continuity property of polynomials, which we take as given here: a polynomial \(P(x)\) can change sign ONLY at its real zeros. Between any two consecutive real zeros—and beyond the smallest and largest real zeros—\(P(x)\) cannot cross \(0\), so it must stay either strictly positive throughout that open interval or strictly negative throughout it.

To solve any factorable polynomial inequality \(P(x) \lesseqqgtr 0\):
1. Move all terms to one side so the inequality compares a single polynomial \(P(x)\) against \(0\).
2. Factor \(P(x)\) completely over \(\mathbb{R}\) into a leading constant \(a\), powers of distinct real linear factors \((x - r_j)^{k_j}\), and any irreducible quadratic factors (those with \(\Delta < 0\), which have no real zeros).
3. List all distinct real zeros \(r_1 < r_2 < \dots < r_m\) in increasing order. These numbers are the critical points of \(P(x)\); they partition \(\mathbb{R}\) into \(m + 1\) open test intervals:
\[
(-\infty, r_1),\quad (r_1, r_2),\quad \dots,\quad (r_{m-1}, r_m),\quad (r_m, \infty).
\]
4. Determine the sign of \(P(x)\) on each open interval—either by building a factor-by-factor sign chart or by testing one sample point per interval—and then decide whether each critical point \(r_j\) itself is included (for \(\le\) or \(\ge\)) or excluded (for \(<\) or \(>\)).

Let us apply this workflow to
\[
(x + 3)(x - 1)(x - 4) \ge 0.
\]
The polynomial is already factored with leading coefficient \(1 > 0\) and three simple real zeros: \(-3\), \(1\), and \(4\). Build a factor-by-factor sign chart recording the sign of each factor on the four open intervals:

[TABLE]
Interval | Sign of \(x + 3\) | Sign of \(x - 1\) | Sign of \(x - 4\) | Sign of \(P(x)\)
\((-\infty, -3)\) | \(-\) | \(-\) | \(-\) | \(-\)
\((-3, 1)\) | \(+\) | \(-\) | \(-\) | \(+\)
\((1, 4)\) | \(+\) | \(+\) | \(-\) | \(-\)
\((4, \infty)\) | \(+\) | \(+\) | \(+\) | \(+\)
[/TABLE]

Because the inequality is non-strict (\(\ge 0\)), we keep the positive intervals together with all three real zeros \(-3\), \(1\), and \(4\):
\[
[-3, 1] \cup [4, \infty).
\]

=== SECTION 2: Multiplicity sign rules and irreducible quadratic factors ===

Building a full row-by-row sign chart always works, but watching how individual factors behave near their roots gives a much faster—and far less error-prone—shortcut called the multiplicity sign-change rule.

Suppose \(P(x)\) contains a factor \((x - r)^k\) where \(k\) is a positive integer (the multiplicity of the root \(r\)). As \(x\) moves from just below \(r\) to just above \(r\), the base \(x - r\) changes sign from negative to positive, while EVERY OTHER factor in \(P(x)\) keeps the same nonzero sign across a small neighborhood of \(r\). What happens to \((x - r)^k\)?
- If \(k\) is ODD (\(k = 1, 3, 5, \dots\)): \((x - r)^k\) changes sign from \(-\) to \(+\), so \(P(x)\) CHANGES SIGN across \(x = r\) (the graph crosses the horizontal axis).
- If \(k\) is EVEN (\(k = 2, 4, 6, \dots\)): \((x - r)^k = \bigl((x - r)^{k/2}\bigr)^2\) is positive on BOTH sides of \(r\), so \(P(x)\) KEEPS THE SAME SIGN across \(x = r\) (the graph touches the horizontal axis and bounces back).

What about an irreducible quadratic factor \(ax^2 + bx + c\) with \(\Delta = b^2 - 4ac < 0\)? By the sign-definiteness criterion from quadratic inequalities, an irreducible quadratic NEVER equals \(0\) and keeps the constant sign of \(a\) on all of \(\mathbb{R}\). It therefore contributes NO critical points to the sign chart—just multiply the overall sign by the sign of \(a\) (positive if \(a > 0\), negative if \(a < 0\)).

[CALLOUT]
id: multiplicity-rule
label: Theorem
title: Multiplicity and irreducible-factor sign rules
tone: key

Let \(P(x)\) be factored completely over \(\mathbb{R}\) with real zeros \(r_1 < r_2 < \dots < r_m\):

(1) Far-right sign: For \(x > r_m\), every factor \((x - r_j)\) is positive, so the sign of \(P(x)\) on \((r_m, \infty)\) equals the sign of the leading coefficient of \(P(x)\).
(2) Odd multiplicity: Moving across a root \(r_j\) of odd multiplicity flips the sign of \(P(x)\).
(3) Even multiplicity: Moving across a root \(r_j\) of even multiplicity leaves the sign of \(P(x)\) unchanged.
(4) Irreducible quadratic (\(\Delta < 0\)): Contributes no critical point and multiplies the sign everywhere by the sign of its leading coefficient.
[/CALLOUT]

Watch out for reversed linear factors such as \((5 - x)\)! Rewrite \((5 - x) = -(x - 5)\) (or compute the leading coefficient carefully: the leading term of \((5 - x)\) is \(-x\), contributing a factor of \(-1\) to the leading coefficient of \(P(x)\)).

Let us apply [REF: multiplicity-rule | the multiplicity rules] to
\[
P(x) = (x - 2)^2(x + 1).
\]
Its real zeros are \(-1\) (multiplicity \(1\), odd) and \(2\) (multiplicity \(2\), even), and its leading coefficient is \(+1\). Starting on the far right \((2, \infty)\), \(P(x)\) is \(+\). Moving left across \(x = 2\) (even multiplicity), the sign STAYS \(+\) on \((-1, 2)\). Moving left across \(x = -1\) (odd multiplicity), the sign FLIPS to \(-\) on \((-\infty, -1)\).

The graph of \(y = (x - 2)^2(x + 1)\) makes this visual immediately: the curve crosses the axis at \(-1\) and merely kisses it from above at \(2\).

=== SECTION 3: Isolated zeros and the factor-division trap ===

Even-multiplicity roots create two classic traps that trip up careful students unless they check boundary zeros deliberately.

Trap 1: Misclassifying an isolated boundary zero (or an interior hole) at an even-multiplicity root. Look back at \(P(x) = (x - 2)^2(x + 1)\) from Section 2, whose sign is \(-\) on \((-\infty, -1)\), \(0\) at \(-1\), \(+\) on \((-1, 2)\), \(0\) at \(2\), and \(+\) on \((2, \infty)\). Compare all FOUR inequalities built from this exact same polynomial:

[TABLE]
Inequality | Open intervals where sign matches | Behavior at \(x = 2\) | Complete solution set
\((x - 2)^2(x + 1) < 0\) | \((-\infty, -1)\) | \(P(2) = 0\), fails \(< 0\) | \((-\infty, -1)\)
\((x - 2)^2(x + 1) \le 0\) | \((-\infty, -1)\) | \(P(2) = 0\), satisfies \(\le 0\)! | \((-\infty, -1] \cup \{2\}\)
\((x - 2)^2(x + 1) > 0\) | \((-1, 2)\) and \((2, \infty)\) | \(P(2) = 0\), fails \(> 0\)! | \((-1, 2) \cup (2, \infty)\)
\((x - 2)^2(x + 1) \ge 0\) | \((-1, 2)\) and \((2, \infty)\) | \(P(2) = 0\), bridges across \(2\)! | \([-1, \infty)\)
[/TABLE]

Study the second and third rows closely! In \((x - 2)^2(x + 1) \le 0\), \(P(x)\) is positive on BOTH sides of \(2\), yet \(x = 2\) itself makes \(P(2) = 0 \le 0\), so the isolated singleton \(\{2\}\) MUST be united with \((-\infty, -1]\). Conversely, in \((x - 2)^2(x + 1) > 0\), \(P(x)\) is positive on both sides of \(2\), yet \(x = 2\) fails strict positivity, punching a hole that splits \((-1, \infty)\) into \((-1, 2) \cup (2, \infty)\).

[CALLOUT]
id: zero-accounting
label: Strategy
title: Accounting for every real zero
tone: note

After reading the open sign intervals from a sign chart, make a separate explicit pass over EVERY real zero \(r_j\):
(1) For \(\le 0\) or \(\ge 0\), EVERY real zero of \(P(x)\) belongs to the solution set—either closing an interval endpoint, merging two adjacent open intervals, or appearing as an isolated singleton \(\{r_j\}\).
(2) For \(< 0\) or \(> 0\), NO real zero belongs to the solution set—so any zero sitting between two matching open intervals punches a hole.
[/CALLOUT]

Trap 2: Dividing both sides of a polynomial inequality by a variable factor such as \((x - 2)\) or \((x - 2)^2\). Suppose you are asked to solve
\[
x(x - 2) \ge 3(x - 2).
\]
If you divide both sides by \((x - 2)\) to get \(x \ge 3\), you make two fatal errors: when \(x = 2\) you divided by zero (dropping the valid solution \(x = 2\)), and when \(x < 2\) the factor \((x - 2)\) is negative (so the inequality should have flipped to \(x \le 3\), which holds for EVERY \(x < 2\)!). The right method NEVER divides by a variable factor; it subtracts to one side and factors:
\[
x(x - 2) - 3(x - 2) \ge 0 \implies (x - 2)(x - 3) \ge 0 \implies (-\infty, 2] \cup [3, \infty).
\]
Even dividing by an even power \((x - a)^2\) is unsafe! Although \((x - a)^2\) is never negative, it vanishes at \(x = a\), so canceling it either drops the valid root \(x = a\) (in \(\le\) or \(\ge\)) or silently keeps the invalid point \(x = a\) (in \(<\) or \(>\)). Subtracting to one side and factoring avoids every version of this trap automatically.
