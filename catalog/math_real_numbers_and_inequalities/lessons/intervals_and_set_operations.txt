=== SECTION 1: Intervals, set-builder notation, and the real line ===

Every inequality in one real variable picks out a subset of the real line \(\mathbb{R}\). Writing "all real numbers \(x\) greater than \(-1\) and at most \(5\)" works for a single condition, but when we combine several conditions—taking overlaps, combining separate regions, or excluding single boundary points—sentences quickly become clumsy and error-prone. Standard set-builder notation and interval notation give a compact, unambiguous language for describing subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\).

In set-builder notation, we describe a subset by naming a variable, stating where it lives, and giving the condition it must satisfy after a colon (or vertical bar):
\[
\{x \in \mathbb{R} : -1 < x \le 5\}.
\]
Read this aloud as "the set of all real numbers \(x\) such that \(-1 < x \le 5\)." When a subset forms a single unbroken stretch of the real line (with no gaps inside it), we write it even more compactly as an interval using parentheses for excluded endpoints and square brackets for included endpoints: \((-1, 5]\).

[CALLOUT]
id: bounded-intervals
label: Definition
title: Bounded intervals on \(\mathbb{R}\)
tone: key

For real numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(a < b\):

(1) Open interval: \((a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x < b\}\), excluding both endpoints.
(2) Closed interval: \([a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x \le b\}\), including both endpoints.
(3) Half-open intervals: \([a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x < b\}\) and \((a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x \le b\}\).
[/CALLOUT]

Always write the smaller endpoint on the left and the larger endpoint on the right. Notice also that the notation \((a, b)\) is context-dependent in mathematics: in the plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\) it denotes an ordered pair of coordinates, whereas on the real line \(\mathbb{R}\) it denotes an open interval. Surrounding notation (such as \(x \in (a, b)\) or a union with another interval) makes the meaning clear.

What happens if the two endpoints coincide (\(a = b\)) or if no real number satisfies the condition at all?
- \([a, a] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x \le a\} = \{a\}\), a singleton set containing only the single number \(a\).
- \((a, a) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x < a\} = \emptyset\), the empty set containing no elements. Likewise, \([a, a) = \emptyset\) and \((a, a] = \emptyset\), because no real number can be both equal to \(a\) and strictly less than (or strictly greater than) \(a\).
- Similarly, a contradictory condition such as \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < 1 \text{ and } x > 4\}\) describes the empty set \(\emptyset\).

Many subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\) extend indefinitely to the left or right, forming unbounded rays. To write them in interval notation, we use the symbols \(-\infty\) and \(\infty\) (sometimes written \(+\infty\)).

[CALLOUT]
id: unbounded-intervals
label: Definition
title: Unbounded intervals (rays) and \(\mathbb{R}\)
tone: key

For any real number \(a \in \mathbb{R}\):

(1) \((a, \infty) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > a\}\) and \([a, \infty) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \ge a\}\).
(2) \((-\infty, a) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < a\}\) and \((-\infty, a] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \le a\}\).
(3) \((-\infty, \infty) = \mathbb{R}\).
[/CALLOUT]

A critical convention: \(\infty\) and \(-\infty\) ALWAYS receive a parenthesis, never a square bracket. A square bracket means the endpoint belongs to the set as a real number, whereas \(\infty\) and \(-\infty\) are not real numbers—they merely indicate that the interval continues without bound. Writing \([2, \infty]\) or \([-\infty, 5)\) is therefore invalid notation.

On a hand-drawn number line, included endpoints (brackets) are drawn as solid dots, excluded endpoints (parentheses) are drawn as open hollow circles, and rays end with an arrow pointing left or right. The table below gathers all standard types side by side.

[TABLE]
Interval notation | Set-builder notation | Endpoint status | Type
\((a, b)\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x < b\}\) | \(a\) open, \(b\) open | Bounded open
\([a, b]\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x \le b\}\) | \(a\) solid, \(b\) solid | Bounded closed
\([a, b)\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x < b\}\) | \(a\) solid, \(b\) open | Bounded half-open
\((a, b]\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x \le b\}\) | \(a\) open, \(b\) solid | Bounded half-open
\([a, \infty)\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \ge a\}\) | \(a\) solid, unbounded right | Closed ray
\((-\infty, b)\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < b\}\) | unbounded left, \(b\) open | Open ray
\(\{a\}\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x = a\}\) | single solid point \(a\) | Singleton
\(\emptyset\) | \(\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < a \text{ and } x > a\}\) | no points | Empty set
[/TABLE]

=== SECTION 2: Union and intersection on the number line ===

In practice, conditions rarely produce just one interval. We often need numbers that satisfy two conditions simultaneously ("and") or at least one of two conditions ("or"). Those two logical connectives correspond directly to the set operations of intersection and union.

[CALLOUT]
id: union-intersection
label: Definition
title: Union and intersection of subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\)
tone: key

For any two subsets \(A, B \subseteq \mathbb{R}\):

(1) Intersection (logical "and"): \(A \cap B = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\}\).
(2) Union (logical "or"): \(A \cup B = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}\), where "or" is inclusive (in \(A\), in \(B\), or in both).
[/CALLOUT]

To find \(A \cap B\) and \(A \cup B\) reliably, visualize \(A\) and \(B\) stacked above the same horizontal real line. Then \(A \cap B\) is the horizontal overlap where BOTH sets are present, and \(A \cup B\) is the total horizontal span where AT LEAST ONE set is present.

Let us work through a concrete example with \(A = [-2, 3)\) and \(B = (1, 5]\).
- For \(A \cap B\): a number \(x\) must satisfy both \(-2 \le x < 3\) and \(1 < x \le 5\). The stricter lower bound is \(x > 1\) (since \(1 > -2\)) and the stricter upper bound is \(x < 3\) (since \(3 < 5\)). Hence \(A \cap B = (1, 3)\). Notice that \(1 \notin B\) so \(1 \notin A \cap B\), and \(3 \notin A\) so \(3 \notin A \cap B\).
- For \(A \cup B\): the two intervals overlap on \((1, 3)\), so they merge into one unbroken stretch from \(-2\) (included via \(A\)) to \(5\) (included via \(B\)): \(A \cup B = [-2, 5]\).

Because plotted markers here cannot distinguish hollow circles from solid dots visually, read the horizontal spans from the plot while verifying each endpoint's strictness directly from the brackets and parentheses.

Endpoints deserve special care when two intervals meet at a single boundary point without overlapping in their interiors. Compare four touching pairs around \(x = 4\):
- \((1, 4] \cap [4, 7) = \{4\}\): \(4\) belongs to both sets, and no other point does, so the intersection is a singleton.
- \((1, 4) \cap [4, 7) = \emptyset\): \(4\) is missing from the first interval, so the two sets share no elements at all.
- \((1, 4) \cup [4, 7) = (1, 7)\): because \(4\) belongs to the second interval, it bridges the gap and merges the union into a single interval.
- \((1, 4) \cup (4, 7)\): here \(4\) belongs to NEITHER interval, so there is a genuine hole at \(4\). This union CANNOT be simplified to \((1, 7)\); it stays written as \((1, 4) \cup (4, 7)\) (or \((1, 7) \setminus \{4\}\), introduced in Section 3).

When combining a union with an intersection—such as \((A \cup B) \cap C\)—it is often cleanest to intersect \(C\) with each piece separately and then take the union of the results:
\[
(A \cup B) \cap C = (A \cap C) \cup (B \cap C).
\]
For example, if \(A = (-\infty, -1]\), \(B = (2, 6)\), and \(C = (-3, 4]\), then \(A \cap C = (-3, -1]\) and \(B \cap C = (2, 4]\), so \((A \cup B) \cap C = (-3, -1] \cup (2, 4]\).

=== SECTION 3: Complements, set difference, and endpoint flips ===

Alongside "and" (\(\cap\)) and "or" (\(\cup\)), we need "not" (complement) and "but not" (set difference).

[CALLOUT]
id: complement-diff
label: Definition
title: Complement and set difference in \(\mathbb{R}\)
tone: key

For subsets \(A, B \subseteq \mathbb{R}\):

(1) Complement in \(\mathbb{R}\): \(A^c = \mathbb{R} \setminus A = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \notin A\}\).
(2) Set difference: \(A \setminus B = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \in A \text{ and } x \notin B\} = A \cap B^c\).
[/CALLOUT]

The single rule that prevents almost every mistake with complements is the endpoint flip: a point \(p\) belongs to \(A^c\) if and only if it does NOT belong to \(A\). Consequently:
- Every included endpoint of \(A\) (square bracket, \(\le\) or \(\ge\)) becomes an excluded endpoint of \(A^c\) (parenthesis, \(<\) or \(>\)).
- Every excluded endpoint of \(A\) (parenthesis) becomes an included endpoint of \(A^c\) (square bracket).

Let us check this on \(A = [1, 3)\). A real number fails to satisfy \(1 \le x < 3\) precisely when either \(x < 1\) or \(x \ge 3\). Therefore
\[
[1, 3)^c = (-\infty, 1) \cup [3, \infty).
\]
Check both boundaries directly: \(1 \in [1, 3)\), so \(1 \notin A^c\) (open parenthesis at \(1\)); \(3 \notin [1, 3)\), so \(3 \in A^c\) (closed bracket at \(3\)). Similarly, for a ray such as \((-\infty, 2]\), the complement is \((2, \infty)\); for a singleton \(\{5\}\), the complement is \((-\infty, 5) \cup (5, \infty)\).

What happens when we complement a union of two disjoint pieces, such as \(S = (-\infty, -2) \cup [3, \infty)\)? A number lies outside \(S\) if and only if it lies outside the first piece AND outside the second piece:
\[
S^c = (-\infty, -2)^c \cap [3, \infty)^c = [-2, \infty) \cap (-\infty, 3) = [-2, 3).
\]
Notice the logical pattern (De Morgan's laws): the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, \((A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c\), and the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements, \((A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c\).

Now apply [REF: complement-diff.2 | set difference] using \(A \setminus B = A \cap B^c\). Suppose \(A = [-4, 6]\) and \(B = (-1, 2]\). First compute \(B^c = (-\infty, -1] \cup (2, \infty)\), flipping both endpoints of \(B\). Then intersect with \(A\):
\[
A \setminus B = [-4, 6] \cap \bigl((-\infty, -1] \cup (2, \infty)\bigr) = [-4, -1] \cup (2, 6].
\]
Double-check the two interior endpoints: \(-1\) is in \(A\) and NOT in \(B\), so \(-1\) remains in \(A \setminus B\) (closed bracket); \(2\) is in \(A\) and IS in \(B\), so \(2\) is removed from \(A \setminus B\) (open parenthesis).

[CALLOUT]
id: endpoint-audit
label: Strategy
title: Three-second endpoint check
tone: note

After computing any union, intersection, complement, or set difference, test every finite boundary number individually against the original definition: ask "is this exact number in \(A\)? is it in \(B\)?" and check whether the logical rule ("and", "or", "not", "in \(A\) but not \(B\)") keeps or drops it.
[/CALLOUT]

=== SECTION 4: Translating compound conditions and simplifying sets ===

In later work, solution sets come from logical combinations of conditions—"satisfies condition 1 AND condition 2, EXCEPT at excluded points"—so fluency means moving smoothly in both directions between logical wording, set-builder form, and simplified interval notation.

Let us work through a multi-condition translation. Suppose a real number \(x\) is admissible if it satisfies:
1. \(x \ge -5\) and \(x < 8\), AND
2. it is NOT true that \(-2 < x \le 1\), AND
3. \(x \ne 4\).

Translate each requirement into a subset of \(\mathbb{R}\):
- Condition 1 gives \(C_1 = [-5, 8)\).
- Condition 2 gives the complement of \((-2, 1]\), which is \(C_2 = (-\infty, -2] \cup (1, \infty)\).
- Condition 3 gives \(C_3 = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{4\} = (-\infty, 4) \cup (4, \infty)\).

Because all three conditions must hold simultaneously ("AND"), the admissible set is the intersection \(C_1 \cap C_2 \cap C_3\). Intersect \(C_1\) with \(C_2\) first:
\[
[-5, 8) \cap \bigl((-\infty, -2] \cup (1, \infty)\bigr) = [-5, -2] \cup (1, 8).
\]
Finally, remove \(4\) (which lies inside \((1, 8)\)) by intersecting with \(C_3\):
\[
[-5, -2] \cup (1, 4) \cup (4, 8).
\]
Run [REF: endpoint-audit | the endpoint check] on every boundary point: \(-5\) satisfies all three conditions (included); \(-2\) is not in \((-2, 1]\) so it survives (included); \(1\) IS in \((-2, 1]\) so it is excluded; \(4\) fails Condition 3 (excluded); \(8\) fails Condition 1 (excluded). Every bracket and parenthesis checks out.

Watch also for redundant pieces when taking unions of three or more intervals. For instance, simplify
\[
(-3, 2) \cup [0, 5] \cup [5, 7) \cup \{2\}.
\]
First merge overlapping intervals: \((-3, 2) \cup [0, 5] = (-3, 5]\). Since \(2\) already lies inside \((-3, 5]\), uniting with \(\{2\}\) adds nothing. Finally, \((-3, 5] \cup [5, 7) = (-3, 7)\) because \(5\) belongs to both intervals. Always check whether separate intervals overlap or meet at an included endpoint before leaving a union split into multiple pieces.
