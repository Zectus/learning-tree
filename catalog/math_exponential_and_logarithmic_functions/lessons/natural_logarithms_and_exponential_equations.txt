=== SECTION 1: The natural logarithm and the universal base-conversion bridge ===

When we introduced Euler's number \(e \approx 2.71828\) and the natural exponential function \(e^x\), we left open how to solve \(e^x = 10\) for \(x\) or how to rewrite a general exponential \(b^x\) using base \(e\). With general logarithms and change of base now in hand, we simply choose the special base \(b = e\).

The base-\(e\) logarithm is called the natural logarithm and is written \(\ln(x) = \log_e(x)\). Every property of \(\log_b(x)\) applies directly to \(\ln(x)\) with \(b = e > 1\): it has domain \((0, \infty)\), range \((-\infty, \infty)\), vertical asymptote \(x = 0\), \(x\)-intercept \((1, 0)\), and is strictly increasing and one-to-one.

[CALLOUT]
id: ln-bridge
label: Identities
title: Natural logarithm identities and universal base conversion
tone: key

For \(x > 0\) (where \(\ln(x)\) appears) and any real \(t\):

(1) Defining equivalence: \(y = \ln(x) \iff e^y = x\), with \(\ln(1) = 0\) and \(\ln(e) = 1\).
(2) Inverse cancellation: \(\ln(e^t) = t\) for all \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), and \(e^{\ln(x)} = x\) for all \(x > 0\).
(3) Universal base-conversion bridge: for every \(b > 0\) and every \(t \in \mathbb{R}\),
\[
b^t = e^{t \ln b}.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

To derive [REF: ln-bridge.3 | the base-conversion bridge], rewrite the positive constant \(b\) using [REF: ln-bridge.2 | inverse cancellation] as \(b = e^{\ln b}\), then raise both sides to the power \(t\):
\[
b^t = \left(e^{\ln b}\right)^t = e^{t \ln b}.
\]
Read what this says structurally: every exponential function \(b^t\) is simply the natural exponential \(e^{kt}\) with horizontal scaling constant \(k = \ln b\). When \(b > 1\), we have \(\ln b > 0\) (growth); when \(0 < b < 1\), we have \(\ln b < 0\) (decay). For example, \(2^x = e^{x \ln 2} \approx e^{0.6931x}\) and \((1/3)^x = e^{x \ln(1/3)} = e^{-x \ln 3} \approx e^{-1.0986x}\). Likewise, change of base lets us convert any logarithm into natural logarithms via \(\log_b(x) = \dfrac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)}\).

Combining logarithm laws with [REF: ln-bridge.2 | inverse cancellation] also simplifies nested exponential-logarithm expressions cleanly. For instance, to simplify \(e^{3\ln 2 - \ln 5}\), first condense the exponent into a single logarithm: \(3\ln 2 - \ln 5 = \ln(2^3) - \ln 5 = \ln(8/5)\). Then \(e^{\ln(8/5)} = 8/5\).

=== SECTION 2: Solving exponential equations: common bases, isolation, and distinct bases ===

An exponential equation is an equation in which the unknown \(x\) appears in one or more exponents. Rather than guessing a single recipe, learn to recognize which algebraic structure is present and apply the cleanest method. We always find exact symbolic solutions (in terms of \(\ln\) or \(\log\)) before taking any optional decimal approximation.

Strategy 1: Matching a common base.
Whenever both sides can be written as powers of the same base \(b\), use the one-to-one property \(b^u = b^v \implies u = v\)—no logarithms needed.
Example: Solve \(4^{x + 1} = 8^{2x - 3}\). Since \(4 = 2^2\) and \(8 = 2^3\),
\[
\left(2^2\right)^{x + 1} = \left(2^3\right)^{2x - 3} \implies 2^{2x + 2} = 2^{6x - 9}.
\]
Equating exponents gives \(2x + 2 = 6x - 9 \implies 4x = 11 \implies x = \frac{11}{4}\).

Strategy 2: Isolating a single exponential term.
When only one exponential term appears and its value is not a clean power of the base, isolate the exponential first, then apply \(\ln\) (or \(\log_b\)) to both sides.
Example: Solve \(5 \cdot 3^{2x - 1} = 20\). First divide by \(5\):
\[
3^{2x - 1} = 4.
\]
Take \(\ln\) of both sides and bring the exponent down with the power rule:
\[
(2x - 1)\ln 3 = \ln 4 \implies 2x - 1 = \frac{\ln 4}{\ln 3} \implies x = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(1 + \frac{\ln 4}{\ln 3}\right) \approx 1.1309.
\]
Watch for the no-solution diagnostic right after isolation: in \(2e^{3x} + 10 = 4\), isolating the exponential gives \(e^{3x} = -3\). Because \(e^{3x} > 0\) for every real number \(x\), this equation has no real solution—do not take \(\ln(-3)\).

Strategy 3: Taking \(\ln\) of both sides with distinct bases.
When two different bases both carry variable exponents, such as \(2^{x + 1} = 5^{2x - 3}\), there is no common rational base. Apply \(\ln\) to both sides immediately, distribute to separate \(x\)-terms from constants, factor out \(x\), and divide:
\[
(x + 1)\ln 2 = (2x - 3)\ln 5
\]
\[
x\ln 2 + \ln 2 = 2x\ln 5 - 3\ln 5
\]
\[
\ln 2 + 3\ln 5 = x(2\ln 5 - \ln 2) \implies x = \frac{\ln 2 + 3\ln 5}{2\ln 5 - \ln 2}.
\]
Using logarithm laws, this exact answer can also be condensed to \(x = \dfrac{\ln(2 \cdot 5^3)}{\ln(5^2 / 2)} = \dfrac{\ln(250)}{\ln(25/2)} \approx 2.1869\).

=== SECTION 3: Factoring and quadratic-type exponential equations ===

When an equation contains sums or differences of exponential terms, taking \(\ln\) of both sides right away fails because \(\ln(A + B) \ne \ln A + \ln B\). Instead, two algebraic structures handle these sums cleanly.

Strategy 4: Factoring out a common exponential factor.
When several terms share the same base with exponents differing by constants, factor out the lowest power of that base.
Example: Solve \(3^{x + 2} - 3^x = 72\). Rewrite \(3^{x + 2} = 3^2 \cdot 3^x = 9 \cdot 3^x\), then factor out \(3^x\):
\[
3^x(9 - 1) = 72 \implies 8 \cdot 3^x = 72 \implies 3^x = 9 = 3^2 \implies x = 2.
\]

Strategy 5: Quadratic substitution \(u = b^x > 0\).
When one exponential term is the square of another—such as \(e^{2x} = (e^x)^2\) or \(4^x = (2^2)^x = (2^x)^2\)—introduce \(u = b^x\), solve the resulting quadratic in \(u\), discard any root with \(u \le 0\), and solve \(b^x = u\) for each positive root.

Example A: Solve \(e^{2x} - 5e^x + 6 = 0\). Setting \(u = e^x > 0\) gives \(u^2 - 5u + 6 = 0\), which factors as \((u - 2)(u - 3) = 0\). Both \(u = 2\) and \(u = 3\) are positive, giving two exact solutions: \(x = \ln 2\) and \(x = \ln 3\).

Example B: Solve \(4^x - 3 \cdot 2^x - 4 = 0\). Since \(4^x = (2^x)^2\), setting \(u = 2^x > 0\) gives
\[
u^2 - 3u - 4 = 0 \implies (u - 4)(u + 1) = 0 \implies u = 4 \text{ or } u = -1.
\]
Because \(2^x > 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), the root \(u = -1\) produces no real solution and must be discarded. Only \(2^x = 4\) survives, giving the single real solution \(x = 2\).

The table below summarizes all five methods so you can match each equation to its cleanest strategy at a glance:

[TABLE]
Equation pattern | Example | First move
Same base or power of a common base | \(4^{x+1} = 8^{2x-3}\) | Rewrite in common base; equate exponents
Single exponential term | \(5 \cdot 3^{2x-1} = 20\) | Isolate \(b^{\text{expr}}\); check RHS \(> 0\); apply \(\ln\)
Two distinct bases, variable exponents | \(2^{x+1} = 5^{2x-3}\) | Apply \(\ln\) to both sides; group \(x\)-terms
Sum/difference with same base, shifted exponents | \(3^{x+2} - 3^x = 72\) | Factor out the smallest \(b^x\) term
One exponential is the square of another | \(4^x - 3 \cdot 2^x - 4 = 0\) | Set \(u = b^x > 0\); solve quadratic; discard \(u \le 0\)
[/TABLE]
