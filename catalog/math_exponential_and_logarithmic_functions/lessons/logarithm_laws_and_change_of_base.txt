=== SECTION 1: Deriving the product, quotient, and power laws ===

Because every logarithm is an exponent, the three fundamental laws of exponents—adding exponents when multiplying powers, subtracting exponents when dividing powers, and multiplying exponents when raising a power to a power—translate directly into algebraic laws for logarithms.

Let \(b > 0\) with \(b \ne 1\), let \(M > 0\) and \(N > 0\), and let \(p\) be any real number. To derive all three laws from the definition of a logarithm, set
\[
u = \log_b(M) \quad \text{and} \quad v = \log_b(N).
\]
By the defining equivalence of logarithms, this means \(M = b^u\) and \(N = b^v\). Now apply the exponent laws directly to \(M\) and \(N\):

1. Product: \(MN = b^u \cdot b^v = b^{u + v}\). Converting back to logarithmic form gives \(\log_b(MN) = u + v = \log_b(M) + \log_b(N)\).
2. Quotient: \(\frac{M}{N} = \frac{b^u}{b^v} = b^{u - v}\). Converting back to logarithmic form gives \(\log_b\!\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = u - v = \log_b(M) - \log_b(N)\).
3. Power: \(M^p = (b^u)^p = b^{pu}\). Converting back to logarithmic form gives \(\log_b(M^p) = pu = p\log_b(M)\).

[CALLOUT]
id: log-laws
label: Laws
title: Algebraic laws of logarithms
tone: key

For any valid base \(b > 0\), \(b \ne 1\), positive quantities \(M > 0\) and \(N > 0\), and any real number \(p\):

(1) Product rule: \(\log_b(MN) = \log_b(M) + \log_b(N)\).
(2) Quotient rule: \(\log_b\!\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M) - \log_b(N)\).
(3) Power rule: \(\log_b(M^p) = p\log_b(M)\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: log-laws | these three laws] work in both directions: expanding turns one complicated logarithm of products, quotients, and powers into a sum and difference of simpler logarithms; condensing runs the same rules in reverse to combine several logarithms with the same base into a single logarithm.

Worked example 1 (expanding): For \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\), expand \(\log_2\!\left(\frac{8x^3}{\sqrt{y}}\right)\):
\[
\log_2\!\left(\frac{8x^3}{y^{1/2}}\right) = \log_2(8) + \log_2(x^3) - \log_2(y^{1/2}) = 3 + 3\log_2(x) - \frac{1}{2}\log_2(y).
\]

Worked example 2 (condensing): For \(x, y, z > 0\), condense \(2\log_5(x) + \frac{1}{3}\log_5(y) - 4\log_5(z)\). Always pull coefficients inside as exponents via [REF: log-laws.3 | the power rule] before combining sums and differences:
\[
2\log_5(x) + \frac{1}{3}\log_5(y) - 4\log_5(z) = \log_5(x^2) + \log_5(y^{1/3}) - \log_5(z^4) = \log_5\!\left(\frac{x^2\sqrt[3]{y}}{z^4}\right).
\]
Watch a subtle grouping detail when two subtracted terms appear: \(\log_b(A) - \log_b(B) - \log_b(C) = \log_b(A) - \bigl(\log_b(B) + \log_b(C)\bigr) = \log_b\!\left(\frac{A}{BC}\right)\). Both \(B\) and \(C\) go in the denominator.

=== SECTION 2: Two persistent traps: illegal distribution and domain alteration ===

The algebraic laws of logarithms are compact, which makes it tempting to invent similar-looking rules that are false—or to apply true rules without checking whether the domain silently changed.

[CALLOUT]
id: log-traps
label: Caution
title: Non-rules and domain changes to avoid
tone: caution

(1) Logarithms do NOT distribute over sums or differences: \(\log_b(A + B) \ne \log_b(A) + \log_b(B)\) and \(\log_b(A - B) \ne \log_b(A) - \log_b(B)\). Counterexample: \(\log_2(4 + 4) = \log_2(8) = 3\), whereas \(\log_2(4) + \log_2(4) = 2 + 2 = 4\).
(2) A quotient of logarithms is NOT a logarithm of a quotient or difference: \(\dfrac{\log_b(A)}{\log_b(B)} \ne \log_b\!\left(\dfrac{A}{B}\right)\) and \(\dfrac{\log_b(A)}{\log_b(B)} \ne \log_b(A - B)\). Counterexample: \(\dfrac{\log_2(8)}{\log_2(2)} = \dfrac{3}{1} = 3\), whereas \(\log_2(8/2) = \log_2(4) = 2\).
(3) Rewriting with [REF: log-laws | the logarithm laws] can alter the domain whenever arguments can be negative.
[/CALLOUT]

Look closely at [REF: log-traps.3 | the third warning]. In [REF: log-laws | the laws of logarithms], we required \(M > 0\) and \(N > 0\). When \(x\) is a variable rather than a known positive number, applying a law blindly can shrink or enlarge the domain:

- Consider \(\log_2(x^2)\). Because \(x^2 > 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\), this function is defined on both \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((0, \infty)\); for example, at \(x = -2\), \(\log_2((-2)^2) = \log_2(4) = 2\). Yet \(2\log_2(x)\) is defined ONLY on \((0, \infty)\)! To preserve the full domain \(x \ne 0\), write
\[
\log_2(x^2) = \log_2(\lvert x \rvert^2) = 2\log_2\lvert x \rvert.
\]
- Now consider the sum \(\log_b(x) + \log_b(x - 1)\). Both logarithms must be defined simultaneously, requiring \(x > 0\) AND \(x - 1 > 0\), so the domain is \((1, \infty)\). When we condense the sum into \(\log_b(x(x - 1))\), the product \(x(x - 1)\) is positive not only for \(x > 1\), but also for \(x < 0\) (where two negative factors multiply to a positive number). Thus the condensed expression has domain \((-\infty, 0) \cup (1, \infty)\)—strictly larger than the original!

Keeping track of domain changes now will be essential when we solve logarithmic equations later.

=== SECTION 3: Change of base and its corollaries ===

Suppose you need to evaluate \(\log_3(20)\), or simplify \(\log_2(x) + \log_8(x)\) where two logarithms have different bases. None of [REF: log-laws | the basic laws] applies directly across different bases. How do we convert a logarithm from one base \(b\) to any other valid base \(c\)?

Start from the definition: let \(y = \log_b(x)\) (where \(x > 0\), \(b > 0\), \(b \ne 1\)), so \(b^y = x\). Take the base-\(c\) logarithm of both sides (for any \(c > 0, c \ne 1\)):
\[
\log_c(b^y) = \log_c(x).
\]
Apply [REF: log-laws.3 | the power rule] on the left-hand side:
\[
y\log_c(b) = \log_c(x).
\]
Because \(b \ne 1\), we have \(\log_c(b) \ne 0\), so we may safely divide both sides by \(\log_c(b)\) to isolate \(y = \log_b(x)\):
\[
\log_b(x) = \frac{\log_c(x)}{\log_c(b)}.
\]

[CALLOUT]
id: change-of-base
label: Formulas
title: Change of base and key corollaries
tone: key

For valid bases \(a, b, c > 0\) (each \(\ne 1\)), any \(x > 0\), and any nonzero real number \(k\):

(1) Change-of-base formula: \(\log_b(x) = \dfrac{\log_c(x)}{\log_c(b)}\).
(2) Reciprocal base-argument swap: \(\log_b(a) = \dfrac{1}{\log_a(b)}\).
(3) Power-in-the-base rule: \(\log_{b^k}(x) = \dfrac{1}{k}\log_b(x)\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: change-of-base.2 | corollary (2)] and [REF: change-of-base.3 | corollary (3)] fall out in a single line from [REF: change-of-base.1 | the change-of-base formula]:
- Setting \(x = a\) and \(c = a\) gives \(\log_b(a) = \frac{\log_a(a)}{\log_a(b)} = \frac{1}{\log_a(b)}\).
- Replacing \(b\) by \(b^k\) and choosing \(c = b\) gives \(\log_{b^k}(x) = \frac{\log_b(x)}{\log_b(b^k)} = \frac{\log_b(x)}{k} = \frac{1}{k}\log_b(x)\).

Here are three ways these formulas work in practice:
1. Evaluating arbitrary bases with the common logarithm (\(c = 10\)):
\[
\log_3(20) = \frac{\log(20)}{\log(3)} \approx \frac{1.3010}{0.4771} \approx 2.7268.
\]
2. Simplifying a chain of products by converting every factor to a single base \(c = 10\):
\[
\log_2(3) \cdot \log_3(5) \cdot \log_5(16) = \frac{\log(3)}{\log(2)} \cdot \frac{\log(5)}{\log(3)} \cdot \frac{\log(16)}{\log(5)} = \frac{\log(16)}{\log(2)} = \log_2(16) = 4.
\]
3. Combining logarithms whose bases are powers of one another using [REF: change-of-base.3 | the power-in-the-base rule]:
\[
\log_2(x) + \log_8(x) = \log_2(x) + \log_{2^3}(x) = \log_2(x) + \frac{1}{3}\log_2(x) = \frac{4}{3}\log_2(x).
\]
