=== SECTION 1: Three equivalent forms of unconstrained exponential change ===

Suppose a bacterial colony starts with \(100\) cells and grows by \(25\%\) every hour. A biologist might describe the same colony by saying it doubles every \(3.11\text{ hours}\), while a physicist might write \(A(t) = 100 e^{0.2231 t}\). All three descriptions represent the exact same curve, written in three different parameterizations of unconstrained exponential change.

Recall from the previous lesson that every positive base \(b\) can be rewritten in base \(e\) via the conversion identity \(b^t = e^{t \ln b}\). Applying that single bridge to an exponential function \(A(t) = A_0 b^t\) (with \(A_0 > 0\) and \(b > 0, b \ne 1\)) links the three standard modeling forms directly:

1. Discrete-rate form: \(A(t) = A_0 (1 + r)^t\), where \(r > -1\) (\(r \ne 0\)) is the fractional change per unit of time (\(r > 0\) for growth, \(-1 < r < 0\) for decay). Writing \((1 + r)^t = e^{t \ln(1 + r)}\) immediately matches it to the continuous-rate form \(A(t) = A_0 e^{kt}\) with
\[
k = \ln(1 + r) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad 1 + r = e^k \quad (r = e^k - 1).
\]

2. Doubling-time form (growth, \(k > 0\)): The doubling time \(T_d > 0\) is the elapsed time required for the quantity to reach \(2A_0\). Setting \(A(T_d) = 2A_0\) in \(A(t) = A_0 e^{kt}\) gives
\[
A_0 e^{k T_d} = 2 A_0 \implies e^{k T_d} = 2 \implies k T_d = \ln 2 \implies T_d = \frac{\ln 2}{k}.
\]
Notice that \(A_0\) canceled on the very first step! Whether the colony starts at \(100\) cells or \(10{,}000\) cells, it takes the exact same time \(T_d\) to double. Substituting \(k = \frac{\ln 2}{T_d}\) back into \(A_0 e^{kt}\) yields \(A_0 e^{(t/T_d)\ln 2} = A_0 \cdot 2^{t / T_d}\).

3. Half-life form (decay, \(k < 0\)): The half-life \(T_{1/2} > 0\) is the elapsed time required for the quantity to fall to \(\frac{1}{2} A_0\). Setting \(A(T_{1/2}) = \frac{1}{2} A_0\) in \(A(t) = A_0 e^{kt}\) cancels \(A_0\) again:
\[
A_0 e^{k T_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2} A_0 \implies e^{k T_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2} \implies k T_{1/2} = \ln\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\ln 2 \implies T_{1/2} = -\frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{\ln 2}{|k|}.
\]
(Note the minus sign: because \(k < 0\) during decay, \(-\frac{\ln 2}{k}\) is positive.) Equivalently, \(A(t) = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t / T_{1/2}}\), and since \(\ln 2 \approx 0.6931\), both formulas scale directly with \(\ln 2\).

[CALLOUT]
id: exp-model-forms
label: Reference
title: Equivalent forms of exponential growth and decay
tone: key

For an unconstrained exponential process with initial value \(A_0 > 0\) at \(t = 0\):

(1) Discrete-rate and continuous-rate conversion:
\[
A(t) = A_0 (1 + r)^t = A_0 e^{kt}, \qquad k = \ln(1 + r), \qquad r = e^k - 1.
\]
(2) Doubling time (growth, \(k > 0\)):
\[
A(t) = A_0 \cdot 2^{t / T_d}, \qquad T_d = \frac{\ln 2}{k}, \qquad k = \frac{\ln 2}{T_d}.
\]
(3) Half-life (decay, \(k < 0\)):
\[
A(t) = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t / T_{1/2}}, \qquad T_{1/2} = -\frac{\ln 2}{k}, \qquad k = -\frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}.
\]
(4) Independence from initial amount: Both \(T_d\) and \(T_{1/2}\) depend only on the rate \(k\), never on \(A_0\). Any equal time step \(\Delta t\) multiplies \(A(t)\) by the same factor \(e^{k \Delta t}\) regardless of how large or small \(A(t)\) currently is.
[/CALLOUT]

Worked example (Converting a decay specification): A radioactive sample loses \(15\%\) of its mass every \(2\text{ days}\). Find its continuous decay constant \(k\) (in \(\text{day}^{-1}\)) and its exact half-life \(T_{1/2}\) in days.

Losing \(15\%\) every \(2\text{ days}\) means \(85\% = 0.85\) remains after each \(2\text{ day}\) interval, so \(A(t) = A_0 (0.85)^{t/2}\). Matching \(e^{2k} = 0.85\) gives
\[
k = \frac{\ln(0.85)}{2} \approx -0.08126\text{ day}^{-1}.
\]
Applying [REF: exp-model-forms.3 | the half-life formula] gives
\[
T_{1/2} = -\frac{\ln 2}{k} = -\frac{2\ln 2}{\ln(0.85)} = \frac{2\ln 2}{\ln(20/17)} \approx 8.53\text{ days}.
\]
Check: after \(8.53\text{ days}\), \((0.85)^{8.53/2} \approx 0.50\).

=== SECTION 2: Calibrating models from data and comparing rate conventions ===

In practice, you rarely receive \(k\) directly—you receive two measurements \((t_1, A_1)\) and \((t_2, A_2)\), often neither of them at \(t = 0\). Dividing the two equations \(A_2 = A_0 e^{k t_2}\) and \(A_1 = A_0 e^{k t_1}\) eliminates the unknown \(A_0\) in one step:
\[
\frac{A_2}{A_1} = \frac{A_0 e^{k t_2}}{A_0 e^{k t_1}} = e^{k(t_2 - t_1)} \implies k = \frac{\ln(A_2 / A_1)}{t_2 - t_1}.
\]
Once \(k\) is known, substitute either measurement back into \(A(t) = A_0 e^{kt}\) to solve for \(A_0 = A_1 e^{-k t_1}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: calibration-rule
label: Workflow
title: Two-point calibration and comparing rate conventions
tone: note

(1) Calibrating from two measurements \((t_1, A_1)\) and \((t_2, A_2)\) with \(A_1, A_2 > 0\) and \(t_1 \ne t_2\):
\[
k = \frac{\ln(A_2 / A_1)}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad A_0 = A_1 e^{-k t_1}.
\]
(2) Comparing models across conventions: Never compare a discrete rate \(r\), a continuous rate \(k\), and a doubling time \(T_d\) directly by their raw numbers. Convert every model to one shared metric—most cleanly the continuous rate \(k\) (using \(k = \ln(1 + r)\) or \(k = \frac{\ln 2}{T_d}\))—and compare the values of \(k\).
[/CALLOUT]

Worked example 1 (Calibrating from two non-zero times): A yeast culture contains \(300\) cells at \(t = 2\text{ hours}\) and \(1200\) cells at \(t = 5\text{ hours}\). Find the continuous growth rate \(k\), the doubling time \(T_d\), and the initial cell count \(A_0\).

Using [REF: calibration-rule.1 | the two-point calibration formula],
\[
k = \frac{\ln(1200 / 300)}{5 - 2} = \frac{\ln 4}{3} = \frac{2\ln 2}{3} \approx 0.4621\text{ h}^{-1}.
\]
Then the doubling time is
\[
T_d = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{\ln 2}{(2\ln 2)/3} = 1.5\text{ hours}.
\]
That makes immediate intuitive sense: quadrupling (two doublings) over a \(3\text{ hour}\) span means one doubling every \(1.5\text{ hours}\). To recover \(A_0\), substitute \((2, 300)\) into \(A(t) = A_0 \cdot 2^{t / 1.5}\):
\[
300 = A_0 \cdot 2^{2 / 1.5} = A_0 \cdot 2^{4/3} \implies A_0 = 300 \cdot 2^{-4/3} = 75 \cdot 2^{2/3} \approx 119\text{ cells}.
\]

Worked example 2 (Comparing rate conventions): Account X earns \(6.5\%\) per year compounded annually, while Account Y grows continuously at \(k_Y = 0.064\text{ yr}^{-1}\). Which account grows faster?

Convert Account X to its continuous rate via [REF: exp-model-forms.1 | the discrete-to-continuous bridge]:
\[
k_X = \ln(1.065) \approx 0.06297\text{ yr}^{-1}.
\]
Because \(k_Y = 0.064 > 0.06297 \approx k_X\), Account Y grows faster! Equivalently, Account Y's effective annual rate is \(r_Y = e^{0.064} - 1 \approx 6.609\% > 6.5\%\). Even though \(6.5\%\) looks larger than \(6.4\%\) at a glance, continuous compounding more than makes up the \(0.1\) percentage-point gap.

=== SECTION 3: Solving for elapsed time in applied models ===

Once a model is calibrated, the most common inverse question asks for the elapsed time \(t\) required to reach a target amount or threshold. Isolate the exponential factor first, then take \(\ln\) of both sides.

Application 1 (Radiocarbon dating): Living organic tissue maintains a steady fraction of radioactive carbon-14 (\(^{14}\text{C}\)), which decays with half-life \(T_{1/2} = 5730\text{ years}\) once the organism dies. Suppose a wooden beam from an archaeological site retains \(35\%\) of its original \(^{14}\text{C}\). How old is the beam?

Using the half-life form \(A(t) = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t / 5730}\), set \(A(t) = 0.35 A_0\):
\[
0.35 A_0 = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t / 5730} \implies 0.35 = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t / 5730}.
\]
Take \(\ln\) of both sides:
\[
\ln(0.35) = \frac{t}{5730}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\frac{t\ln 2}{5730} \implies t = 5730 \cdot \frac{-\ln(0.35)}{\ln 2} \approx 8679\text{ years}.
\]

Application 2 (Time to financial target): An investment of \(\$5000\) grows continuously at \(6\%\) per year, so \(A(t) = 5000 e^{0.06 t}\). How long does it take to triple to \(\$15{,}000\)?

Divide by \(5000\) first to obtain \(e^{0.06 t} = 3\). Taking \(\ln\) gives
\[
0.06 t = \ln 3 \implies t = \frac{\ln 3}{0.06} \approx 18.31\text{ years}.
\]

Application 3 (Drug clearance below a threshold): A patient receives a \(240\text{ mg}\) dose of a medication whose half-life in the bloodstream is \(6\text{ hours}\). When does the amount fall below \(15\text{ mg}\)?

Solve the boundary equation \(240 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/6} = 15\):
\[
\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/6} = \frac{15}{240} = \frac{1}{16} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 \implies \frac{t}{6} = 4 \implies t = 24\text{ hours}.
\]
Because the amount decreases strictly, the level stays below \(15\text{ mg}\) for all \(t > 24\text{ hours}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: time-solve-pitfall
label: Common Pitfall
title: Remaining fraction versus decayed fraction
tone: caution

(1) Exponential decay formulas track the REMAINING quantity \(A(t)\), not the amount lost. If a problem states that "\(75\%\) has decayed," the remaining fraction is \(100\% - 75\% = 25\% = \frac{1}{4}\), so set \(A(t) / A_0 = 0.25\) (never \(0.75\)).
(2) Decay is multiplicative, not linear: losing \(50\%\) in one half-life does not mean losing \(100\%\) in two half-lives. After \(n\) half-lives, \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n\) of the original quantity still remains.
[/CALLOUT]
