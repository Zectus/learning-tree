=== SECTION 1: Domain, vertical asymptote, and shifts of logarithmic graphs ===

For a transformed exponential function \(a \cdot b^{k(x - h)} + c\), the vertical shift \(c\) determines the horizontal asymptote \(y = c\), while horizontal shifts leave the asymptote untouched. Because logarithms are the mirror inverses of exponentials, those roles reverse completely for a transformed logarithmic function
\[
g(x) = a \cdot \log_b\!\bigl(k(x - h)\bigr) + c,
\]
where \(b > 0\), \(b \ne 1\), \(a \ne 0\), and \(k \ne 0\).

A logarithm is defined only when its argument is strictly positive:
\[
k(x - h) > 0.
\]
The boundary where the argument hits \(0\) is \(x = h\). As \(x\) approaches \(h\) from inside the domain, the argument approaches \(0^+\), so \(\log_b(k(x - h))\) plunges toward \(-\infty\) or climbs toward \(+\infty\). Thus the vertical line \(x = h\) is always the vertical asymptote. Meanwhile, adding \(c\) shifts the graph vertically, sliding the vertical line \(x = h\) along itself without changing its location. Because a parent logarithm already achieves every real output, scaling vertically by \(a \ne 0\) and shifting by \(c\) leaves the range equal to \((-\infty, \infty)\).

[CALLOUT]
id: log-sketch-method
label: Strategy
title: Anchor method for sketching transformed logarithms
tone: key

To sketch \(g(x) = a \cdot \log_b\!\bigl(k(x - h)\bigr) + c\):

(1) Solve \(k(x - h) > 0\) to find the domain: \((h, \infty)\) if \(k > 0\), or \((-\infty, h)\) if \(k < 0\). Draw the vertical asymptote \(x = h\) as a dashed line.
(2) Find two exact anchor points by solving \(k(x - h) = 1\) (which gives \(y = c\), since \(\log_b(1) = 0\)) and \(k(x - h) = b\) (which gives \(y = a + c\), since \(\log_b(b) = 1\)).
(3) Connect the anchor points with a smooth logarithmic curve that approaches \(x = h\) asymptotically at one end and extends slowly to \(\pm\infty\) across the rest of the domain.
[/CALLOUT]

Let us apply [REF: log-sketch-method | the anchor method] to \(g(x) = \log_2(x - h) + 1\) for varying horizontal shift \(h\). Here \(a = 1\), \(k = 1 > 0\), \(b = 2\), and \(c = 1\), so the domain is \((h, \infty)\) and the vertical asymptote is \(x = h\). Setting \(x - h = 1\) gives the anchor point \((h + 1, 1)\), and setting \(x - h = 2\) gives \((h + 2, 2)\).

=== SECTION 2: Reflections, negative arguments, and base inversion ===

Reflections behave symmetrically with what we saw for exponentials, with one important twist: horizontal reflection flips the domain to the left of the asymptote, while vertical reflection corresponds algebraically to inverting the base.

First, look at a horizontal reflection (\(k < 0\)), such as \(g(x) = \log_2(3 - x)\). Before reading off the horizontal shift, factor out the coefficient of \(x\):
\[
3 - x = -(x - 3).
\]
Here \(k = -1\) and \(h = 3\) (not \(-3\)!). The domain inequality \(3 - x > 0\) gives \(x < 3\), so the domain is \((-\infty, 3)\) and the vertical asymptote is \(x = 3\). Because the argument shrinks toward \(0^+\) as \(x\) approaches \(3\) from the left, the graph lies entirely to the left of \(x = 3\) and is strictly decreasing: setting \(3 - x = 1\) gives \((2, 0)\) and setting \(3 - x = 2\) gives \((1, 1)\).

Second, look at a vertical reflection across the \(x\)-axis: \(y = -\log_b(x)\). Multiply both sides by \(-1\) to write \(-y = \log_b(x)\), then convert to exponential form:
\[
x = b^{-y} = \left(\frac{1}{b}\right)^y \iff y = \log_{1/b}(x).
\]
This derives a clean bridge between vertical reflections and base inversion.

[CALLOUT]
id: log-base-flip
label: Identity
title: Reflection across the \(x\)-axis as base inversion
tone: key

For every valid base \(b > 0\), \(b \ne 1\), and every \(x > 0\):
\[
-\log_b(x) = \log_{1/b}(x).
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: log-base-flip | this identity] mirrors \(b^{-x} = (1/b)^x\): for exponentials, reflecting across the \(y\)-axis inverts the base; for logarithms, reflecting across the \(x\)-axis inverts the base!

=== SECTION 3: Exact algebraic inverses of transformed exponentials and logarithms ===

Because every transformed exponential \(f(x) = a \cdot b^{k(x - h)} + c\) is strictly monotonic, it has an explicit inverse—and that inverse is always a transformed logarithm. Conversely, the inverse of every transformed logarithm is a transformed exponential.

To compute the inverse algebraically, swap \(x\) and \(y\), isolate the exponential (or logarithmic) piece, and convert using the defining equivalence \(u = \log_b(v) \iff b^u = v\).

Worked example 1 (exponential to logarithm): Find the inverse of \(f(x) = 2 \cdot 3^{x - 1} - 4\).
1. Write \(y = 2 \cdot 3^{x - 1} - 4\) and swap \(x\) and \(y\):
\[
x = 2 \cdot 3^{y - 1} - 4.
\]
2. Isolate the exponential term \(3^{y - 1}\) by adding \(4\) and dividing by \(2\):
\[
\frac{x + 4}{2} = 3^{y - 1}.
\]
3. Convert to logarithmic form and solve for \(y\):
\[
y - 1 = \log_3\!\left(\frac{x + 4}{2}\right) \implies f^{-1}(x) = \log_3\!\left(\frac{x + 4}{2}\right) + 1.
\]

Look at how every geometric feature swaps between \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\):
- \(f\) has domain \((-\infty, \infty)\), range \((-4, \infty)\), and horizontal asymptote \(y = -4\).
- \(f^{-1}\) has domain \(\frac{x + 4}{2} > 0 \iff (-4, \infty)\), range \((-\infty, \infty)\), and vertical asymptote \(x = -4\).
- The point \((1, -2)\) on \(f\) reflects across \(y = x\) to \((-2, 1)\) on \(f^{-1}\).

Worked example 2 (logarithm to exponential): Find the inverse of \(g(x) = -2\log_5(3 - x) + 1\).
1. Swap \(x\) and \(y\): \(x = -2\log_5(3 - y) + 1\).
2. Isolate the logarithm:
\[
\log_5(3 - y) = \frac{x - 1}{-2} = \frac{1 - x}{2}.
\]
3. Convert to exponential form and solve for \(y\):
\[
3 - y = 5^{(1 - x)/2} \implies g^{-1}(x) = 3 - 5^{(1 - x)/2}.
\]
Again, the asymptotes and bounds match in exact mirror symmetry: \(g\) has vertical asymptote \(x = 3\) and domain \((-\infty, 3)\), while \(g^{-1}\) has horizontal asymptote \(y = 3\) and range \((-\infty, 3)\).

Finally, just as we reconstructed \(a \cdot b^x + c\) from a horizontal asymptote and two points, we can reconstruct \(g(x) = a\log_b(x - h) + c\) from its vertical asymptote \(x = h\) and two points. The asymptote gives \(h\) immediately; plugging in a point where \(x - h = 1\) gives \(c\) directly (because \(\log_b(1) = 0\)); and the second point then determines \(a\).
