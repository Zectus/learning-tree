=== SECTION 1: Periodic compound interest and effective annual yield ===

Suppose you deposit a principal \(P\) into an account paying a nominal annual interest rate \(r\) (written as a decimal, so \(12\%\) means \(r = 0.12\)). If interest is paid once per year, the balance after \(1\) year is \(P + rP = P(1 + r)\), and after \(t\) years of reinvesting the interest it is \(P(1 + r)^t\).

Banks, however, rarely wait a full year to credit interest. If the same nominal rate \(r\) is compounded \(n\) times per year—quarterly (\(n = 4\)), monthly (\(n = 12\)), or daily (\(n = 365\))—each compounding period earns a fraction \(r/n\) of the current balance. That multiplies the balance by \(1 + r/n\) at the end of every period. Over \(t\) years there are \(nt\) compounding periods, so repeated multiplicative growth produces the compound-interest formula.

[CALLOUT]
id: compound-formula
label: Formula
title: Discrete periodic compounding and effective annual yield
tone: key

For principal \(P > 0\), nominal annual rate \(r > 0\), and \(n\) compounding periods per year:

(1) The balance after \(t\) years is
\[
A(t) = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.
\]
(2) The effective annual yield (APY) is the equivalent once-per-year rate \(r_{\mathrm{eff}}\) giving the same annual growth:
\[
r_{\mathrm{eff}} = \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^n - 1.
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Where does [REF: compound-formula.2 | the APY formula] come from? Rewrite \(A(t)\) using the power-of-a-power law:
\[
A(t) = P\left[\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^n\right]^t = P(1 + r_{\mathrm{eff}})^t.
\]
The quantity inside the brackets, \((1 + r/n)^n\), is the exact annual growth factor. Subtracting \(1\) isolates the net percentage gain over one full year.

To see the effect of compounding frequency concretely, deposit \(P = \$1{,}000\) at nominal rate \(r = 12\% = 0.12\) for \(t = 1\) year:
- Annually (\(n = 1\)): \(1{,}000(1 + 0.12)^1 = \$1{,}120.00\) (APY \(12.00\%\)).
- Quarterly (\(n = 4\)): \(1{,}000(1 + 0.03)^4 \approx \$1{,}125.51\) (APY \(12.55\%\)).
- Monthly (\(n = 12\)): \(1{,}000(1 + 0.01)^{12} \approx \$1{,}126.83\) (APY \(12.68\%\)).

Because interest earned in earlier periods earns interest itself during later periods of the same year, increasing \(n\) raises the effective yield above the nominal rate \(r\). APY provides a single apples-to-apples yardstick for comparing two accounts with different nominal rates and compounding schedules.

=== SECTION 2: Euler's number \(e\) and continuous compounding ===

In the \(\$1{,}000\) example above, jumping from annual to quarterly compounding added \(\$5.51\), while jumping from quarterly to monthly added only \(\$1.32\). If we keep increasing \(n\)—compounding daily, hourly, every second, and beyond—does the balance grow without bound, or does it hit a finite ceiling?

To isolate the core behavior, set \(P = 1\), \(r = 1\) (\(100\%\) nominal annual rate), and \(t = 1\) year. The ending balance is
\[
\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n.
\]
Two opposing forces compete as \(n\) grows: the base \(1 + 1/n\) shrinks toward \(1\), while the exponent \(n\) grows toward \(\infty\). Computing values for larger and larger \(n\) reveals a striking stabilization:

[TABLE]
Compounding frequency \(n\) | Growth factor \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\)
\(1\) (annually) | \(2.000000\)
\(2\) (semiannually) | \(2.250000\)
\(4\) (quarterly) | \(2.441406\)
\(12\) (monthly) | \(2.613035\)
\(365\) (daily) | \(2.714567\)
\(1{,}000\) | \(2.716924\)
\(1{,}000{,}000\) | \(2.718280\)
[/TABLE]

Rather than diverging to \(\infty\), the sequence rises steadily and levels off against an exact irrational ceiling. We accept as a supplied mathematical fact (whose rigorous limit proof belongs to calculus and real analysis) that as \(n \to \infty\), \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\) converges to Euler's number, denoted \(e\):
\[
e \approx 2.718281828459\dots
\]

[/GRAPH]

Now apply this limit to an arbitrary nominal rate \(r > 0\) and time \(t\). To make the fraction \(r/n\) match the form \(1/m\), introduce the substitution \(m = n/r\), so that \(r/n = 1/m\) and \(n = mr\). Then the exponent \(nt\) becomes \(mrt\), and [REF: compound-formula.1 | the periodic compounding formula] rearranges to
\[
A(t) = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} = P\left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^{mrt} = P\left[\left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^m\right]^{rt}.
\]
Because \(r > 0\) is fixed, letting \(n \to \infty\) forces \(m = n/r \to \infty\). As a result, the bracketed base approaches \(e\), and we accept the continuous limit that the entire expression approaches \(P e^{rt}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: continuous-formula
label: Model
title: Continuous compounding
tone: key

When principal \(P > 0\) earns nominal annual interest rate \(r\) compounded continuously, the balance after \(t\) years is
\[
A(t) = P e^{rt}.
\]
Its effective annual growth factor is \(e^r\), and its effective annual yield is \(r_{\mathrm{eff}} = e^r - 1\).
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: continuous-formula | continuous compounding] turns the two-parameter discrete factor \((1 + r/n)^n\) into the single base \(e^r\). In a later lesson, once the natural logarithm is available, we will also solve \(A(t) = P e^{rt}\) for elapsed time \(t\) and convert any general exponential \(b^x\) into base \(e\).

=== SECTION 3: The natural exponential function \(e^x\) and discrete versus continuous growth ===

Because \(e \approx 2.71828\) satisfies \(2 < e < 3\), the natural exponential function \(f(x) = e^x\) is an ordinary exponential growth function sitting cleanly between \(2^x\) and \(3^x\). Every parent property from exponential functions applies directly to \(f(x) = e^x\):

- Domain: \((-\infty, \infty)\).
- Range: \((0, \infty)\).
- \(y\)-intercept: \((0, 1)\), with no \(x\)-intercept.
- Horizontal asymptote: \(y = 0\) as \(x \to -\infty\).
- Strict monotonicity: strictly increasing on \(\mathbb{R}\), hence one-to-one (\(e^{x_1} = e^{x_2} \implies x_1 = x_2\)).

Comparing \(2^x\), \(e^x\), and \(3^x\) requires care on the two sides of \(x = 0\). For \(x > 0\), larger bases yield larger outputs, so \(1 < 2^x < e^x < 3^x\). At \(x = 0\), all three curves cross at \((0, 1)\). For \(x < 0\), taking negative powers inverts the order: \(0 < 3^x < e^x < 2^x < 1\).

In financial and scientific modeling, comparing discrete periodic compounding with [REF: continuous-formula | continuous compounding] highlights how rapidly the discrete curve approaches its continuous upper limit. Consider \(P = \$5{,}000\) invested at nominal rate \(r = 6\% = 0.06\) for \(t = 5\) years:
- Annual compounding (\(n = 1\)): \(5{,}000(1.06)^5 \approx \$6{,}691.13\).
- Monthly compounding (\(n = 12\)): \(5{,}000(1.005)^{60} \approx \$6{,}744.25\).
- Daily compounding (\(n = 365\)): \(5{,}000(1 + 0.06/365)^{1825} \approx \$6{,}749.13\).
- Continuous compounding: \(5{,}000 e^{0.06 \cdot 5} = 5{,}000 e^{0.3} \approx \$6{,}749.29\).

For any fixed \(P > 0\), \(r > 0\), and \(t > 0\), continuous compounding \(P e^{rt}\) strictly exceeds every finite compounding frequency \(P(1 + r/n)^{nt}\), because \((1 + r/n)^n\) increases strictly toward \(e^r\) as \(n \to \infty\). At the same time, daily compounding already captures almost the entire gap between annual and continuous compounding—the difference over five years is just \(16\) cents on \(\$5{,}000\).
