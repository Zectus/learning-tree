=== SECTION 1: Straightening exponential curves on a semi-log plot ===

Suppose an experiment produces a table of positive measurements \((x, y)\) whose scatter plot bends sharply upward. Is the underlying relationship an exponential law \(y = a \cdot b^x\) or a power law \(y = a \cdot x^p\)? By eye alone, both curves look smoothly upward-bending and can appear nearly indistinguishable over a modest window. Yet our eyes—and linear algebra—are extraordinarily sharp at spotting a STRAIGHT LINE. By applying logarithms to one or both axes, we can straighten each model type into a linear equation \(Y = m X + c\), diagnose which model fits the data, and read its exact parameters off the slope and vertical intercept.

Start with an exponential relationship \(y = a \cdot b^x\) (with \(a > 0, b > 0\)). Taking the natural logarithm \(\ln\) of both sides and applying the product and power laws of logarithms gives
\[
\ln y = \ln(a \cdot b^x) = \ln a + \ln(b^x) = (\ln b)\, x + \ln a.
\]
If we set \(Y = \ln y\) while keeping the horizontal coordinate \(x\) untransformed, this equation takes the exact linear form
\[
Y = m x + c, \qquad \text{where } m = \ln b \text{ and } c = \ln a.
\]
A plot of \((x, \ln y)\) (or equivalently \((x, \log_{10} y)\)) is called a SEMI-LOG plot because only ONE axis (the vertical axis) is logarithmic. Every exponential function becomes a straight line on a semi-log plot, and back-transforming the line's slope \(m\) and vertical intercept \(c\) recovers the original parameters exactly:
\[
a = e^c \qquad \text{and} \qquad b = e^m \qquad (\text{so } y = e^c \cdot (e^m)^x = e^c e^{mx}).
\]
Notice that if you write the exponential model in continuous form \(y = a e^{kx}\), then \(\ln y = kx + \ln a\), so the semi-log slope equals \(k\) directly while \(a = e^c\). If base-10 logarithms are used instead (\(\log_{10} y = m x + c\)), the exact same derivation gives \(a = 10^c\) and \(b = 10^m\).

An exponential model \(y = a \cdot b^x\) (\(a > 0, b > 0\)) linearizes on a semi-log plot as follows:

(2) Back-transformation: Recover \(a = e^c\) and \(b = e^m\) (or \(y = e^c e^{mx}\) in continuous form).
(3) Base-10 variant: Plotting \(Y = \log_{10} y\) against \(x\) gives \(Y = m x + c\) with \(a = 10^c\) and \(b = 10^m\).

Worked example (Recovering an exponential formula from a semi-log line): Suppose a semi-log plot of \((x, \ln y)\) produces a straight line passing through \((0, 1.5)\) and \((4, 3.5)\). Find the exact equation for \(y\) in terms of \(x\).

1. Compute the slope and vertical intercept of the line \(Y = m x + c\):
\[
m = \frac{3.5 - 1.5}{4 - 0} = 0.5, \qquad c = 1.5.
\]
2. Back-transform using [REF: semilog-bridge.2 | the semi-log back-transformation]:
\[
a = e^{1.5} \approx 4.482, \qquad b = e^{0.5} \approx 1.649.
\]
Therefore \(y = e^{1.5} e^{0.5 x} \approx 4.482 (1.649)^x\). Resist the temptation to read \(c = 1.5\) directly as \(a\): at \(x = 0\), \(\ln y = 1.5\) means \(y = e^{1.5}\), not \(y = 1.5\)!

=== SECTION 2: Straightening power laws on a log-log plot ===

Why did \(y = 2 x^2\) remain curved on the semi-log plot? Because taking \(\ln\) of a power law \(y = a \cdot x^p\) (for \(a > 0\) and \(x > 0\)) produces a logarithm on BOTH sides:
\[
\ln y = \ln(a \cdot x^p) = \ln a + p \ln x.
\]
Now set \(X = \ln x\) AND \(Y = \ln y\). In the transformed coordinates \((X, Y)\), the equation becomes a straight line:
\[
Y = p X + \ln a.
\]
A plot of \((\ln x, \ln y)\) (or \((\log_{10} x, \log_{10} y)\)) is called a LOG-LOG plot because BOTH axes are logarithmic. Compare the parameter recovery carefully with semi-log:
- The slope \(m\) of the log-log line equals the exponent \(p\) DIRECTLY (\(p = m\))—do NOT exponentiate \(m\)! Notice also that \(p\) is identical whether you use \(\ln\) or \(\log_{10}\).
- The vertical intercept \(c\) (evaluated at \(X = 0\), which corresponds to \(x = 1\) because \(\ln 1 = 0\)) equals \(\ln a\) (or \(\log_{10} a\)), so you still back-transform \(a = e^c\) (or \(a = 10^c\) in base \(10\)).

Now compare the exact same two functions \(y = 2 x^2\) and \(y = 2(1.6)^x\) on log-log coordinates \((X, Y) = (\ln x, \ln y)\). Here \(x = e^X\), so the power law turns into the straight line \(Y = \ln 2 + 2 X\), while the exponential turns into the upward-curving graph \(Y = \ln 2 + \ln(1.6)\, e^X\).

[CALLOUT]
id: intercept-pitfall
label: Common Pitfall
title: Two high-frequency back-transformation traps
tone: caution

(1) Never read the vertical intercept \(c\) directly as \(a\): on BOTH semi-log and log-log plots, \(c = \ln a\) (or \(\log_{10} a\)), so \(a = e^c\) (or \(10^c\)). On a log-log plot, remember that the vertical axis \(X = 0\) corresponds to \(x = 1\), not \(x = 0\).
(2) Keep the slope roles distinct: on a semi-log plot, \(b = e^m\) (exponentiate the slope to get the base \(b\)); on a log-log plot, \(p = m\) directly (never compute \(e^m\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Worked example (Recovering a power law from a log-log line): Measurements of orbital period \(y\) versus orbital radius \(x\) produce a log-log line \((\ln x, \ln y)\) through \((0, \ln 5)\) and \((2, \ln 5 + 3)\). Find \(y\) as a function of \(x\).

The slope is
\[
p = m = \frac{(\ln 5 + 3) - \ln 5}{2 - 0} = \frac{3}{2},
\]
and the vertical intercept is \(c = \ln 5\), so \(a = e^{\ln 5} = 5\). By [REF: loglog-bridge.2 | the log-log recovery rule], \(y = 5 x^{3/2}\).

=== SECTION 3: Diagnosing exponential versus power-law behavior from tables ===

When handed a table of raw \((x, y)\) pairs, you can test linearity on semi-log and log-log axes directly by computing consecutive slopes:
- Semi-log slope test (for exponential \(y = a b^x\)): check whether \(\dfrac{\ln y_2 - \ln y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \dfrac{\ln(y_2 / y_1)}{x_2 - x_1}\) stays constant across all pairs of points. Equivalently, equal ADDITIVE steps in \(x\) produce equal MULTIPLICATIVE ratios in \(y\).
- Log-log slope test (for power law \(y = a x^p\)): check whether \(\dfrac{\ln y_2 - \ln y_1}{\ln x_2 - \ln x_1} = \dfrac{\ln(y_2 / y_1)}{\ln(x_2 / x_1)}\) stays constant across all pairs of points. Equivalently, equal MULTIPLICATIVE ratios in \(x\) produce equal MULTIPLICATIVE ratios in \(y\).

[TABLE]
Model | Linearizing plot | Linear equation | Slope \(m\) | Intercept \(c\) | Raw-data signature
Exponential \(y = a b^x\) | Semi-log \((x, \ln y)\) | \(\ln y = (\ln b) x + \ln a\) | \(\ln b\) (so \(b = e^m\)) | \(\ln a\) (so \(a = e^c\)) | Equal \(\Delta x \implies\) equal \(y\)-ratio
Power law \(y = a x^p\) | Log-log \((\ln x, \ln y)\) | \(\ln y = p \ln x + \ln a\) | \(p\) directly | \(\ln a\) (so \(a = e^c\)) | Equal \(x\)-ratio \(\implies\) equal \(y\)-ratio
[/TABLE]

Worked diagnostic comparison: Test the following two datasets to see which is exponential and which is a power law:
- Dataset 1: \((1, 4),\; (2, 16),\; (4, 64)\).
  - Semi-log slopes: from \(x = 1\) to \(2\), \(\frac{\ln(16/4)}{2 - 1} = \ln 4\); from \(x = 2\) to \(4\), \(\frac{\ln(64/16)}{4 - 2} = \frac{\ln 4}{2}\). Not constant—NOT exponential!
  - Log-log slopes: from \(x = 1\) to \(2\), \(\frac{\ln(16/4)}{\ln(2/1)} = \frac{\ln 4}{\ln 2} = 2\); from \(x = 2\) to \(4\), \(\frac{\ln(64/16)}{\ln(4/2)} = \frac{\ln 4}{\ln 2} = 2\). Constant! So \(p = 2\), and plugging \((1, 4)\) into \(y = a x^2\) gives \(a = 4\), hence \(y = 4 x^2\).
- Dataset 2: \((1, 6),\; (2, 18),\; (4, 162)\).
  - Semi-log slopes: from \(x = 1\) to \(2\), \(\frac{\ln(18/6)}{2 - 1} = \ln 3\); from \(x = 2\) to \(4\), \(\frac{\ln(162/18)}{4 - 2} = \frac{\ln 9}{2} = \ln 3\). Constant! So \(b = e^{\ln 3} = 3\), and plugging \((1, 6)\) into \(y = a \cdot 3^x\) gives \(a = 2\), hence \(y = 2 \cdot 3^x\).
