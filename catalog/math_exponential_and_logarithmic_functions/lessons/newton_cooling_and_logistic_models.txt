=== SECTION 1: Shifted exponential models and Newton's Law of Cooling ===

Unconstrained exponential decay \(A_0 e^{-kt}\) always approaches \(0\), yet many real processes settle toward a NONZERO equilibrium. A mug of \(90^\circ\text{C}\) coffee left in a \(22^\circ\text{C}\) room does not cool to \(0^\circ\text{C}\); it approaches the ambient room temperature \(22^\circ\text{C}\). Likewise, a cold \(4^\circ\text{C}\) drink in the same room warms upward toward \(22^\circ\text{C}\).

Exponential graph transformations immediately supply the right structure. Shifting an exponential decay curve vertically by an equilibrium value \(T_{\text{env}}\) moves its horizontal asymptote from \(y = 0\) to \(y = T_{\text{env}}\). Instead of the raw temperature \(T(t)\) decaying exponentially, it is the TEMPERATURE DIFFERENCE \(D(t) = T(t) - T_{\text{env}}\) that decays exponentially toward \(0\):
\[
D(t) = D_0 e^{-kt} = (T_0 - T_{\text{env}}) e^{-kt} \qquad (k > 0).
\]
Adding \(T_{\text{env}}\) to both sides produces Newton's Law of Cooling (and Warming):
\[
T(t) = T_{\text{env}} + (T_0 - T_{\text{env}}) e^{-kt}.
\]
Notice how the sign of the initial gap \((T_0 - T_{\text{env}})\) controls the shape:
- Cooling (\(T_0 > T_{\text{env}}\)): the coefficient \(T_0 - T_{\text{env}}\) is positive, so \(T(t)\) decreases from \(T_0\) toward the horizontal asymptote \(y = T_{\text{env}}\) from above.
- Warming or bounded saturation (\(T_0 < T_{\text{env}}\)): the coefficient is negative (a vertical reflection of decay), so \(T(t)\) increases from \(T_0\) toward \(y = T_{\text{env}}\) from below. In saturation contexts (such as capacitor charging or bounded skill acquisition toward a ceiling \(L\)), the same shifted exponential is often written as
\[
y(t) = L - (L - y_0) e^{-kt} \qquad (k > 0,\; y_0 < L).
\]

Worked example (Calibrating \(k\) and solving for time): Freshly brewed coffee starts at \(92^\circ\text{C}\) in a \(20^\circ\text{C}\) room. After \(10\text{ minutes}\), its temperature is \(56^\circ\text{C}\). How many total minutes after brewing will it take to reach \(38^\circ\text{C}\), and when will it reach \(30^\circ\text{C}\)?

1. Subtract \(T_{\text{env}} = 20\) to work with the decaying gap \(D(t) = T(t) - 20\):
\[
D_0 = 92 - 20 = 72^\circ\text{C}, \qquad T(t) = 20 + 72 e^{-kt}.
\]
2. Calibrate \(k\) using \(T(10) = 56\) (so \(D(10) = 56 - 20 = 36\)):
\[
72 e^{-10 k} = 36 \implies e^{-10 k} = \frac{1}{2} \implies k = \frac{\ln 2}{10} \approx 0.06931\text{ min}^{-1}.
\]
3. Target \(T(t) = 38\) gives gap \(D(t) = 38 - 20 = 18\):
\[
72 e^{-kt} = 18 \implies e^{-kt} = \frac{1}{4} \implies -kt = -\ln 4 = -2\ln 2 \implies t = \frac{2\ln 2}{(\ln 2)/10} = 20\text{ minutes}.
\]
4. Target \(T(t) = 30\) gives gap \(D(t) = 30 - 20 = 10\):
\[
72 e^{-kt} = 10 \implies e^{kt} = 7.2 \implies t = \frac{\ln(7.2)}{k} = 10 \cdot \frac{\ln(7.2)}{\ln 2} \approx 28.48\text{ minutes}.
\]
Notice why you must ALWAYS subtract \(T_{\text{env}}\) before taking ratios: \(T(t)\) itself is not purely exponential, only \(T(t) - T_{\text{env}}\) is.

=== SECTION 2: The logistic growth model and its S-shaped curve ===

Shifted exponential saturation slows down right from \(t = 0\). By contrast, many populations, epidemics, and technology adoptions begin with near-exponential growth when the population is small relative to available resources, then bend over and level off as crowding sets in. This S-shaped behavior is captured by the logistic growth model, whose explicit formula (derived from differential equations in calculus, and supplied here directly) is
\[
P(t) = \frac{L}{1 + A e^{-kt}} \qquad (L > 0,\; A > 0,\; k > 0).
\]

Every major feature of this S-curve follows directly from exponential algebra:

1. Carrying capacity (upper horizontal asymptote): As \(t \to \infty\), \(e^{-kt} \to 0\), so the denominator \(1 + A e^{-kt} \to 1\) and \(P(t) \to L\). Because \(A e^{-kt} > 0\), \(P(t)\) stays strictly below \(L\) for all finite \(t\). The ceiling \(L\) is called the carrying capacity. (As \(t \to -\infty\), \(e^{-kt} \to \infty\) and \(P(t) \to 0\), giving a lower horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\).)

2. Initial population \(P_0 = P(0)\) and the meaning of \(A\): Setting \(t = 0\) yields
\[
P_0 = \frac{L}{1 + A} \implies 1 + A = \frac{L}{P_0} \implies A = \frac{L - P_0}{P_0}.
\]
Thus \(A\) is the ratio of initially unused capacity \((L - P_0)\) to the starting population \(P_0\).

3. Early near-exponential phase: When \(P_0\) is very small compared with \(L\), \(A\) is large. For small \(t\) where \(A e^{-kt} \gg 1\), the "\(1+\)" in the denominator is negligible, so \(P(t) \approx \frac{L}{A} e^{kt} \approx P_0 e^{kt}\): the curve initially looks almost indistinguishable from unconstrained exponential growth at rate \(k\).

4. Half-capacity point (fastest growth): When does \(P(t)\) reach half the carrying capacity, \(P = \frac{L}{2}\)? Set the formula equal to \(\frac{L}{2}\):
\[
\frac{L}{1 + A e^{-kt}} = \frac{L}{2} \iff 1 + A e^{-kt} = 2 \iff A e^{-kt} = 1 \iff e^{kt} = A \iff t = \frac{\ln A}{k}.
\]
When \(P_0 < \frac{L}{2}\) (so \(A > 1\)), this time \(t = \frac{\ln A}{k} > 0\) marks the exact transition where the curve shifts from accelerating upward to decelerating toward \(L\)—the point of fastest growth (whose exact rate-of-change proof uses derivatives in calculus). If \(P_0 \ge \frac{L}{2}\), then \(A \le 1\) and growth slows down from \(t = 0\) onward.

=== SECTION 3: Calibrating logistic models and solving for time ===

To calibrate \(A\) and \(k\) when \(L\) is known, or to solve \(P(t) = P_{\text{target}}\) for elapsed time \(t\), isolate the exponential term \(A e^{-kt}\) cleanly:
\[
P = \frac{L}{1 + A e^{-kt}} \iff 1 + A e^{-kt} = \frac{L}{P} \iff A e^{-kt} = \frac{L - P}{P} \iff e^{-kt} = \frac{L - P}{A P}.
\]
Notice the structural elegance: although \(P(t)\) itself is not exponential, the ratio \(\frac{L - P(t)}{P(t)}\) (unused capacity per current individual) decays exponentially as \(A e^{-kt}\)!

Worked example (Calibrating and predicting): A lake has carrying capacity \(L = 800\) trout. Initially \(P(0) = 100\) trout are stocked, and after \(2\text{ years}\) the population reaches \(P(2) = 200\). Find \(A\) and \(k\), determine when the population reaches half capacity (\(400\) trout), and find when it reaches \(640\) trout (\(80\%\) of capacity).

1. Find \(A\) from [REF: logistic-features.2 | the initial condition]:
\[
A = \frac{800 - 100}{100} = 7, \qquad \text{so } P(t) = \frac{800}{1 + 7 e^{-kt}}.
\]
2. Calibrate \(k\) using \(P(2) = 200\):
\[
200 = \frac{800}{1 + 7 e^{-2k}} \implies 1 + 7 e^{-2k} = 4 \implies 7 e^{-2k} = 3 \implies e^{-2k} = \frac{3}{7}.
\]
Taking \(\ln\) gives \(-2k = \ln(3/7) = -\ln(7/3)\), so
\[
k = \frac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{7}{3}\right) \approx 0.4236\text{ yr}^{-1}.
\]
3. Time to reach half capacity \(P = 400\): By [REF: logistic-features.3 | the half-capacity formula],
\[
t = \frac{\ln 7}{k} = \frac{2\ln 7}{\ln(7/3)} \approx 4.59\text{ years}.
\]
4. Time to reach \(P = 640\):
\[
\frac{800}{1 + 7 e^{-kt}} = 640 \implies 1 + 7 e^{-kt} = \frac{5}{4} \implies 7 e^{-kt} = \frac{1}{4} \implies e^{kt} = 28.
\]
Hence
\[
t = \frac{\ln 28}{k} = \frac{2\ln 28}{\ln(7/3)} \approx 7.87\text{ years}.
\]
