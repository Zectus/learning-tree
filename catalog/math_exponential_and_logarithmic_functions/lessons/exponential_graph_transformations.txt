=== SECTION 1: Vertical shifts, reflections, and the horizontal asymptote ===

Parent exponential curves \(y = b^x\) always stay strictly above the \(x\)-axis and level off toward \(y = 0\). Yet many real and algebraic curves approach a nonzero ceiling or floor—such as a hot drink cooling toward room temperature, or a flipped exponential bounded above by \(y = 5\). By applying the standard function transformations to \(y = b^x\), we obtain the general transformed exponential function
\[
f(x) = a \cdot b^{k(x - h)} + c,
\]
where \(b > 0\), \(b \ne 1\), \(a \ne 0\), and \(k \ne 0\).

Start with vertical transformations alone: \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x + c\). Because \(b^x\) approaches \(0\) at one end of the real line (as \(x \to -\infty\) when \(b > 1\), or as \(x \to \infty\) when \(0 < b < 1\)), the scaled term \(a \cdot b^x\) also approaches \(0\) at that end. Adding \(c\) shifts every point on the graph vertically by \(c\) units, moving the horizontal asymptote from \(y = 0\) to \(y = c\).

The sign of \(a\) determines which side of the asymptote the graph occupies:
- When \(a > 0\), we have \(a \cdot b^x > 0\) for all \(x\), so \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x + c > c\). The graph lies strictly above the asymptote, and the range is \((c, \infty)\).
- When \(a < 0\), the graph reflects vertically across the asymptote's level before shifting: \(a \cdot b^x < 0\) for all \(x\), so \(f(x) < c\). The graph lies strictly below the asymptote, and the range is \((-\infty, c)\).

In every case, the domain remains \((-\infty, \infty)\), and the \(y\)-intercept is \(f(0) = a \cdot b^0 + c = a + c\)—not \((0, a)\) and not \((0, c)\).

To sketch any transformed exponential \(f(x) = a \cdot b^{k(x - h)} + c\) accurately, follow a three-step anchor method:

[CALLOUT]
id: sketch-method
label: Strategy
title: Anchor method for sketching transformed exponentials
tone: key

To sketch \(f(x) = a \cdot b^{k(x - h)} + c\):

(1) Draw the horizontal asymptote \(y = c\) as a dashed line. This immediately fixes the boundary of the range: \((c, \infty)\) if \(a > 0\), or \((-\infty, c)\) if \(a < 0\).
(2) Plot two clean anchor points by choosing \(x\)-values that make the exponent \(k(x - h)\) equal to \(0\) and \(1\) (giving \(y = a + c\) and \(y = ab + c\)).
(3) Connect the points with a smooth exponential curve that hugs \(y = c\) on the decaying end and grows without bound away from \(y = c\) on the other end.
[/CALLOUT]

Notice how [REF: sketch-method.2 | step (2)] avoids messy decimals. For \(f(x) = -2^x + 3\), choosing \(x = 0\) gives \((0, -1 + 3) = (0, 2)\) and choosing \(x = 1\) gives \((1, -2 + 3) = (1, 1)\). Since \(y = 3\) is the asymptote as \(x \to -\infty\), the curve stays just under \(y = 3\) on the far left and drops rapidly downward through \((0, 2)\) and \((1, 1)\).

=== SECTION 2: Horizontal transformations and algebraic equivalence ===

Horizontal reflections, horizontal stretches, and horizontal shifts interact with exponential laws in a remarkable way that has no counterpart in polynomials or radical functions.

First, look at a reflection across the \(y\)-axis, replacing \(x\) by \(-x\):
\[
b^{-x} = (b^{-1})^x = \left(\frac{1}{b}\right)^x.
\]
Reflecting a growth curve \(y = 2^x\) across the \(y\)-axis produces the decay curve \(y = 2^{-x} = (1/2)^x\), and reflecting a decay curve across the \(y\)-axis produces a growth curve. More generally, a horizontal scaling factor \(k\) inside the exponent simply changes the effective base to \(B = b^k\):
\[
b^{kx} = (b^k)^x.
\]
For example, \(2^{3x} = (2^3)^x = 8^x\) and \(9^{-x/2} = (9^{-1/2})^x = (1/3)^x\).

Second, look at a horizontal shift \(x \mapsto x - h\):
\[
b^{x - h} = b^{-h} \cdot b^x.
\]
Read that equation geometrically. Shifting the graph of \(y = b^x\) to the right by \(h\) units produces the exact same curve as scaling \(y = b^x\) vertically by the factor \(b^{-h}\)! For instance, shifting \(y = 2^x\) right by \(3\) units gives \(y = 2^{x - 3} = \frac{1}{8} \cdot 2^x\), which is identical to compressing \(y = 2^x\) vertically by a factor of \(8\).

Drag the slider \(h\) in the plot above: the point \((0, 1)\) slides horizontally to \((h, 1)\), while simultaneously the \(y\)-intercept scales vertically from \(1\) to \(2^{-h}\). Because this identity holds for every real \(h\) and \(k\), any general transformed exponential can be collapsed into a form without an explicit horizontal shift or horizontal scaling:

[CALLOUT]
id: horiz-equiv
label: Identity
title: Collapsing horizontal transformations
tone: key

Every transformed exponential function \(f(x) = a \cdot b^{k(x - h)} + c\) can be rewritten as
\[
f(x) = A \cdot B^x + c,
\]
where \(A = a \cdot b^{-kh}\) and \(B = b^k > 0\).
[/CALLOUT]

To check [REF: horiz-equiv | this equivalence] on an example, take \(f(x) = 3 \cdot 4^{2(x - 1)} - 7\). Distribute the exponent: \(4^{2x - 2} = 4^{-2} \cdot (4^2)^x = \frac{1}{16} \cdot 16^x\). Multiplying by \(3\) gives \(f(x) = \frac{3}{16} \cdot 16^x - 7\). Note that horizontal shifts and scalings never move the horizontal asymptote \(y = c\).

=== SECTION 3: Diagnosing monotonicity and reconstructing formulas ===

When both \(a\) and \(k\) can be negative, deciding whether \(f(x) = a \cdot b^{k(x - h)} + c\) is increasing or decreasing requires tracking both the effective base \(B = b^k\) and the sign of \(a\). First rewrite the exponential factor using [REF: horiz-equiv | the collapsed form] \(A \cdot B^x + c\) (noting that \(A = a \cdot b^{-kh}\) always has the exact same sign as \(a\), because \(b^{-kh} > 0\)). Then combine the behavior of \(B^x\) with the sign of \(a\):

[TABLE]
Sign of \(a\) | Effective base \(B = b^k\) | Monotonicity of \(f\) | End behavior near asymptote \(y = c\)
\(a > 0\) | \(B > 1\) | Strictly increasing | \(f(x) \to c\) from above as \(x \to -\infty\)
\(a > 0\) | \(0 < B < 1\) | Strictly decreasing | \(f(x) \to c\) from above as \(x \to \infty\)
\(a < 0\) | \(B > 1\) | Strictly decreasing | \(f(x) \to c\) from below as \(x \to -\infty\)
\(a < 0\) | \(0 < B < 1\) | Strictly increasing | \(f(x) \to c\) from below as \(x \to \infty\)
[/TABLE]

Pay special attention to the fourth row: when both \(a < 0\) and \(0 < B < 1\), two reversals cancel to produce a strictly increasing function! For instance, in \(g(x) = -2 \cdot 3^{-x} + 4 = -2 \cdot (1/3)^x + 4\), the factor \((1/3)^x\) decreases toward \(0\) as \(x \to \infty\); multiplying by \(-2\) flips it to negative values that rise toward \(0\); adding \(4\) makes \(g(x)\) increase smoothly from \(-\infty\) up toward the horizontal ceiling \(y = 4\).

Now reverse the graphing problem: suppose you are given a graph showing the horizontal asymptote \(y = c\) and two points \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) on the curve, and you want to write the formula \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x + c\). A common mistake is to divide \(y_2 / y_1\) directly. That fails whenever \(c \ne 0\), because the constant \(c\) prevents \(a\) from canceling:
\[
\frac{a b^{x_2} + c}{a b^{x_1} + c} \ne b^{x_2 - x_1}.
\]
Instead, subtract the asymptote \(c\) from every \(y\)-coordinate first. The vertical offsets \(y_1 - c = a b^{x_1}\) and \(y_2 - c = a b^{x_2}\) form an unshifted exponential, so their ratio cancels \(a\) cleanly:
\[
\frac{y_2 - c}{y_1 - c} = b^{x_2 - x_1}.
\]

Let us apply this to a graph with horizontal asymptote \(y = 4\) passing through \((0, 1)\) and \((2, -8)\).

1. Identify \(c = 4\) from the asymptote.
2. Subtract \(c = 4\) from both \(y\)-values: \(y_1 - 4 = 1 - 4 = -3\) and \(y_2 - 4 = -8 - 4 = -12\).
3. Divide the shifted outputs to find \(b\):
\[
\frac{-12}{-3} = b^{2 - 0} \implies 4 = b^2 \implies b = 2,
\]
taking the positive root since \(b > 0\).
4. Find \(a\) using \((0, 1)\): \(1 - 4 = a \cdot 2^0 \implies a = -3\).

Thus \(f(x) = -3 \cdot 2^x + 4\). Checking the second point confirms \(f(2) = -3(4) + 4 = -8\).
