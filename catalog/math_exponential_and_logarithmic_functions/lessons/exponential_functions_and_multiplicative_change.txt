=== SECTION 1: Multiplicative change and exponential functions ===

Suppose a biologist tracks two laboratory populations starting at \(200\) cells at time \(x = 0\) hours. Population A gains \(400\) cells every hour, whereas Population B triples in size every hour. After one hour both populations stand at \(600\) cells, so a single snapshot cannot tell them apart. Yet by hour \(4\), Population A has grown to \(1{,}800\) cells while Population B has surged to \(16{,}200\) cells. The difference lies in how each quantity changes over a fixed step: Population A grows by a constant additive amount each hour, while Population B grows by a constant multiplicative factor.

[TABLE]
Hour \(x\) | Population A: \(g(x) = 200 + 400x\) | Step difference \(g(x) - g(x - 1)\) | Population B: \(f(x) = 200 \cdot 3^x\) | Step ratio \(f(x) / f(x - 1)\)
\(0\) | \(200\) | — | \(200\) | —
\(1\) | \(600\) | \(+400\) | \(600\) | \(\times 3\)
\(2\) | \(1{,}000\) | \(+400\) | \(1{,}800\) | \(\times 3\)
\(3\) | \(1{,}400\) | \(+400\) | \(5{,}400\) | \(\times 3\)
\(4\) | \(1{,}800\) | \(+400\) | \(16{,}200\) | \(\times 3\)
[/TABLE]

In a linear function \(g(x) = mx + c\), increasing \(x\) by \(1\) always adds the same constant \(m\): \(g(x + 1) - g(x) = m\). In an exponential function \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\), increasing \(x\) by \(1\) always multiplies the output by the same constant \(b\):
\[
\frac{f(x + 1)}{f(x)} = \frac{a \cdot b^{x+1}}{a \cdot b^x} = b.
\]
More generally, advancing the input by any step \(\Delta x\) multiplies the output by \(b^{\Delta x}\), regardless of where \(x\) starts:
\[
\frac{f(x + \Delta x)}{f(x)} = \frac{a \cdot b^{x + \Delta x}}{a \cdot b^x} = b^{\Delta x}.
\]
Because the variable \(x\) appears in the exponent rather than the base, exponential functions behave very differently from power functions such as \(x^3\). In \(x^3\), the exponent is fixed and the base varies; in \(3^x\), the base is fixed and the exponent varies.

[CALLOUT]
id: exp-def
label: Definition
title: Exponential function
tone: key

An exponential function has the form
\[
f(x) = a \cdot b^x,
\]
where:

(1) \(a \ne 0\) is the initial value (the output at \(x = 0\), since \(f(0) = a \cdot b^0 = a\)).
(2) \(b > 0\) and \(b \ne 1\) is the base (also called the growth or decay factor per unit increase in \(x\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Why do we restrict the base \(b\) to \(b > 0\) and \(b \ne 1\) in [REF: exp-def.2 | condition (2)]? Each excluded case breaks the behavior we need from a real-valued function defined for every real number \(x\):

- If \(b < 0\), expressions like \((-2)^x\) fail to be real numbers at infinitely many rational exponents: for instance, \((-2)^{1/2} = \sqrt{-2}\) is not a real number, and even where rational powers exist (such as \((-2)^{1/3} = -\sqrt[3]{2}\) and \((-2)^{2/3} = \sqrt[3]{4}\)), the sign flips erratically.
- If \(b = 0\), then \(0^x = 0\) for \(x > 0\), while \(0^0\) is indeterminate and \(0^{-1} = 1/0\) is undefined.
- If \(b = 1\), then \(f(x) = a \cdot 1^x = a\) is a constant function with no growth or decay, and every input produces the same output.

From prerequisite exponent laws, \(b^x\) already has an exact algebraic meaning whenever \(x\) is an integer or a rational number \(p/q\) (with \(q > 0\)):
\[
b^0 = 1, \qquad b^{-n} = \frac{1}{b^n}, \qquad b^{p/q} = \left(\sqrt[q]{b}\right)^p.
\]
What about irrational exponents such as \(2^{\sqrt{2}}\) or \(3^\pi\)? Because \(\sqrt{2} = 1.4142135\dots\) lies between closer and closer rational bounds—such as \(1.41 < \sqrt{2} < 1.42\), and even tighter, \(1.4142 < \sqrt{2} < 1.4143\)—the corresponding rational powers pinch down on a single real number:
\[
2^{1.4142} \approx 2.66512 < 2^{\sqrt{2}} < 2.66530 \approx 2^{1.4143}.
\]
In this lesson, we accept as a supplied mathematical fact (whose formal limit construction belongs to real analysis) that for every \(b > 0\), \(b^x\) extends uniquely from the rational numbers to all real numbers \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) as a continuous function that preserves all the familiar exponent laws: \(b^{r+s} = b^r b^s\), \(b^{r-s} = b^r / b^s\), and \((b^r)^s = b^{rs}\) for all \(r, s \in \mathbb{R}\). Moreover, this extension preserves strict order: if \(b > 1\) and \(r < s\), then \(b^r < b^s\); if \(0 < b < 1\) and \(r < s\), then \(b^r > b^s\).

=== SECTION 2: Exponential growth, decay, and parent graphs ===

Because \(b > 0\) and \(b \ne 1\), every valid base falls into one of two regimes: \(b > 1\) or \(0 < b < 1\). To see how the base controls the shape of the graph, compare the parent functions \(f(x) = b^x\) (where \(a = 1\)) for \(b = 3\), \(b = 2\), and \(b = 1/2\).

When \(b > 1\), multiplying repeatedly by \(b\) makes \(b^x\) grow without bound as \(x\) moves to the right (\(x \to \infty\)). Moving to the left (\(x \to -\infty\)) means multiplying by negative powers \(b^{-n} = 1/b^n\), which shrinks toward \(0\) without ever reaching \(0\) or turning negative. For example, \(2^{-1} = 1/2\), \(2^{-5} = 1/32\), and \(2^{-10} = 1/1024\). Thus the \(x\)-axis (\(y = 0\)) acts as a horizontal asymptote as \(x \to -\infty\). This is exponential growth.

When \(0 < b < 1\), the roles of left and right reverse. For \(b = 1/2\), increasing \(x\) halves the output at each step: \((1/2)^1 = 1/2\), \((1/2)^2 = 1/4\), \((1/2)^{10} = 1/1024\), so the curve approaches the horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\) as \(x \to \infty\). Moving to the left raises \(1/2\) to negative powers—\((1/2)^{-1} = 2\), \((1/2)^{-3} = 8\)—so the output grows without bound as \(x \to -\infty\). This is exponential decay.

Notice two subtle features in the graph above. First, every parent curve \(y = b^x\) passes through the exact same \(y\)-intercept \((0, 1)\), because \(b^0 = 1\) for all \(b > 0\). Second, compare \(y = 3^x\) and \(y = 2^x\): for \(x > 0\), the larger base rises faster (\(3^x > 2^x\)), but for \(x < 0\), the inequality reverses (\(3^x < 2^x\), since \(1/3^n < 1/2^n\)). Dragging the slider \(b\) past \(b = 1\) shows the curve pivoting around \((0, 1)\) as it transitions from growth to decay.

Multiplying \(b^x\) by a nonzero constant \(a\) scales every output vertically and sets the \(y\)-intercept to \((0, a)\). Because \(b^x > 0\) for every real number \(x\), the sign of \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\) is locked to the sign of \(a\): if \(a > 0\), every output is strictly positive; if \(a < 0\), every output is strictly negative.

[CALLOUT]
id: exp-features
label: Summary
title: Key properties of \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\) (with \(a > 0\))
tone: key

For \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\) with \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), and \(b \ne 1\):

(1) Domain: all real numbers, \((-\infty, \infty)\).
(2) Range: all positive real numbers, \((0, \infty)\). (If \(a < 0\), the range is \((-\infty, 0)\).)
(3) Intercepts: \(y\)-intercept at \((0, a)\), and no \(x\)-intercept, since \(a \cdot b^x \ne 0\) for all \(x\).
(4) Horizontal asymptote: the line \(y = 0\), approached as \(x \to -\infty\) when \(b > 1\) and as \(x \to \infty\) when \(0 < b < 1\).
(5) Strict monotonicity and injectivity: \(f\) is strictly increasing when \(b > 1\) and strictly decreasing when \(0 < b < 1\). Consequently, \(f\) is one-to-one: \(a \cdot b^{x_1} = a \cdot b^{x_2}\) implies \(x_1 = x_2\).
[/CALLOUT]

The one-to-one property in [REF: exp-features.5 | property (5)] is especially powerful: whenever two powers with the same valid base are equal, their exponents must be equal. For instance, if \(2^{x+1} = 8 = 2^3\), injectivity immediately forces \(x + 1 = 3\), so \(x = 2\).

=== SECTION 3: Constructing \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\) from two points ===

Just as two points with distinct \(x\)-coordinates determine a unique line \(g(x) = mx + c\), any two points \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) with \(x_1 \ne x_2\) and \(y_1, y_2\) sharing the same nonzero sign determine a unique exponential function \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\). (If \(y_1 = y_2\), the unique formula has \(b = 1\), which is constant rather than exponential.)

Why is the same-sign requirement essential? Since \(b > 0\), we always have \(b^x > 0\), so \(y_1 = a b^{x_1}\) and \(y_2 = a b^{x_2}\) must both have the sign of \(a\). If one point lies above the \(x\)-axis and the other lies on or below it, no function of the form \(a \cdot b^x\) can pass through both.

To find \(a\) and \(b\) when both points are valid, we cannot subtract the two equations to eliminate \(a\)—that works for linear functions, where the intercept is added. Because \(a\) multiplies \(b^x\), we divide the two equations instead:
\[
\frac{y_2}{y_1} = \frac{a \cdot b^{x_2}}{a \cdot b^{x_1}} = b^{x_2 - x_1}.
\]
Because \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) have the same nonzero sign, the ratio \(y_2 / y_1\) is strictly positive. Raising both sides to the power \(\frac{1}{x_2 - x_1}\) isolates the unique positive base:
\[
b = \left(\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right)^{\frac{1}{x_2 - x_1}}.
\]
Once \(b\) is known, substitute either point back into \(y_1 = a \cdot b^{x_1}\) to solve for \(a = y_1 / b^{x_1}\).

Let us work through two examples. First, find the exponential function passing through \((1, 6)\) and \((4, 162)\). Dividing the second output by the first eliminates \(a\):
\[
\frac{162}{6} = \frac{a \cdot b^4}{a \cdot b^1} \implies 27 = b^3 \implies b = 27^{1/3} = 3.
\]
Substituting \(b = 3\) and the point \((1, 6)\) into \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\) gives \(6 = a \cdot 3^1\), so \(a = 2\). The formula is \(f(x) = 2 \cdot 3^x\). As a quick check, \(f(4) = 2 \cdot 3^4 = 2 \cdot 81 = 162\).

Next, find the exponential function passing through \((-1, 20)\) and \((2, 5/2)\). Be careful with the sign when subtracting \(x\)-coordinates: \(x_2 - x_1 = 2 - (-1) = 3\). Dividing gives
\[
\frac{5/2}{20} = \frac{a \cdot b^2}{a \cdot b^{-1}} \implies \frac{1}{8} = b^3 \implies b = \left(\frac{1}{8}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{1}{2}.
\]
Using \((-1, 20)\) to find \(a\):
\[
20 = a \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1} = 2a \implies a = 10.
\]
Hence \(f(x) = 10 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^x\). Notice that even when \(x = 0\) is not one of the given points, the method recovers the initial value \(a = 10\) directly.

What if the exponent difference \(x_2 - x_1\) is even, say \(b^2 = 9\)? Algebraically, \(b^2 = 9\) has two solutions, \(b = 3\) and \(b = -3\), but [REF: exp-def.2 | the definition of an exponential function] requires \(b > 0\), so we always discard the negative root and take \(b = 3\).
