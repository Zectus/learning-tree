=== SECTION 1: Two-way contingency tables, joint, marginal, and conditional proportions ===

When every observational unit in a dataset carries two categorical variables—such as a commuter's transit mode and whether they arrived on time, or a patient's treatment protocol and recovery outcome—means and standard deviations do not apply. Instead, we organize all counts into a two-way contingency table (also called a cross-tabulation) with \(r\) rows for the categories of one variable and \(c\) columns for the categories of the other.

Suppose an online course platform tracks \(n = 400\) enrolled learners across two categorical variables: Study Track (Self-paced versus Cohort) and Completion Status (Completed versus Dropped). The raw counts form a \(2 \times 2\) contingency table with row totals and column totals written along the margins:

[TABLE]
Study Track | Completed | Dropped | Row total
Self-paced | 120 | 180 | 300
Cohort | 70 | 30 | 100
Column total | 190 | 210 | 400
[/TABLE]

Raw counts alone can mislead when group sizes differ: \(120\) Self-paced learners completed the course versus \(70\) Cohort learners, yet Self-paced had three times as many enrollees (\(300\) versus \(100\)). Turning counts into proportions requires deciding WHICH total belongs in the denominator. Three distinct families of proportions answer three very different questions.

[CALLOUT]
id: contingency-proportions
label: Definition
title: Joint, marginal, and conditional relative frequencies
tone: key

In an \(r \times c\) contingency table of \(n\) observations, let \(n_{ij}\) be the count in row \(i\) and column \(j\), \(R_i = \sum_{j=1}^c n_{ij}\) the \(i\)-th row total, and \(C_j = \sum_{i=1}^r n_{ij}\) the \(j\)-th column total (so \(\sum_i R_i = \sum_j C_j = n\)).

(1) Joint relative frequency: \(p_{ij} = \dfrac{n_{ij}}{n}\) is the proportion of the ENTIRE dataset falling in both row \(i\) and column \(j\). All \(r c\) joint proportions sum to \(1\).
(2) Marginal distributions: the row marginal proportions \(p_{i\bullet} = \dfrac{R_i}{n}\) summarize the row variable alone (summing to \(1\) across rows), and the column marginal proportions \(p_{\bullet j} = \dfrac{C_j}{n}\) summarize the column variable alone (summing to \(1\) across columns).
(3) Conditional distributions: conditioning on row \(i\) divides each cell in row \(i\) by its row total \(R_i\), giving the empirical conditional proportion \(P(\text{Col } j \mid \text{Row } i) = \dfrac{n_{ij}}{R_i}\) (each row sums to \(1\)). Conditioning on column \(j\) divides each cell in column \(j\) by its column total \(C_j\), giving \(P(\text{Row } i \mid \text{Col } j) = \dfrac{n_{ij}}{C_j}\) (each column sums to \(1\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Never confuse \(P(A \mid B)\) with \(P(B \mid A)\)! In our course table, look closely at the single cell "Cohort and Completed" (\(n_{21} = 70\)):
- Joint proportion: \(70 / 400 = 0.175\) (\(17.5\%\) of ALL learners were Cohort completers).
- Row-conditioned proportion \(P(\text{Completed} \mid \text{Cohort}) = 70 / 100 = 0.70\) (\(70\%\) of Cohort learners completed the course).
- Column-conditioned proportion \(P(\text{Cohort} \mid \text{Completed}) = 70 / 190 \approx 0.368\) (\(36.8\%\) of all completers came from the Cohort track).

Notice how changing the conditioning event changes the denominator from \(100\) to \(190\) and flips the percentage from \(70\%\) to \(36.8\%\). Always identify the conditioning group ("of the Cohort learners..." versus "of the completers...") FIRST, and place THAT group's total in the denominator.

=== SECTION 2: Visualizing categorical association, independence, and 2x2 summaries ===

To see whether two categorical variables are associated in our dataset, compare the conditional distributions across the groups of the explanatory variable. In the course table above, computing the row-conditioned completion distribution for each Study Track gives:
- Self-paced (\(R_1 = 300\)): \(120 / 300 = 40\%\) Completed, \(180 / 300 = 60\%\) Dropped.
- Cohort (\(R_2 = 100\)): \(70 / 100 = 70\%\) Completed, \(30 / 100 = 30\%\) Dropped.

A \(100\%\) stacked bar chart (or a side-by-side bar chart of conditional percentages) displays these conditional distributions directly: every conditioning group gets a full bar of height \(100\%\), partitioned into its outcome percentages. If the two bars have identical splits, the variables are unassociated; when the splits differ visibly—\(40\%\) versus \(70\%\) completed—there is a clear descriptive association.

This comparison defines descriptive independence and association precisely.

[CALLOUT]
id: descriptive-independence
label: Definition
title: Descriptive independence versus association
tone: key

Two categorical variables in a contingency table are descriptively independent if the conditional distribution of one variable is IDENTICAL across every category of the other variable:
\[
\frac{n_{ij}}{R_i} = \frac{C_j}{n} \quad \text{for every row } i \text{ and column } j.
\]
Equivalently, every joint relative frequency equals the product of its row and column marginal proportions, \(p_{ij} = p_{i\bullet}\, p_{\bullet j}\), and every cell count matches \(n_{ij} = R_i C_j / n\). Whenever conditional distributions differ across groups, the two variables exhibit descriptive association in the observed dataset.
[/CALLOUT]

Notice a key scope boundary: in descriptive statistics, [REF: descriptive-independence | descriptive independence] checks whether the observed table's conditional percentages match exactly. Real finite samples rarely match to the last decimal even when two variables are unrelated in a larger population; formal probability axioms and chi-square hypothesis tests for deciding whether an observed association exceeds random sampling noise belong to Probability and Statistical Inference.

When both variables are binary (a \(2 \times 2\) table), two standard numerical summaries quantify the strength and direction of a descriptive association between Group 1 (with outcome proportion \(p_1\)) and Group 2 (with outcome proportion \(p_2 > 0\)):
- Proportion difference (also called risk difference):
\[
\Delta p = p_1 - p_2.
\]
It measures the absolute gap in percentage points. For our course tracks, \(\Delta p = 0.70 - 0.40 = +0.30\) (Cohort completion is \(30\) percentage points higher than Self-paced).
- Descriptive relative risk (ratio of proportions):
\[
\text{RR} = \frac{p_1}{p_2}.
\]
It measures the multiplicative ratio of rates. For our course tracks, \(\text{RR} = 0.70 / 0.40 = 1.75\) (Cohort learners completed at \(1.75\) times the rate of Self-paced learners). Under [REF: descriptive-independence | descriptive independence], \(p_1 = p_2\), so \(\Delta p = 0\) and \(\text{RR} = 1\).

Always report BOTH \(\Delta p\) and \(\text{RR}\) (or the two baseline proportions \(p_1\) and \(p_2\)) together. Saying "risk doubled" (\(\text{RR} = 2.0\)) sounds dramatic whether a rate rises from \(0.1\%\) to \(0.2\%\) (\(\Delta p = 0.001\)) or from \(25\%\) to \(50\%\) (\(\Delta p = 0.25\)), yet the practical impact differs by a factor of \(250\).

=== SECTION 3: Confounding variables and Simpson's paradox ===

Suppose a hospital compares two surgical protocols—Protocol A and Protocol B—across \(n = 1{,}000\) patients (\(500\) treated with each protocol) and publishes the following pooled \(2 \times 2\) table:

[TABLE]
Pooled table (all patients) | Recovered | Did not recover | Total | Recovery rate
Protocol A | 330 | 170 | 500 | 66.0%
Protocol B | 390 | 110 | 500 | 78.0%
[/TABLE]

At first glance, Protocol B looks far superior: \(78\%\) recovery versus \(66\%\), a \(12\)-percentage-point lead. Now suppose a clinician stratifies the exact same \(1{,}000\) patients by initial case severity (Mild versus Severe), splitting the single table into two stratified sub-tables:

[TABLE]
Stratum and Protocol | Recovered | Did not recover | Total | Recovery rate
Mild: Protocol A | 90 | 10 | 100 | 90.0%
Mild: Protocol B | 340 | 60 | 400 | 85.0%
Severe: Protocol A | 240 | 160 | 400 | 60.0%
Severe: Protocol B | 50 | 50 | 100 | 50.0%
[/TABLE]

Check the totals first: for Protocol A, \(90 + 240 = 330\) recovered out of \(100 + 400 = 500\) (\(66.0\%\)); for Protocol B, \(340 + 50 = 390\) recovered out of \(400 + 100 = 500\) (\(78.0\%\)). Not a single patient was added, dropped, or miscounted! Yet look at the comparison inside EVERY stratum:
- Among Mild patients, Protocol A WINS: \(90.0\% > 85.0\%\).
- Among Severe patients, Protocol A WINS AGAIN: \(60.0\% > 50.0\%\).
- Yet in the pooled table, Protocol B appears to win: \(78.0\% > 66.0\%\)!

How can a protocol perform BETTER on Mild cases AND BETTER on Severe cases, yet look WORSE overall? The mathematics is simply a weighted average of subgroup rates! Letting \(w\) denote the fraction of a protocol's patients who are Severe (so \(1 - w\) are Mild):
\[
\text{Pooled rate}_A = (1 - w_A)(90\%) + w_A(60\%) = 90\% - 30\%\,w_A,
\]
\[
\text{Pooled rate}_B = (1 - w_B)(85\%) + w_B(50\%) = 85\% - 35\%\,w_B.
\]
Because doctors assigned \(w_A = 400/500 = 80\%\) of Protocol A's patients from the high-risk Severe group (giving \(90\% - 30\%(0.80) = 66\%\)), while assigning only \(w_B = 100/500 = 20\%\) of Protocol B's patients from the Severe group (giving \(85\% - 35\%(0.20) = 78\%\)), the pooled rates compare A's mostly Severe caseload against B's mostly Mild caseload!

The graph below plots both pooled recovery lines as functions of the Severe-case share \(w\). Protocol A's line sits strictly ABOVE Protocol B's line at every single value of \(w \in [0, 1]\), yet evaluating A at \(w = 0.80\) and B at \(w = 0.20\) places B's pooled point higher vertically than A's.

[CALLOUT]
id: simpsons-paradox
label: Principle
title: Simpson's paradox and confounding
tone: caution

Simpson's paradox occurs when the direction of an association between two categorical variables reverses (or disappears) after aggregating data across strata of a third variable. Two conditions together produce the reversal:

(1) The third variable (a lurking or confounding variable) is strongly associated with the outcome (here, Mild patients recover at far higher rates than Severe patients).
(2) The subgroup weights are heavily imbalanced across the two groups being compared (here, Protocol A received \(80\%\) Severe cases while Protocol B received only \(20\%\) Severe cases).

Always check plausible confounding variables via stratified tables before drawing conclusions from a pooled contingency table.
[/CALLOUT]

Which table—pooled or stratified—should guide a clinician choosing a protocol for a new patient? Because case severity is known before treatment and drives both the hospital's assignment pattern and recovery odds, the stratified table gives the honest comparison: whether a new patient's case is Mild or Severe, Protocol A has the higher observed recovery rate.
