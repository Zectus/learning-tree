=== SECTION 1: Fitting a descriptive line by least squares ===

Pearson's correlation \(r\) tells us how tightly a scatterplot hugs a straight line, but it does not give us the equation of that line or let us predict a response \(y\) from an explanatory value \(x\). Given \(n \ge 2\) paired observations \((x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\) (with \(s_x > 0\)), we seek a descriptive linear trendline
\[
\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x,
\]
where \(b_0\) is the vertical intercept, \(b_1\) is the slope, and \(\hat{y}_i = b_0 + b_1 x_i\) denotes the fitted (predicted) value at \(x_i\). For each observation, the vertical discrepancy between the observed response and the line is the residual
\[
e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - (b_0 + b_1 x_i).
\]
Notice the sign convention: \(e_i > 0\) when the observed point sits ABOVE the line, and \(e_i < 0\) when it sits BELOW the line.

How should we choose \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) so the line fits the point cloud best? Simply minimizing \(\sum e_i\) fails because huge positive and negative residuals cancel out. Instead, the ordinary least-squares (OLS) criterion chooses \((b_0, b_1)\) to minimize the sum of squared vertical residuals (sum of squared errors):
\[
\text{SSE}(b_0, b_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2.
\]
We can find the unique minimizing pair \((b_0, b_1)\) completely algebraically—without calculus—by completing the square twice! First rewrite each residual by adding and subtracting \(\bar{y}\) and \(b_1 \bar{x}\), and set \(c = \bar{y} - b_0 - b_1 \bar{x}\):
\[
y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i = \big[(y_i - \bar{y}) - b_1(x_i - \bar{x})\big] + c.
\]
Square both sides and sum over \(i = 1, \dots, n\). Because \(\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y}) = 0\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0\), the cross-term \(2 c \sum_{i=1}^n \big[(y_i - \bar{y}) - b_1(x_i - \bar{x})\big]\) vanishes identically, leaving
\[
\text{SSE}(b_0, b_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big[(y_i - \bar{y}) - b_1(x_i - \bar{x})\big]^2 + n c^2.
\]
Look at the two nonnegative terms:
1. For ANY slope \(b_1\), the term \(n c^2 \ge 0\) is minimized uniquely when \(c = 0\), which forces
\[
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}.
\]
2. With \(c = 0\), introduce the shorthand \(S_{xx} = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 > 0\), \(S_{yy} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2\), and \(S_{xy} = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})\). Expanding the remaining sum and completing the square in \(b_1\) yields
\[
S_{yy} - 2 b_1 S_{xy} + b_1^2 S_{xx} = S_{xx}\left(b_1 - \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\right)^2 + \left(S_{yy} - \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}}\right).
\]
Because \(S_{xx} > 0\), the squared term is \(\ge 0\) and vanishes uniquely at \(b_1 = S_{xy} / S_{xx}\). Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n - 1\) and recalling \(s_{xy} = r s_x s_y\) produces the OLS formulas (the exact same solution obtained from the calculus normal equations \(\sum e_i = 0\) and \(\sum x_i e_i = 0\)).

[CALLOUT]
id: ols-formulas
label: Theorem
title: Descriptive ordinary least-squares (OLS) trendline
tone: key

For \(n \ge 2\) paired observations \((x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\) with \(s_x > 0\), the unique line \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\) minimizing \(\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2\) has slope and intercept:

(1) Slope:
\[
b_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{s_{xy}}{s_x^2} = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x}.
\]
(2) Intercept:
\[
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}.
\]
(3) Centroid and residual orthogonality: the fitted line ALWAYS passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\), and its residuals \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) satisfy both \(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i e_i = 0\) (and hence \(\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{y}_i e_i = 0\)).
[/CALLOUT]

Let us verify [REF: ols-formulas.3 | the two residual identities] directly from \(b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}\) and \(b_1 = S_{xy} / S_{xx}\):
- \(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \big[(y_i - \bar{y}) - b_1(x_i - \bar{x})\big] = 0 - b_1(0) = 0\).
- \(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) e_i = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) - b_1 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = S_{xy} - \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} S_{xx} = 0\).
Adding \(\bar{x} \sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0\) immediately gives \(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i e_i = 0\) as well.

Work through a complete numerical example with \(n = 5\) pairs of weekly ad spend \(x\) and revenue \(y\) (in thousands of dollars): \((1, 9), (2, 11), (3, 12), (4, 17), (5, 21)\). Here \(\bar{x} = 3\) and \(\bar{y} = 70 / 5 = 14\), with \(S_{xx} = (-2)^2 + (-1)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 10\) and \(S_{xy} = (-2)(-5) + (-1)(-3) + 0(-2) + 1(3) + 2(7) = 30\). Therefore
\[
b_1 = \frac{30}{10} = 3, \qquad b_0 = 14 - 3(3) = 5, \qquad \hat{y} = 5 + 3 x.
\]

=== SECTION 2: Standardized form, regression toward the mean, and non-invertibility ===

Substitute \(b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}\) and \(b_1 = r(s_y / s_x)\) into \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\), subtract \(\bar{y}\), and divide both sides by \(s_y\) (when \(s_y > 0\)):
\[
\frac{\hat{y} - \bar{y}}{s_y} = r \left(\frac{x - \bar{x}}{s_x}\right), \qquad \text{or simply} \qquad z_{\hat{y}} = r\, z_x.
\]
This remarkably compact standardized equation explains two phenomena that frequently surprise newcomers.

First, regression toward the mean: whenever \(|r| < 1\), multiplying \(z_x\) by \(r\) shrinks the magnitude toward zero! If \(r = 0.50\) between parents' heights \(x\) and adult children's heights \(y\), a parent who is \(z_x = +2.0\) standard deviations taller than average has a predicted child height only \(z_{\hat{y}} = 0.50(2.0) = +1.0\) standard deviation above average—closer to the mean in standardized units. This is a purely mathematical property of imperfect linear correlation (\(|r| < 1\)), not a biological force pushing heights toward homogeneity.

Second, switching the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) produces a DIFFERENT line! Regressing \(y\) on \(x\) minimizes VERTICAL squared residuals and gives \(z_{\hat{y}} = r\, z_x\) (slope \(r\) in standardized coordinates). Regressing \(x\) on \(y\) minimizes HORIZONTAL squared residuals and gives \(z_{\hat{x}} = r\, z_y\), which rearranges to \(z_y = (1 / r)\, z_{\hat{x}}\) (slope \(1 / r\) in standardized coordinates). Because \(r \ne 1 / r\) whenever \(0 < |r| < 1\), you must NEVER algebraically solve \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x\) backward for \(x\) to predict \(x\) from \(y\)—always refit \(x\) on \(y\) using \(b_{x|y} = r (s_x / s_y)\).

=== SECTION 3: Variance decomposition, \(R^2\), residual plots, and extrapolation ===

How well does the OLS line capture the overall variability in \(y\)? Split every observation's total deviation from \(\bar{y}\) into two pieces—the part captured by the fitted line plus the leftover residual:
\[
y_i - \bar{y} = (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) + (y_i - \hat{y}_i) = (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) + e_i.
\]
Square both sides and sum over \(i = 1, \dots, n\):
\[
\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}_{\text{SST}} = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2}_{\text{SSR}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2}_{\text{SSE}} + 2\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) e_i.
\]
Now inspect the cross-term: because \(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y} = b_1 (x_i - \bar{x})\), we have \(\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) e_i = b_1 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) e_i = 0\) by [REF: ols-formulas.3 | residual orthogonality]! The cross-term vanishes completely, yielding the exact sum-of-squares decomposition \(\text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE}\).

Moreover, from our completing-the-square minimum in Section 1, the minimized error sum of squares is
\[
\text{SSE} = S_{yy} - \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} = S_{yy}\left(1 - \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx} S_{yy}}\right) = \text{SST}\,(1 - r^2).
\]
Therefore \(\text{SSR} = \text{SST} - \text{SSE} = r^2\,\text{SST}\).

[CALLOUT]
id: r-squared-decomp
label: Identity
title: Sum-of-squares decomposition and coefficient of determination \(R^2\)
tone: key

For descriptive simple linear regression (with \(S_{yy} = \text{SST} > 0\)):

(1) Total variation splits orthogonally into explained variation plus residual variation:
\[
\text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE},
\]
where \(\text{SST} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\), \(\text{SSR} = \sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2\), and \(\text{SSE} = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\).
(2) The coefficient of determination \(R^2\) is the fraction of total \(y\)-variation accounted for by the linear trendline, and in simple linear regression it equals the square of Pearson's correlation:
\[
R^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = r^2 \in [0, 1].
\]
[/CALLOUT]

Check [REF: r-squared-decomp | this identity] on our Section 1 revenue example: \(\text{SST} = \sum (y_i - 14)^2 = 25 + 9 + 4 + 9 + 49 = 96\), \(\text{SSR} = \sum (\hat{y}_i - 14)^2 = 36 + 9 + 0 + 9 + 36 = 90\), and \(\text{SSE} = 6\). Indeed \(90 + 6 = 96\), and \(R^2 = 90 / 96 = 15 / 16 = 0.9375\) (\(93.75\%\) of the variation in weekly revenue is captured by the linear trendline), with \(r = +\sqrt{15/16} \approx +0.9682\). Remember that because \(R^2 = r^2\) erases the sign, you must check the sign of \(b_1\) to recover the sign of \(r\)!

Even a high \(R^2\) (like \(0.92\)) does NOT prove a straight line is the right summary! Always plot the residuals \(e_i\) against \(x_i\) (or \(\hat{y}_i\)) around the horizontal line \(e = 0\). Magnifying the vertical errors exposes four critical pathologies immediately:
- Patternless horizontal band around \(0\): supports a linear descriptive trend with roughly uniform spread.
- Systematic curvature (U-shape or downward arch): signals a curved nonlinear trend—fitting a straight line misses the real form (and points toward re-expression in the next lesson).
- Funnel or fan shape (residuals widening or narrowing across \(x\)): signals non-constant vertical spread.
- Outliers, high-leverage points, and influential points: a vertical outlier has a large \(|e_i|\) near the middle of \(x\) (inflating \(\text{SSE}\) without much power to tilt the slope); a high-leverage point has an extreme \(x_i\) far from \(\bar{x}\); an influential point substantially shifts \(b_1\) or \(b_0\) when removed (typically combining high leverage with a departure from the rest of the line).

Finally, respect two clear boundaries of descriptive OLS:
1. Never extrapolate blindly: the trendline summarizes linear behavior ONLY across the observed \(x\)-range; outside that range, physical or economic curves often bend sharply, making extrapolated \(\hat{y}\) values unreliable or impossible.
2. Descriptive versus inferential scope: everything here describes the observed dataset alone. Treating \((b_0, b_1)\) as random estimates of population parameters, building confidence intervals for slopes, and running hypothesis tests belong strictly to Statistical Inference.
