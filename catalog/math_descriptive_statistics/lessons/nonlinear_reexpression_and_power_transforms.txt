=== SECTION 1: Taming skewness with Tukey's ladder of powers ===

Real-world quantitative measurements—city populations, insurance claims, biological weights, network latencies—are frequently strongly skewed or follow curved relationships where raw means, standard deviations, and straight trendlines perform poorly. Rather than discarding informative structure or accepting a distorted summary, exploratory data analysis often re-expresses a strictly positive variable \(x > 0\) by applying a smooth, strictly increasing nonlinear function \(g(x)\)—such as \(\sqrt{x}\), \(\ln x\), or \(x^2\)—so the distribution becomes more symmetric, group spreads stabilize across side-by-side boxplots, or a curved scatterplot straightens into a line.

John Tukey organized the most useful re-expressions into a single ordered sequence called the ladder of powers.

[CALLOUT]
id: tukey-ladder
label: Framework
title: Tukey's ladder of powers (for strictly positive \(x > 0\))
tone: key

Arrange power transformations by exponent \(p\), adding a minus sign for \(p < 0\) so EVERY rung stays strictly increasing (preserving the ordering of all observations and quantiles):

(1) Moving DOWN the ladder (\(p < 1\): \(\sqrt{x}\), \(\ln x\) at the \(p = 0\) slot, \(-1/\sqrt{x}\), \(-1/x\)): these functions are CONCAVE—their slopes decrease as \(x\) grows, compressing large values relative to small values. Moving down pulls in a long RIGHT tail (reducing positive skewness) and stabilizes spread when groups with larger centers have wider spreads.
(2) Identity rung (\(p = 1\)): raw data \(x\) (no change in shape).
(3) Moving UP the ladder (\(p > 1\): \(x^2, x^3\)): these functions are CONVEX—their slopes increase as \(x\) grows, stretching large values relative to small values. Moving up pulls in a long LEFT tail (reducing negative skewness).
[/CALLOUT]

Why does \(\ln x\) sit precisely at the \(p = 0\) rung between \(x^{1/2}\) and \(-x^{-1/2}\)? Write each power in the rescaled, order-preserving Box-Cox form \(T_p(x) = \frac{x^p - 1}{p}\) for \(p \ne 0\). Every \(T_p\) passes through \((1, 0)\) with slope \(1\) at \(x = 1\), and as \(p \to 0\), \(T_p(x)\) converges smoothly to \(\ln x\)! Drag the slider \(p\) up and down Tukey's ladder in the graph below to see how \(p < 1\) bends downward (compressing the right tail) while \(p > 1\) bends upward (stretching the right tail).

See [REF: tukey-ladder.1 | moving down the ladder] in action on the right-skewed \(n = 7\) dataset \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\). On the raw scale, its quartiles are \(Q_1 = 2\), \(M = 8\), \(Q_3 = 32\), giving a large positive Bowley quartile skewness \(\text{Sk}_B = \frac{32 + 2 - 2(8)}{32 - 2} = \frac{18}{30} = +0.60\). Because \(\ln x\) is strictly increasing, it maps each order statistic directly: \(\ln 1, \ln 2, 2\ln 2, 3\ln 2, 4\ln 2, 5\ln 2, 6\ln 2\). The re-expressed values are evenly spaced! Their quartiles are \(Q_{1,\ln} = \ln 2\), \(M_{\ln} = 3\ln 2\), \(Q_{3,\ln} = 5\ln 2\), yielding \(\text{Sk}_B = 0\) and \(g_1 = 0\)—exact symmetry!

Watch the domain condition carefully: \(\ln x\), \(\sqrt{x}\), and negative powers require \(x > 0\) (with \(\sqrt{x}\) allowing \(x = 0\)). If a right-skewed variable contains zeros (such as count data), adding a small shift \(\ln(x + c)\) is sometimes used exploratorily, but the resulting shape depends on the arbitrary choice of \(c\), so any shift must be reported transparently rather than hidden.

=== SECTION 2: Linearizing exponential and power-law trends ===

When a scatterplot of \((x_i, y_i)\) bends smoothly upward or downward—and an OLS residual plot on the raw scale displays a clear U-shape or arch—two logarithmic re-expressions straighten the two most common nonlinear patterns in science and economics into ordinary straight lines.

[CALLOUT]
id: log-linearization
label: Method
title: Semi-log and log-log linearization for positive response \(y > 0\)
tone: key

(1) Semi-log re-expression (\(\ln y\) versus \(x\)) linearizes an EXPONENTIAL trend \(y \approx a b^x\) (with \(a > 0, b > 0\)): taking natural logarithms yields
\[
\ln y \approx \ln a + (\ln b)\,x = c_0 + c_1 x.
\]
Fit descriptive OLS to \((x_i, \ln y_i)\) to obtain \(\widehat{\ln y} = c_0 + c_1 x\), then back-transform as \(\hat{y} = e^{c_0} (e^{c_1})^x\). Increasing \(x\) by \(\Delta x\) multiplies \(\hat{y}\) by \(e^{c_1 \Delta x}\) (a constant multiplicative growth or decay factor per unit of \(x\)).
(2) Log-log re-expression (\(\ln y\) versus \(\ln x\), requiring \(x > 0, y > 0\)) linearizes a POWER-LAW trend \(y \approx a x^p\) (with \(a > 0\)): taking natural logarithms of both sides yields
\[
\ln y \approx \ln a + p \ln x = c_0 + c_1 \ln x.
\]
Fit descriptive OLS to \((\ln x_i, \ln y_i)\) to obtain \(\widehat{\ln y} = c_0 + c_1 \ln x\), then back-transform as \(\hat{y} = e^{c_0} x^{c_1}\). Multiplying \(x\) by any factor \(m > 0\) multiplies \(\hat{y}\) by \(m^{c_1}\).
[/CALLOUT]

How do you know whether an upward-curving scatterplot is better linearized by semi-log or log-log? Fit OLS on the transformed coordinates and ALWAYS inspect the residual plot ON THE TRANSFORMED SCALE! Look at the graph below: on a semi-log plot (\(\ln y\) on the vertical axis against raw \(x\) on the horizontal axis), a genuine exponential curve \(y = 2 e^{0.4 x}\) flattens into an exact straight line \(\ln y = \ln 2 + 0.4 x\), whereas a power law \(y = 2 x^{1.5}\) remains visibly concave (\(\ln y = \ln 2 + 1.5 \ln x\)), leaving an unmistakable arched residual plot until you also take \(\ln x\).

Work through a quick semi-log check. Suppose a descriptive OLS fit of \(\ln y\) against time \(x\) (in days) yields \(\widehat{\ln y} = 1.5 + 0.20 x\) with a patternless transformed residual plot. Back-transforming gives \(\hat{y} = e^{1.5} e^{0.20 x} \approx 4.48 (1.221)^x\). Each additional day (\(\Delta x = 1\)) multiplies predicted \(y\) by \(e^{0.20} \approx 1.221\)—a roughly \(22.1\%\) daily increase—rather than adding a fixed constant amount.

=== SECTION 3: Back-transformation, elasticity, and the geometric-mean trap ===

Re-expression simplifies exploration and linear fitting, but translating results back to the original units demands precision on two key points.

First, interpret the log-log slope \(c_1\) as an elasticity. In the back-transformed power law \(\hat{y} = e^{c_0} x^{c_1}\), replacing \(x\) by \(m x\) yields
\[
\hat{y}(m x) = e^{c_0} (m x)^{c_1} = m^{c_1}\,\hat{y}(x).
\]
For any exact scaling factor \(m\)—such as doubling \(x\) (\(m = 2\))—predicted \(\hat{y}\) multiplies by \(2^{c_1}\) (for instance, if \(c_1 = 0.5\), doubling \(x\) multiplies \(\hat{y}\) by \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\)). For small percentage changes (say a \(1\%\) increase, \(m = 1.01\)), the elementary approximation \((1 + \delta)^{c_1} \approx 1 + c_1 \delta\) implies that a \(1\%\) increase in \(x\) corresponds to roughly a \(c_1\%\) change in \(\hat{y}\). That percentage-to-percentage ratio is why economists and biologists call \(c_1\) the elasticity of \(y\) with respect to \(x\).

Second, beware of back-transforming an arithmetic mean computed on the log scale!

[CALLOUT]
id: backtransform-mean-trap
label: Common pitfall
title: Exponentiating the mean of logs gives the geometric mean, NOT the arithmetic mean
tone: caution

For strictly positive observations \(x_1, \dots, x_n\), averaging their natural logarithms and exponentiating gives
\[
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ln x_i\right) = \exp\!\left(\ln \left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i\right)^{1/n}\right) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i\right)^{1/n} = G,
\]
which is the GEOMETRIC mean \(G\), NOT the arithmetic mean \(\bar{x}\)! Because \(\ln x\) is strictly concave, whenever the values are not all equal we have \(G < \bar{x}\), so \(\exp(\overline{\ln x})\) systematically understates the arithmetic mean \(\bar{x}\). (By contrast, because \(\exp\) is strictly increasing, exponentiating the median of \(\ln x\) recovers the median of \(x\) directly whenever \(n\) is odd, and recovers the geometric mean of the two middle values when \(n\) is even.)
[/CALLOUT]

Notice what [REF: backtransform-mean-trap | this identity] also means for a back-transformed regression prediction \(\hat{y} = \exp(\widehat{\ln y})\): on a right-skewed log-transformed dataset, \(\exp(\widehat{\ln y})\) tracks the typical multiplicative center (approximating the conditional median or geometric center) rather than the arithmetic average of \(y\) at a given \(x\). State clearly whether a back-transformed summary represents a typical multiplicative level or a raw arithmetic average.
