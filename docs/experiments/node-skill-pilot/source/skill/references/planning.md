HOW MANY QUESTIONS, AND HOW EACH ONE IS MARKED

The number of main questions should be however many it actually takes to verify that every main idea in this document has landed — and landed as something the reader can use, not just something they can recognize or recite. For a topic where the ideas are mathematical, that means confirming the reader can actually apply the mathematics, not just state the definition or theorem back; for a topic without computation, the equivalent bar still applies in whatever form real use takes there — correctly applying a principle to a new case, telling apart two things that are easy to confuse, recognizing which situation calls for which idea — rather than settling for "can restate it." Don't add questions to build up volume, for variety, or as extra practice — that's what the bonus section further down is for. A document with few main ideas, each confirmed by one solid question, is complete as it is; one with many interlocking ideas needs as many questions as those ideas actually require, no more.

Every main question marks its own correct option inline, with an [ANSWER: X] line inside its [QUESTION N]...[/QUESTION] block — see OUTPUT FORMAT below. This tag is never shown to the reader; it's read only by the app that scores the reader's answer. Design each question's setup and its wrong options with real care — don't let whichever answer happens to fall out of a quick derivation dictate lazy distractors (see DISTRACTORS under QUESTION CONSTRUCTION RULES below) — but there is no target letter to hit. Work out the question's correct answer as a normal part of designing that question, mark it with [ANSWER: X], and move on. The app shuffles each question's options into random order and relabels them before anyone ever sees them, so don't try to steer which letter the correct answer lands on, and don't worry about the distribution of correct letters across the document — none of that is this document's concern, and time spent on it is time not spent on making the question itself sharp.

PLAN FIRST

- Read CONTEXT as a learning goal and scope boundary. Identify the ability the reader should gain and distinguish central content from supporting cases. An older scope note may be only a topic list: organize it into a coherent progression instead of treating every item as equally important. Cover required content economically without expanding into excluded methods.
- Identify what the reader already knows from prerequisites or explicitly calibrated background. For each central new statement, decide whether it is a definition, a supplied fact or rule, or a consequence to be developed here. Do not invent proofs of definitions or supplied inputs. Do not silently treat the result this node is meant to develop as prior knowledge. Check supporting shortcuts against that background too: if one needs an unfamiliar theorem, explain the needed step or choose a more accessible route.
- Choose a meaningful question and an accessible case that can carry its explanation. Identify the actual obstacle the new idea resolves, such as choosing a useful representation, determining an unknown quantity, or relating two descriptions. Keep the example's background within CONTEXT.
- Plan the route from the problem to each central result: what choice is made, why that choice helps, what operation is performed, and what intermediate relation connects it to the conclusion. Make these bridges visible in the lesson. A correct final formula followed by a substitution check is not a derivation of how to obtain that formula.
- Audit first-use notation, including operations and conventions, not only named variables. Check superscripts such as transpose and adjoint, component and state labels, differential measures, ordered products, and graph aliases or scaled axes. Explain a new operation before relying on it; give a brief concrete example when its action would otherwise remain unclear, ideally within the lesson's main worked case. Remove unnecessary shorthand instead of defining more shorthand to explain it. Familiar prerequisite notation needs no repeated tutorial.
- Choose the amount of justification the goal needs. Show the step carrying the new idea; compress routine algebra the reader already knows. Supplied facts and rules may be accepted explicitly when scope calls for that, but the new consequences drawn from them still need their connecting argument. Do not expand into sibling or future lessons to justify a local step.
- For a procedure or predictive model, distinguish what is actually done, what information is reported or retained, and what licenses the next prediction. For a sequence, identify the state or information passed to each stage, any selection already made, and the assumed intervening changes. For statistical claims, decide whether the statement concerns an individual event, a distribution over repeated preparations, or an estimate from finite data. Plan these distinctions only where the learning goal needs them.
- Plan accessible questions and fresh transfer at the intended level. Identify the work left to the reader in each question and independently work out its answer and distractors. A late question should not merely ask for the slope, coefficient, sign, or conclusion just stated; where appropriate, ask the reader to compare cases, diagnose an incorrect use, or combine established steps in a new setup.
- For visual objects, plan graphs where they first help understanding. Explain how the quantities in the prose map to plotted coordinates and parameters. Choose ranges and marks that show the relevant feature honestly within the supported graph format. Do not add a graph to purely symbolic work merely to fill a quota.
